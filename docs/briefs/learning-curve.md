## What the ranking pages miss

The US results for "learning curve" are dictionary definitions and business or L&D explainers
(types of curve, a formula, "how to shorten it for employees"). None goes back to the primary
sources. None explains that a curve averaged across many people can look smooth even when each
person's learning comes in jumps. And none says what any of this means for designing a course.
That's the gap: the history told from the sources, the research on what the curve really looks
like, and what a course designer should do about it.

## Angle

"Learning curve" is one of the few research terms everyone uses, and most people use it
backwards: a steep curve means fast learning, not hard learning. The idea began with a
psychologist memorising nonsense syllables in 1885. It became a tool for planning aircraft
production costs in 1936. Since then, researchers have argued about its shape. For a course
designer, the useful lessons are about plateaus, practice and what averages hide.

## Outline (library shape, `docs/editorial.md` section 5)

1. **What the research says**
   - Origins: Ebbinghaus (1885) plotted his own learning of nonsense-syllable lists over repeated
     trials. Bryan and Harter (1897, 1899) studied telegraph operators: the plateau, and their
     claim that skills build in layers. The earliest recorded use of the phrase "learning curve"
     is around 1903 (check the primary source before citing it).
   - Into industry: Wright (1936) found aircraft labour costs fell by a steady percentage each
     time cumulative output doubled ("Wright's law"). Henderson at BCG (1968) turned this into the
     "experience curve". Keep this short: it's context, not the subject.
   - The shape debate: Newell and Rosenbloom (1981) argued for a power law of practice. Heathcote,
     Brown and Mewhort (2000) found that individual learners' data fit an exponential curve better,
     and that the power law came from averaging. Gallistel, Fairhurst and Balsam (2004) found that
     individual learning curves often show abrupt steps. Possibly Gray and Lindstedt (2017) on
     plateaus, dips and leaps.
   - The "steep" confusion: the technical meaning against everyday usage, with a dictionary or
     usage source.
   - Evidence level: **strong** that practice gains follow a curve of diminishing returns;
     **moderate** on its exact mathematical form and on plateaus.
   - Research cards: one from Ebbinghaus or Bryan and Harter (the plateau), and one from
     Heathcote et al. (2000) on averaging. A chart only if a source table gives the numbers
     (e.g. Wright 1936, or Bryan and Harter's operator data); otherwise no chart.
2. **How to apply it in a course.** Plan for fast early gains and slower later ones (front-load
   quick wins, then spaced, varied practice). Plan for plateaus: warn learners and change the task
   at a plateau rather than just adding more of the same. Don't read a smooth cohort average as
   any one learner's path, and look at individual progress data. "Try this in your course"
   callout: plot one learner's quiz scores across attempts. `[JIM:]` slot for a course where
   learners stalled and what he changed.
3. **How the platforms support it.** Per-learner progress and quiz-attempt data in Thinkific,
   LearnWorlds, Teachable and Kajabi. Every feature claim gets `[TO VERIFY]` unless it's in Jim's
   testing notes or current documentation.
4. **Further reading.** Ebbinghaus (Classics in the History of Psychology translation), Bryan and
   Harter (1897), Wright (1936), Heathcote et al. (2000), Gallistel et al. (2004).

  quantitative analysis. *PNAS*.
