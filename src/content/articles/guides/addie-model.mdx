The ADDIE model is the most widely taught process in instructional design. Its five letters name the
work any course needs: **Analysis** (who are the learners, and what must they be able to do?),
**Design** (how will they get there, and how will we know?), **Development** (building the
materials), **Implementation** (delivering the course) and **Evaluation** (did it work, and what
should change?).

That's the textbook answer. The more useful answer is that ADDIE is a checklist of decisions, and
each phase should end with something concrete you can hold up and check. This guide walks through
what each phase should produce, using a small self-paced online course as the example, and deals
with the most common complaint about the model: that it's rigid and out of date.

## Where ADDIE came from, and why it matters

ADDIE is often presented as if someone designed it. Nobody did, at least not under that name.

The process behind it comes from the instructional systems design (ISD) movement of the 1960s and
70s. Its best-known ancestor is the *Interservice Procedures for Instructional Systems Development*,
written in 1975 at Florida State University's Center for Educational Technology for the US armed
services' joint training programme ([Branson et al., 1975](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED122018)). As the
educational technologist Michael Molenda records, its top-level phases were "analyze, design, develop, implement, and control": close
to ADDIE, but not the same.

When Molenda went looking for the original ADDIE model, he
searched the field's textbooks and histories and asked experienced practitioners. He didn't find one.

That history matters for two reasons. First, there is no official version to follow, so the
diagrams you'll find online, circles, waterfalls and loops, are each someone's interpretation.
Second, Molenda notes that the processes are generally understood as "sequential but also
iterative" ([Molenda, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/pfi.21461)). The strictly linear ADDIE that
critics like to attack is a reading of the model, not the model itself.

## The five phases at a glance

| Phase | The question it answers | What it should produce |
|---|---|---|
| Analysis | Who are the learners, and what must they be able to do afterwards? | A one-page learner and performance brief |
| Design | How will they get there, and how will we know they can do it? | Objectives, assessments and a course map |
| Development | What will they read, watch and practise? | A built, reviewed pilot module, then the full course |
| Implementation | How does the course reach learners, and who supports them? | A live course, a pilot group and a support plan |
| Evaluation | Did they learn it, and what should change? | Findings and a revision list, fed back into the other phases |

To make each phase concrete, we'll follow one example throughout: a bookkeeper building a short,
self-paced online course that teaches small-business owners to reconcile their accounts every month.
It's an invented example, but it's typical of the courses independent creators build.

## Analysis: define the gap before the content

Analysis answers three questions: who the learners are, what they need to be able to *do* after the
course, and what's stopping them now. The last question is the one most course creators skip, and
it's the one that tells you whether a course is even the right fix.

For our bookkeeper, analysis might reveal that most business owners already understand what
reconciliation is. What they lack is a routine: they put it off for months, then face hundreds of
unmatched transactions. That changes the course. Less explanation, more guided practice, and a
monthly checklist they'll actually use.

**What it should produce:** a one-page brief covering the learners (who they are, what they already
know, how much time they have), the performance goal, the gap, and the constraints (budget,
platform, deadline).

<Callout>
  Write your performance goal as one sentence that starts "By the end of this course, learners
  will be able to…" and ends with something you could watch them do. "Reconcile a month of
  transactions in under an hour" passes. "Understand reconciliation" doesn't.
</Callout>

## Design: plan the assessment before the lessons

Design turns the brief into a plan. The most useful habit here is to design the assessment first:
decide how learners will show they can do the task, then plan the lessons that get them there. This
is often called backward design, and it stops a course drifting into everything the expert knows
rather than what the learner needs.

For the bookkeeper, the final assessment might be a sample month of bank transactions that learners
reconcile themselves. Working backwards, the course needs lessons on matching transactions, handling
the awkward ones (split payments, refunds, bank fees), and building the monthly habit, each with
practice before the next.

**What it should produce:** measurable objectives, the assessments that test them, and a course map:
modules, lessons, the practice in each, and the order they come in.

## Development: build one module, then stop

Development is where most of the time goes: writing, recording, building quizzes and setting up the
course platform. It's also where the most time is wasted, because problems in the design only become
visible once something is built.

The fix is simple. Build one complete module first, including its practice and quiz, and put it in
front of a few real learners before building the rest. For the bookkeeper, that might be the module
on matching transactions. If testers stall on the practice exercise, it's far cheaper to find out
now than after recording eight more modules in the same style.

**What it should produce:** a finished pilot module that has been reviewed by someone who knows the
subject and tried by someone who doesn't, then the remaining modules built to the same pattern.

## Implementation: launch small and support learners

Implementation is more than pressing publish. It covers setting up enrolment and access, testing the
course on the devices learners will use, writing the welcome and reminder messages, and deciding who
answers questions and how fast.

For a new course, a pilot cohort is worth the delay: five to ten learners who get the course free or
discounted in return for honest feedback. Our bookkeeper might run the course with a handful of
existing clients before opening it to the public.

**What it should produce:** a live course, a pilot group, a support plan, and a way to collect
feedback that's built into the course rather than bolted on.

## Evaluation: measure whether they can do it

Evaluation runs throughout, not just at the end. *Formative* evaluation happens while you build:
reviews, tests with real learners, the pilot. *Summative* evaluation happens after launch and asks
whether the course achieved its goal.

For a self-paced course, three kinds of evidence are usually within reach:

- **Can learners do the task?** Results on the final assessment are the most direct evidence. For the
  bookkeeper, how many learners reconcile the sample month correctly?
- **Where do learners struggle or stop?** Quiz questions most people get wrong and lessons where
  people drop out point to specific fixes. Check what your course platform reports on both.
- **What do learners say?** A short survey at the end, and a follow-up a month later asking whether
  they're still using what they learned.

**What it should produce:** a short findings report and a revision list, with each item tied back to
the phase where the fix belongs. A confusing quiz question is a development fix; learners who didn't
need the course at all is an analysis problem.

## Is ADDIE outdated?

The most common criticism of ADDIE is that it's a waterfall: long phases completed in order, with
learners seeing nothing until the end. That criticism is fair of the way ADDIE is sometimes run, and
it's the reason Michael Allen and Richard Sites proposed the Successive Approximation Model (SAM), which
replaces long phases with rapid cycles of design, prototype and review
([Allen & Sites, 2012](https://td.org/product/book--leaving-addie-for-sam-an-agile-model-for-developing-the-best-learning-experiences/111218)).

But as we saw, the strictly linear version was never the only reading. In practice, the choice is
less ADDIE versus SAM than how often you loop. For a small online course, a sensible rhythm is:

1. A short analysis and design pass for the whole course.
2. Build and test one module.
3. Revise the design based on what you learned.
4. Build the rest, testing as you go.
5. Launch to a pilot group, evaluate, and revise again.

That's ADDIE run in loops, and it's close to what SAM recommends. What matters is that each kind of
work happens, and that evaluation feeds back into the earlier phases.

## Four common mistakes

1. **Skipping analysis because you're the expert.** Expertise tells you what's true, not what
   learners are missing. Talk to five of them before you plan anything.
2. **Writing objectives you can't observe.** If an objective starts with "understand" or "know",
   rewrite it until you could test it.
3. **Building everything before testing anything.** Pilot one module. It's the single biggest saving
   in the whole process.
4. **Treating evaluation as a satisfaction survey.** Learners can enjoy a course and still be unable
   to do the task. Measure the task.

## What to do next

Write the one-page analysis brief for your next course before you plan a single lesson: the
learners, the performance goal in one observable sentence, the gap, and the constraints. Everything
else in ADDIE builds on it.
