An affective learning objective describes an attitude, value or willingness you want a course to
build, written as something a learner would *do* if the course worked. "Value patient privacy" is
the intention. "In a role-play, chooses to ask the patient before discussing their results in front
of a family member" is the objective, because you can watch it happen. This guide gives examples at
each of the five levels of the affective domain, then deals with the part most example lists skip:
how to gather evidence without grading anyone's feelings.

That part matters, because the people who built the taxonomy ran into it straight away. Writing in
1964, Krathwohl and his colleagues reported that teachers didn't think it was appropriate "to grade
with respect to interests, attitude, and character development" (as quoted in
[Kretchmar, 2019](https://ebsco.com/research-starters/education/affective-domain)). More than four
decades later, Shephard, drawing on Bloom and colleagues, described educators as still wary of
affective objectives, partly because they are "concerned about charges of indoctrination or
brainwashing" ([Shephard, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1108/14676370810842201)).
We think the worry points to the answer: write affective objectives, but track them across the group
rather than grade them one learner at a time.

## What makes an objective affective

Bloom and his colleagues split educational objectives into three domains: cognitive (knowing and
thinking), psychomotor (physical skill) and affective (feelings, attitudes, values and motivation).
The affective volume, published in 1964 by Krathwohl, Bloom and Masia, ordered its objectives by
internalisation: how far a value has moved from something a learner notices to something that
shapes what they do without prompting ([Kretchmar, 2019](https://ebsco.com/research-starters/education/affective-domain)).

Training researchers make the same split. Kraiger, Ford and Salas proposed cognitive, skill-based
and affective learning outcomes as separate things to evaluate, each needing its own measures
([Kraiger et al., 1993](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.78.2.311)).

A quick test for whether a goal is affective: imagine a learner who aces every quiz in the course.
Would they still fail to do the thing you care about? A support agent can know the complaints
procedure perfectly and still dread calls from angry customers. A nurse can recite the privacy rules
and still discuss a patient's results in a busy corridor. If knowing isn't enough, part of the goal
is affective, and it deserves its own objective.

## The five levels, with example objectives

Here are Krathwohl's five levels in plain English, each with an example objective and the evidence
you could look for. The examples come from four kinds of course: a clinic induction, a workplace
safety module, a creator's photography course and a university first-year unit.

| Level | What it means | Example objective | Evidence to look for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Receiving** | Aware of something and willing to attend to it | "During the case video, notes two moments where the patient seems uncomfortable." | Notes or a short answer that pick out the right moments |
| **Responding** | Takes part, first because asked, then willingly | "Completes the optional near-miss report exercise and suggests one change to the site's process." | Uptake of optional tasks; quality of the suggestion |
| **Valuing** | Treats it as worthwhile and acts on it unprompted | "When a scenario offers a shortcut, chooses the full privacy check and explains why." | Scenario choices with the learner's reasons |
| **Organisation** | Fits the value alongside others and settles conflicts | "Given a deadline that clashes with an equipment check, decides which comes first and justifies the trade-off." | A written or recorded justification that weighs both |
| **Characterisation** | Acts on the value consistently, until it describes them | "Over a semester, cites and credits sources in every submission, including informal drafts." | A pattern across many pieces of work, over time |

Two things stand out. First, every example names a situation where the learner could choose
either way. The shortcut, the deadline clash and the optional task are what make the behaviour
mean something. Second, the higher the level, the longer it takes to see. Characterisation is a
pattern over months; no ninety-minute module can honestly claim it. For most short online courses,
responding and valuing are the realistic targets.

## How to write an affective objective, step by step

We'll follow one invented example: a 45-minute online induction for new reception staff at a
community health clinic. The cognitive content is the clinic's privacy policy. The affective goal
is that staff protect patients' privacy when it's inconvenient.

### Step 1: Name the moment where attitude shows

Start from the job, not the value. Ask: where would a new staff member who knows the policy still
slip? For the clinic, it's the busy waiting room: a patient's family member asks at the desk
whether the test results are back, and it would be quicker and friendlier just to answer.

That moment is the objective's raw material. If you can't name one, the goal may really be
cognitive, and the [standard approach to learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/)
will serve you better.

### Step 2: Pick the level you can honestly reach

Decide what the course can change. A 45-minute induction can make staff aware of the moments that
matter (receiving) and get them choosing the right response in practice (valuing, at a stretch). It
can't make privacy part of their character; the clinic's culture and supervisors do that over months.

Write the level next to the objective while you draft it. It stops you claiming more than the course
can deliver.

### Step 3: Write the behaviour that would signal the attitude

Now write the objective in the same shape as a cognitive one: who, does what, under what
conditions, how well. The difference is that the behaviour stands in for an attitude you can't see
directly.

- Weak: "Staff will value patient privacy."
- Better: "Staff will follow the privacy policy."
- Good: "In a waiting-room scenario where a family member asks for a patient's results, the learner
  chooses to check the patient's consent before sharing anything, and explains the choice in a
  sentence."

The good version names the moment, the choice and a short explanation. The explanation is what lets
you tell a learner who values privacy from one who has worked out which button the course wants.

### Step 4: Pair it with the cognitive objective it depends on

Attitudes need knowledge to act on. Staff can't protect privacy if they don't know what counts as
health information. Put the affective objective beside the cognitive one it relies on, and teach
both in the same lesson: "Identify which details in a conversation count as health information"
alongside the waiting-room objective above.

If you use [Bloom's taxonomy to plan a course](/guides/blooms-taxonomy-examples/), the cognitive
grid covers the first objective. The affective domain is a second column next to it, not a
replacement.

### Step 5: Decide how you'll gather evidence

Krathwohl's team found "little information in the research literature about how to assess affect"
(as quoted in [Kretchmar, 2019](https://ebsco.com/research-starters/education/affective-domain)),
and Shephard notes it is "notoriously difficult for teachers to assess performance and give credit
for achievement" in this domain ([Shephard, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1108/14676370810842201)).
Difficult isn't impossible. There are four kinds of evidence a self-paced online course can collect:

1. **Scenario choices with reasons.** A branching scenario where the tempting option is plausible,
   followed by "Why did you choose that?" The reason matters more than the click.
2. **Reflections.** Shephard points to portfolios used to relate experience to learning through
   reflection. In a short course, two or three prompts tied to the moment from step 1 do the same job.
3. **Behaviour on the job.** For workplace courses, the strongest evidence comes after the course:
   a supervisor's observation, a spot check, near-miss reports filed. Plan who will look, and when.
4. **Attitude surveys before and after.** A few agree-disagree statements ("I'd feel comfortable
   telling a family member I need to check with the patient first"), asked at the start and the end.

### Step 6: Track it across the group; grade the reasoning, not the value

This is where the self-rating research becomes useful. Sitzmann and colleagues pooled 222 samples
covering 41,237 learners to ask what learners' ratings of their own knowledge actually measure.
Those self-ratings correlated .34 with cognitive learning, a moderate relationship, but .51 with
satisfaction and .59 with motivation. They also found that nearly a third of evaluation studies had
treated self-assessed knowledge as evidence of cognitive learning
([Sitzmann et al., 2010](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/cognitive-bias/illusion-of-depth/2010-sitzmann.pdf)).

For cognitive objectives, that's a warning: "How well do you understand this?" isn't a test. For
affective objectives, it's closer to the point. A survey of how learners feel is a reasonable
measure of how learners feel. The study was about self-assessed *knowledge* in training and
education, not attitude surveys as such, so treat this as an inference rather than a finding, but
it supports using self-report for the affective side and keeping it away from the cognitive side.

What we'd do with it:

- **Grade the reasoning.** Mark the scenario explanation or the reflection on whether it applies
  the policy and weighs the situation, not on whether the learner professes the right feelings.
- **Read attitudes across the group.** Shephard, drawing on Bloom and colleagues, describes
  evaluating affective outcomes for a group, through surveys and questionnaires. If the average
  answer to the waiting-room statement barely moves between the start and the end, the course needs
  work. That's a judgement about the course, not about any one learner.
- **Keep the stakes low.** Learners who know their values are being scored will tell you what you
  want to hear. Ungraded surveys and anonymous group results get closer to the truth.

This also answers the indoctrination worry. You aren't certifying that someone holds the right
values. You're checking whether your course gives people the awareness, practice and reasons to
act well, and whether, on average, it shifts how they feel about doing so.

## Common mistakes

1. **Unobservable verbs.** "Appreciate", "value" and "be aware of" describe the goal, not the
   objective. Ask what a learner who appreciates it would do differently.
2. **Claiming characterisation.** A short module can't make a value part of someone's character.
   Aim for responding or valuing and say so.
3. **No real choice in the scenario.** If the right answer is obvious, the scenario tests reading,
   not valuing. Make the tempting option genuinely tempting.
4. **Using confidence ratings as proof of learning.** Learners' self-ratings track motivation more
   closely than test scores. Use them for the affective objective, and a real task for the cognitive one.
5. **Grading feelings.** It rewards performance of the right attitude and puts learners off honest
   reflection. Grade the reasoning; read attitudes across the group.

## What to do next

Find one goal in your course that knowing alone won't achieve. Name the moment on the job where
the attitude shows, write the behaviour you'd see at that moment as an objective, and add one
ungraded survey statement about it to the start and end of the course. After your next cohort,
compare the two averages.
