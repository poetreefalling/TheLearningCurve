Agile instructional design means building a course in short cycles instead of one long sequence.
Each cycle, usually called a sprint, takes a few items from a prioritised list, builds them to a
finished state that learners could use, shows the result to the people who matter, and re-plans
before the next one. It borrows its vocabulary from software: backlogs, sprints, reviews and
retrospectives.

Here's what most guides to it leave out. The few studies of agile in course development measure how
the *team* works: communication, coordination, how faithfully the method was followed. We found none
that measured whether learners learned more. That isn't a reason to avoid agile. It's a reason to put
the learning check into the method yourself, and this guide shows how, with a two-week sprint plan
you can copy.

## Where agile comes from, and what carries over

Agile started as a reaction against long software projects that delivered nothing until the end. The
2001 *Manifesto for Agile Software Development* set out four preferences, including "Working software
over comprehensive documentation" and "Responding to change over following a plan", adding that
"while there is value in the items on the right, we value the items on the left more"
([Beck et al., 2001](https://agilemanifesto.org/)). Its principles ask teams to deliver working
software "from a couple of weeks to a couple of months", and say that "Working software is the
primary measure of progress" ([Beck et al., 2001](https://agilemanifesto.org/principles)).

Swap "software" for "a lesson learners can use" and most of it carries straight over. A storyboard
approved by a stakeholder is documentation; a module someone has worked through is progress.

Scrum, the best-known agile framework for teams, supplies the mechanics. Its guide calls it "a lightweight
framework" and defines a few pieces you'll use ([Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020](https://scrumguides.org/docs/scrumguide/v2020/2020-Scrum-Guide-US.pdf)):

| Scrum term | What the Scrum Guide says | In a course build |
|---|---|---|
| Sprint | "fixed length events of one month or less" | One to four weeks of building, usually two |
| Product Backlog | "an emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product" | Learner stories, lessons, practice tasks and fixes, in priority order |
| Definition of Done | "a formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets the quality measures required" | The checklist a lesson must pass before it counts as finished |
| Sprint Review | "to inspect the outcome of the Sprint and determine future adaptations" | Stakeholders and a few learners try what was built |
| Sprint Retrospective | "to plan ways to increase quality and effectiveness" | The team asks what to change about how it works |

## Agile, ADDIE, SAM and LLAMA

Agile isn't a rival to the [ADDIE model](/guides/addie-model/) so much as a different way of
scheduling the same work. ADDIE names what needs doing; agile says do a little of each, often.

Two adaptations for learning are worth knowing. **SAM**, the Successive Approximation Model from Allen
Interactions, opens with what the company calls "an active brainstorming and sketching session, which
is called the Savvy Start", then iterates through rough prototypes
([Allen Interactions, 2024](https://blog.alleninteractions.com/3-ways-to-start-sam)). **LLAMA**, from
Megan Torrance, keeps more of Scrum's structure; her publisher describes it as adapting "the common
phases of ADDIE to incorporate the incremental, iterative nature of Agile projects"
([Torrance, 2019](https://www.td.org/product/book--agile-for-instructional-designers/111910)).
ATD's summary of LLAMA lists five steps: identify a business goal, start with the learner in mind,
define the scope with learner stories, chunk the work, then plan the work and work the plan
([ATD](https://www.td.org/insights/tips-for-using-agile-and-llama)).

| Approach | How work is scheduled | What each cycle produces | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|
| ADDIE as a sequence | Five phases, one after another | Nothing usable until development ends | Fixed content, one formal sign-off |
| ADDIE in loops | A short design pass, a pilot module, then the rest | A tested pilot, then the full course | Small self-paced courses |
| SAM | A Savvy Start, then rounds of prototype and review | Rough, disposable prototypes that firm up | Uncertain briefs built with a client |
| Scrum-style agile and LLAMA | Fixed-length sprints from a prioritised backlog | Finished lessons every sprint | Larger builds and courses kept up to date |

Our comparison of [instructional design models](/guides/instructional-design-models/) covers the
wider family.

## What the research says

Less than the confident vendor pages suggest. Most of the pages ranking for this topic are written by
companies selling an LMS or authoring tool; the claims about better engagement and retention we saw
there came without a study behind them.

The independent work is small and mostly qualitative. Erickson's case study of agile at BYU
Independent Study, which builds online courses, combined interviews with administrators, managers and
designers, weekly surveys and observation of three Scrum teams
([Erickson, 2018](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/6780)). It focused on making the method work:
part-time team members, people split across projects, and communication when teams weren't in the same
room. Reviewing the literature first, he noted that "Most of what has been published specifically for

The nearest thing to a learner measure we found is a redesign of an information systems course in an
online MBA, built collaboratively by faculty from several universities using an agile approach
([Woszczynski et al., 2021](https://digitalcommons.kennesaw.edu/facpubs/5268)). The authors compared
three semesters of students before the redesign (n = 101) with three after (n = 162). Student
evaluations varied less afterwards, and scores rose, "but the increases were not statistically
significant." Evaluations measure how students rated the course, not what they learned, and the
comparison wasn't randomised.

**In practice**, we read this as: agile has early, case-study evidence that it helps teams coordinate,
and no evidence yet, for or against, on learning. The method doesn't make a course teach well. What
happens inside each sprint does.

## How to run a course build in sprints

The example: a training lead building a six-module onboarding course for new customer-support staff,
with two subject experts who can give a few hours a week. It's invented, but typical of workplace
builds.

### 1. Write the goal and the learner on one page

Before the backlog, settle what the course is for. LLAMA starts here, and so does any sound process.
For our training lead: new agents should resolve the five most common ticket types on their own within
their first fortnight. Name who they are, what they already know and how much time they have.

### 2. Turn the scope into learner stories

Write the backlog as small, testable pieces of what learners will be able to do. ATD's summary of
LLAMA describes user stories as "independent units of scope that can be prioritized, assigned,
developed, and tested". A learner story might read: "As a new agent, I can process a refund request
end to end, so that the customer isn't transferred." Each story gets an assessment item before it gets
any content. That's [backward design](/guides/backward-design/) at the scale of a single story.

Order the backlog by what matters most to the goal, not by the order lessons will appear. The refund
story might be built first because refunds are the most common ticket, even though it sits in module
four.

### 3. Pick a sprint length and keep it

The Scrum Guide caps a sprint at one month; the manifesto prefers "the shorter timescale". For a
course build, two weeks is a sensible default. Subject experts have time to review, and a lesson that
has gone wrong is found within a fortnight. If your experts can only give a few hours a week, three
weeks may be the honest choice. Don't change the length every sprint: the regular rhythm is what makes
reviews happen.

### 4. Put learning in the definition of done

This is the step that fills the gap in the research. A typical definition of done checks production:
media edited, accessibility checked, expert sign-off. Add the learning:

<Callout>
  **Try this in your course.** A lesson counts as done only when: its objective has a matching
  practice task or quiz item; a subject expert has checked the content; three people from the target
  audience have worked through it; and you've read their practice answers and fixed anything two of
  them got wrong for the same reason.
</Callout>

Three testers is small, but it catches the confusing instruction and the practice task nobody can
start. It's a fast version of the [pilot test](/guides/how-to-pilot-an-online-course/) you'd run
before launch, done every sprint. Their answers are [formative assessment](/library/formative-assessment/)
of the course itself.

### 5. Hold the review with learners in the room

A sprint review that only shows stakeholders a demo tells you whether they like it. Ask two or three
learners to try the new lessons, live or recorded, and let stakeholders watch. Then re-order the
backlog: what learners struggled with goes near the top.

### 6. Run a short retrospective

Fifteen minutes on how the team worked, not on the content: where reviews stalled, which handovers
were slow. Erickson's three issues, part-time schedules, people split across projects and remote
communication, are good prompts.

### A two-week sprint, day by day

| Day | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1 | Planning: pick two or three stories from the top of the backlog; confirm the expert review slot |
| 2–6 | Write and build the lessons, practice first; expert review by day 6 |
| 7–8 | Revise after expert review; finish media and accessibility checks |
| 9 | Three learners work through the lessons; collect their practice answers |
| 10 | Fix what they got wrong; review with stakeholders; retrospective; re-order the backlog |

## Agile on your own

Most guides assume a team with a product owner, a facilitator and subject experts. A solo course
creator plays every role, so drop the ceremonies and keep three things: a written, ordered backlog; a
fixed cycle (two weeks works here too); and a definition of done that includes three learners trying
each lesson. Recruit those testers from your audience before you start: past students, a mailing list,
a community you belong to.

At Avondale University, I had the privilege of building the microcredential program from the ground
up, and that came with its challenges. Have you heard the old phrase "jump off a cliff and build a
plane on the way down"? In many ways, that's what my team and I were doing. Tech was building a new
student management system, admin was working on new policy, and my team was building short courses
while developing a sound approach for rolling out microcredentials in the years ahead.

That left little feedback time on each individual course. My team was often jumping from department
to department, filming lectures, meeting stakeholders and shaping our approach from student feedback
on full units. The real test comes in 2027, when the first MBA microcredentials are released: they're
designed from the feedback and insight my team has gathered through the rollout of a number of
non-credit-bearing short courses, and they'll show how those lessons translate into learner outcomes.

## Where agile goes wrong in course builds

1. **Changing a course under enrolled learners.** Agile's habit of frequent release fits a course
   still in development. Once people are enrolled, batch changes and tell learners what moved,
   especially if an assessment changes.
2. **Treating rapid authoring as agile.** Building fast in a template tool isn't the same as building
   in reviewed cycles. Without the review and the re-plan, it's just a quicker waterfall.
3. **Experts who can't keep the rhythm.** If reviews slip every sprint, lengthen the sprint or book
   the review slots for the whole build on day one.
4. **More meetings, same course.** As a chapter in EdTech Books puts it, "Regular communication with
   team members and clients takes time and can slow down some aspects of design"
   ([Cullen](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/3700)). For fixed content with one sign-off, a lightweight
   ADDIE run in loops may serve you better.

## What to do next

Take the course you're about to build and write its first five learner stories, each with the practice
task or quiz item that would show a learner can do it. Then write your definition of done, with three
learner testers in it, before you plan a single sprint.
