Bloom's taxonomy sorts what you want learners to do by the kind of thinking it takes: **remember**,
**understand**, **apply**, **analyse**, **evaluate** and **create**. For course design, its real
use isn't choosing verbs. It's checking. Put every objective, every practice activity and every
quiz question on the same grid, and you can see at once whether your course asks learners to do
anything beyond recall, and whether your assessments test the thinking your objectives promise.

Most pages on Bloom's taxonomy stop at the six levels and a list of verbs. This guide works through
one course from start to finish, uses the half of the revised taxonomy most guides leave out (the
kind of knowledge), and looks at what the research says about the most common way the taxonomy is
misused: as a ladder learners must climb one rung at a time.

## What Bloom's taxonomy is, in its revised form

The original *Taxonomy of Educational Objectives* was published in 1956 by Benjamin Bloom and
colleagues. It sorted objectives into six categories: knowledge, comprehension, application,
analysis, synthesis and evaluation. A revision, led by Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl, was
published in 2001. Krathwohl, one of the original authors, summarised the changes a year later
([Krathwohl, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4104_2)):

- The categories became verbs, so they describe what a learner does.
- Synthesis was renamed **create** and moved to the top, above evaluate.
- Each objective now has two parts: a **cognitive process** (the verb) and a **kind of
  knowledge** (the noun).

The six cognitive processes, in Krathwohl's definitions, with an example objective from a short
gardening course we'll use throughout:

| Process | Krathwohl's definition | Example objective |
|---|---|---|
| Remember | "Retrieving relevant knowledge from long-term memory" | Name the five most common pests in a home vegetable garden. |
| Understand | "Determining the meaning of instructional messages, including oral, written, and graphic communication" | Explain why an overwatered plant wilts. |
| Apply | "Carrying out or using a procedure in a given situation" | Mix a liquid fertiliser at the label rate for a bed of a given size. |
| Analyse | "Breaking material into its constituent parts and detecting how the parts relate to one another" | From photos of a damaged plant, distinguish nutrient deficiency from pest damage. |
| Evaluate | "Making judgments based on criteria and standards" | Choose between two treatments for an infestation and justify the choice. |
| Create | "Putting elements together to form a novel, coherent whole or make an original product" | Plan a season's care schedule for a garden you haven't seen before. |

The four kinds of knowledge, again in Krathwohl's words:

- **Factual:** "The basic elements that students must know to be acquainted with a discipline or
  solve problems in it." The names of pests, the symptoms of overwatering.
- **Conceptual:** "The interrelationships among the basic elements within a larger structure that enable them to
  function together."
  Why nitrogen deficiency yellows older leaves first.
- **Procedural:** "How to do something." How to test soil, how to mix a spray.
- **Metacognitive:** "Knowledge of cognition in general as well as awareness and knowledge of one's
  own cognition." Knowing when you can't tell two problems apart and need a second opinion.

Cross the two dimensions and you get a grid of 24 cells, which Krathwohl and his colleagues called
the Taxonomy Table. That grid is the tool.

The example course is invented, but typical of what independent creators sell: a horticulturist
building a short self-paced course that teaches home gardeners to diagnose and fix common plant
problems.

## Step 1: Write the objectives, then place each one on the grid

Start with objectives you've already written. If you haven't, our guide to
[writing learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/) covers how; the
performance goal from the analysis phase of the [ADDIE model](/guides/addie-model/) is the place
to start.

Then place each objective in one cell: which process does it ask for, and what kind of knowledge
does it work on? Here is the gardener's first draft, eight objectives:

| Objective | Process | Knowledge |
|---|---|---|
| Name the five most common pests | Remember | Factual |
| List the symptoms of overwatering | Remember | Factual |
| Recall the three main plant nutrients | Remember | Factual |
| Identify common leaf diseases from a chart | Remember | Factual |
| Explain why overwatered plants wilt | Understand | Conceptual |
| Describe the steps of a soil test | Understand | Procedural |
| Mix a fertiliser at the label rate | Apply | Procedural |
| Name the safety gear for spraying | Remember | Factual |

<Callout>
  Draw the six processes across the top of a page and the four kinds
  of knowledge down the side. Write each objective's number in its cell. If you can't decide
  between two cells, that is useful too: the objective is probably vague.
</Callout>

## Step 2: Read the gaps

Now look at where the numbers fall. Krathwohl describes this as one of the main uses of the table:

<ResearchCard n={0} />

The gardener's grid has five of eight objectives in one cell, remember-factual, and nothing at all
beyond apply. Yet the course promise is "diagnose and fix common plant problems": analysis and
evaluation, on conceptual and procedural knowledge. The grid shows the course as drafted would
teach learners to recite pest names and leave them stuck in front of a real yellowing tomato plant.

This pattern is common. Krathwohl notes that when the original taxonomy was used to analyse
curricula and tests, "almost always, these analyses have shown a heavy emphasis on objectives
requiring only recognition or recall of information" ([Krathwohl, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4104_2)).

A gap doesn't mean every cell needs filling. A short course might sensibly have nothing in
create. The question is whether the cells you've filled match the job the learner will do
afterwards. The gardener's revision:

| Revised objective | Process | Knowledge |
|---|---|---|
| Recognise the five most common pests and their damage | Remember | Factual |
| Explain how water, light and nutrients each show up in a leaf | Understand | Conceptual |
| Carry out a soil test and read the result | Apply | Procedural |
| Distinguish nutrient, water and pest problems from photos of an unfamiliar plant | Analyse | Conceptual |
| Choose a treatment, or decide to wait, and justify it | Evaluate | Procedural |
| Notice when a diagnosis is uncertain and say what would settle it | Evaluate | Metacognitive |

Fewer objectives, and every one of them now points at the course promise.

## Step 3: Put activities and assessments in the same cells

An objective on the grid is only a promise. The table earns its keep when you plot the practice and
the assessment for each objective in the same cell. Krathwohl notes it can classify "the
instructional and learning activities used to achieve the objectives", and the assessments too.
If an analyse objective is practised with a recall quiz, the grid makes the mismatch visible.

For the gardener's analysis objective:

| | What it looks like | Cell |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | Distinguish nutrient, water and pest problems from photos of an unfamiliar plant | Analyse, conceptual |
| Practice | Six photo cases, each with a "what's your diagnosis?" question and feedback explaining the clues | Analyse, conceptual |
| Assessment | Four new photo cases the course hasn't shown, diagnosed with a one-line reason | Analyse, conceptual |

One catch is easy to miss: a question's level depends on what the learner has already seen.
Crowe, Dirks and Wenderoth, who built a tool for rating biology exam questions on Bloom's taxonomy,
point out that if students are given the answer to an analysis question in class and then meet the
same question on an exam, "then that question only requires recall"
([Crowe et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1187/cbe.08-05-0024)). If the gardener's assessment reuses
the practice photos, it has quietly become a memory test. New cases keep it at analysis.

Rating questions also takes judgement. In the same study, at least two of the three raters agreed on a
question's level more than 91% of the time, which the authors report as high reliability. That's
good, but it isn't automatic. If you can, ask a colleague to place your questions on the grid
without seeing your labels, and talk about the ones you disagree on.

## Step 4: Don't climb the ladder one rung at a time

The taxonomy is often drawn as a pyramid, and the pyramid invites an assumption: learners must
master the facts at the bottom before they can analyse or judge anything. Many courses follow it
literally, with several modules of definitions before the first real problem.

The revision itself softened this. In Krathwohl's words, "the requirement of a strict hierarchy has
been relaxed to allow the categories to overlap one another"
([Krathwohl, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4104_2)). And there is some direct evidence
on the assumption. Agarwal ran two experiments with university students in a laboratory and a third
in a sixth-grade classroom, testing retrieval practice (recalling information from memory rather
than re-reading it) with quizzes of fact questions, higher order questions, or a mix
([Agarwal, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000282)).

<ResearchCard n={1} />

Fact quizzes improved performance on a later fact test, higher order quizzes improved performance
on a later higher order test, and a mix of questions helped on both kinds of test in the second and
third experiments. The evidence comes from a small number of experiments in specific subjects, so
we'd call it some evidence rather than settled. But it gives no support to the idea that a course
has to finish the facts before it reaches the thinking.

In practice, we'd mix levels from the first module. The gardener can open with a photo of a sick
plant and ask for a guess, teach the three nutrient symptoms, then return to the same photo and ask
again. The facts are learned in the act of using them. Our guide to
[writing multiple-choice questions](/guides/writing-multiple-choice-questions/) shows how to write
quiz questions that test application and analysis rather than recall.

## Step 5: Check the whole course on one page

Once every objective, activity and assessment is on the grid, look at the whole course on one page.
Three questions are worth asking:

1. **Do the assessments sit in the same cells as the objectives?** An objective with no assessment
   in its cell is one you can't show learners have met.
2. **Is the mix right for the course promise?** A course about doing something should have most
   of its weight in apply, analyse and evaluate, with recall objectives only where they support them.
3. **Is there any metacognitive knowledge?** It is the row most courses leave empty, and for
   self-paced learners, who have no teacher watching, knowing what they don't yet know matters.

The grid also helps with load. If one lesson asks for analysis of new cases before learners have
the concepts they need, it will feel overwhelming; our library entry on
[cognitive load theory](/library/cognitive-load-theory/) explains why, and the fix is usually a
worked example first, not a module of definitions.

When developing online courses at Avondale University, we have to make a conscious effort to build out the multi modal interaction with the material. University lecturers are often great in a classroom, but translating that personal touch into online learning, particularly asynchronous courses, forces the microcredential team to build in reflections, interactivity and personalisation to make sure the learner is putting the knowledge to use.

## Bloom's taxonomy examples across three courses

The same six processes look different in different subjects. Here is one objective at each level
for three typical online courses:

| Process | Home gardening | Spreadsheets for a finance team | Customer service training |
|---|---|---|---|
| Remember | Name the five most common pests | Recall the shortcut for an absolute cell reference | List the steps of the refund policy |
| Understand | Explain why overwatered plants wilt | Explain why a formula breaks when copied down a column | Explain why the policy limits refunds to 30 days |
| Apply | Mix a fertiliser at the label rate | Build a lookup that pulls prices from a second sheet | Process a standard refund in the system |
| Analyse | Distinguish pest from nutrient damage in an unfamiliar plant | Find the error in a colleague's broken budget model | Identify what a frustrated customer actually wants from a transcript |
| Evaluate | Choose between two treatments and justify it | Judge which of two forecast models is better suited to a decision | Decide whether an exception to the policy is justified |
| Create | Plan a season's care for a new garden | Design a reusable monthly reporting template | Write a response guide for a new type of complaint |

Notice that "explain", "identify" and "choose" could sit at more than one level depending on the
task. That is the problem with verb lists: when Stanny analysed 30 published lists, 45.5% of the
verbs appeared at two or more levels, and "choose", "relate" and "select" appeared at all six
([Stanny, 2016](https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci6040037)). The task decides the level, not the verb.

## Four common mistakes

1. **Using the taxonomy only to pick verbs.** A verb from the "analyse" column doesn't make an
   analysis objective. Check the task.
2. **Treating higher as better.** Remember objectives are fine when the job needs instant recall.
   The goal is a match with the job, not a course full of "create".
3. **Testing at a lower level than you taught.** Or, more often, reusing practice items in the
   final quiz, which turns analysis into memory.
4. **Saving the thinking for the end.** Mix levels from the first module rather than spending the
   first half of the course on definitions.

## What to do next

Take your current course outline and place every objective and every final-quiz question on a
six-by-four grid. Circle any objective with no question in its cell. That list is your revision
plan.
