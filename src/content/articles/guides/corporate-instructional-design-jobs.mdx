Search for corporate instructional design jobs and you get job boards: thousands of postings, none of
which tell you what changes when you move from a school or university into a company. The research on
job ads gives a first answer, and it starts with the degree. In a study of 93 US instructional design
job ads from 2016, corporate postings were the least likely of any sector to state a formal education
requirement, 67.6 per cent against 93.3 per cent in higher education, and the most likely to ask for
eLearning authoring software ([Raynis, 2018](https://core.ac.uk/download/229375901.pdf)). It's a small,
dated US sample, so treat it as a map, not a measurement. But a 2023 interview study heard the same
from a corporate hiring leader ([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606)).

This guide sets out what the research says changes in corporate roles, step by step, with an example of
how each change shows up in the work, and ends with how to show an employer you've made the shift. If
you want the wider picture of where instructional design jobs are and what all employers ask for, start
with our guide to [instructional design jobs](/guides/instructional-design-jobs/), which found six in
ten US postings in a 2023 sample were corporate.

## What the research compares

Two job-ad studies and one interview study do most of the work here, and all three have limits worth
knowing before the numbers.

Marina Raynis collected 93 US instructional design postings from Monster, Indeed, ATD and EDUCAUSE in
September and October 2016 and coded what each asked for. Thirty-four were corporate, 34 were in
health, 15 in higher education, six in non-profits and four in government or the military
([Raynis, 2018](https://core.ac.uk/download/229375901.pdf), Table 3). The corporate and higher education
groups are small, so a difference of a few ads moves a percentage a long way.

Weber, McCarthy, Campbell and Bauer analysed 130 US postings from May 2023 and interviewed 12 hiring
leaders, three from each of corporate, higher education, non-profit and school settings. They pooled
the ads across sectors, so their sector findings come from the interviews: three people per sector
([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606)). A third study of 100 postings, by North
and colleagues, reports that educational requirements were "statistically significantly associated with
job sectors" ([North et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11528-021-00636-2)); we could read only its
abstract, so we use it for that one point.

The idea that the sectors differ isn't new. A case study of a graduate programme that prepared designers
for business careers opened with it.

## The chart: corporate against higher education

<Chart id="raynis-2018-corporate-vs-higher-ed" />

Four requirements separate the two sectors most clearly in Raynis's data. The sections below take each
one, plus two changes the job ads only hint at: who you work for and how your work is judged.

## 1. The proof you need changes: less degree, more evidence

Corporate ads were the only group in which Raynis's "Knowledge" category was mentioned more often than
a formal education requirement, 76.5 per cent against 67.6 per cent. Corporate was the lowest of all
sectors on education: higher education stated a requirement in 93.3 per cent of postings, health in
97.1 per cent and government and military in all of them. Higher education was also the only sector
that required or preferred a doctorate (6.7 and 20 per cent of its ads)
([Raynis, 2018](https://core.ac.uk/download/229375901.pdf), pp. 184–186).

<ResearchCard n={0} />

Weber's interviews, seven years later, point the same way. The authors report that in some sectors,
corporate among them, employers seemed to be moving away from formal degree requirements towards
experience and skills, while a higher education leader said his roles needed a master's, ideally in instructional
design or something adjacent ([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606), pp. 6–7).
That's three corporate interviewees, not a survey, so it supports the job-ad finding rather than
proving it.

**In practice:** a former teacher with a bachelor's and two strong portfolio pieces is a credible
corporate applicant. The same person applying for a university learning designer role may be screened
out by a master's requirement before anyone opens the portfolio. A degree still helps in both; it just
does less of the work in corporate hiring.

## 2. The tools change: authoring software over the LMS

In Raynis's sample, 58.8 per cent of corporate ads asked for eLearning authoring software, against 33.3
per cent of higher education ads. LMS, LCMS or CMS skills ran the other way: 41.2 per cent of corporate
ads and 73.3 per cent of higher education ads ([Raynis, 2018](https://core.ac.uk/download/229375901.pdf),
p. 187).

The pattern fits how the work is organised. Raynis, drawing on earlier research, notes that authoring
tools such as Captivate and Articulate are more prevalent in corporate work, while managing a learning
content management system is more prevalent in higher education
([Raynis, 2018](https://core.ac.uk/download/229375901.pdf), p. 187). University designers are often
the people who support academics in the institution's platform; corporate designers more often build
the module themselves. A higher
education leader in Weber's study put the university view plainly: it helps if a candidate knows an
eLearning tool, "but it's not strictly required"
([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606), p. 13).

**In practice:** if your experience is mostly building in Canvas, Moodle or Blackboard, learn one
authoring tool well enough to build a portfolio piece in it. Our guide to
[instructional design tools](/guides/instructional-design-tools/) covers which ones show up in job ads.

## 3. The job changes: you build the learning, someone else may deliver it

Teaching or training was listed as a responsibility in 80 per cent of higher education ads and 35.3 per
cent of corporate ones ([Raynis, 2018](https://core.ac.uk/download/229375901.pdf), p. 185). University
designers often teach academics how to design, or teach students directly. One higher education team
described in a 2019 survey study said its top priority was "to teach faculty how to be their own
builders of quality online content"
([Magruder et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.24059/olj.v23i3.1546), p. 139).

Corporate designers are more often the builders, and one of Weber's corporate interviewees described
increasingly specialised teams. She said "We no longer are looking for one person to have every skill or ability",
and described a senior learning architect who keeps an eye on every course in a programme
([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606), pp. 9 and 11).

**In practice:** expect to spend more of your week designing, scripting and building, and less running
sessions or coaching others to build. If you love the teaching part, look at corporate roles that
include facilitation, which some L&D teams combine with design.

## 4. Your client changes: a business owner, not a faculty member

In higher education, your main collaborator is usually an academic who owns the course, has academic
freedom and may not have asked for help. Instructional designers have named a lack of faculty buy-in
as their biggest barrier to success
([Magruder et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.24059/olj.v23i3.1546), pp. 140–141, citing a 2016 report).

In corporate work, the person who commissions the course usually owns a business problem: a sales
leader whose new hires take too long to get productive, or a compliance manager facing an audit.
Raynis summarises earlier research describing corporate designers as "more customer-oriented"
([Raynis, 2018](https://core.ac.uk/download/229375901.pdf), p. 187, citing Sugar and colleagues). The
Ohio State chapter on job postings notes that "Many companies turn to training programs for legal
defensibility in some situations"
([Bowman et al., 2019](https://ohiostate.pressbooks.pub/6223ebook2019/?p=20)), which is why compliance
work fills so much of a corporate designer's year.

**In practice:** the first meeting is about the problem, not the content. Ask what people need to do
differently, how the business will know, and what happens if nothing changes. Our guide to
[writing learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/) shows how to turn those answers
into objectives a stakeholder can sign off.

Over the past 20 years, I’ve run a video production company and have often been brought in to create assets for digital learning. During those projects, I was only dealing with a learning designer about half of the time. The other half, it was with someone in a medium-sized business who was wearing multiple hats. I think that holds throughout the industry. Large multinationals usually have an HR team and dedicated trainers. The rest of the businesses out there are looking for curiosity, an ability to create engaging courses, and a willingness to monitor, test and improve those courses on a quarterly or even monthly basis.

## 5. The pace changes, and project management is the constant

Project management is often described as the corporate difference. The ads don't support that. Raynis
found it in 82.4 per cent of corporate postings and 80 per cent of higher education postings, and in
83.9 per cent of all 93 ads ([Raynis, 2018](https://core.ac.uk/download/229375901.pdf), pp. 180 and
184–185). Every sector expects you to run your own projects.

What changes is the shape of the deadline. One higher education team in the 2019 survey study described
a "16-week schedule wherein each designer is their own project manager"
([Magruder et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.24059/olj.v23i3.1546), p. 139), tied to the teaching
calendar. Corporate deadlines follow a product launch, a system go-live or a regulator's date. One
corporate leader in Weber's study said "Production quality has definitely gone up, and we expect it to
be higher", and added that timelines are not always realistic for the quality people want
([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606), p. 12).

**In practice:** get comfortable scoping a project in writing, naming what's out of scope and agreeing
review rounds before you build. Our guide to
[instructional design contract work](/guides/instructional-design-contract-work/) covers those steps,
and they apply just as well inside a company.

## 6. How success is judged changes, though less than you'd expect

You'll often read that corporate designers are judged on business results while university designers
are judged on course quality. The job ads we could read don't show a clean split. In the Ohio State
chapter's small sample, 12 of 25 training-sector postings mentioned evaluating learning impact,
against six of 10 higher education postings
([Bowman et al., 2019](https://ohiostate.pressbooks.pub/6223ebook2019/?p=20)), and in Raynis's data
assessment and evaluation appeared in 91.2 per cent of corporate ads
([Raynis, 2018](https://core.ac.uk/download/229375901.pdf), p. 184). Evaluation is expected everywhere.

What differs is the question you're answering. In a university, success is usually students passing a
well-designed course. In a company, the stakeholder in step 4 will ask whether people now do the job
differently. That's a harder thing to show. We'd lean on the research on
[formative assessment](/library/formative-assessment/) and on
[effective feedback](/library/effective-feedback/) here: in our view, practice with feedback on the real task is
the closest a course gets to evidence of changed performance.

<Callout>
  Try this before you apply: take one course or unit you built in education and rewrite its first page
  for a corporate audience. Replace the topic with a performance problem, the learning outcomes with
  what people will do on the job, and the final exam with a realistic task and how a manager would see
  the result. If you can do that in a page, you can do it in an interview.
</Callout>

## How to show an employer you've made the shift

The research points to evidence over credentials, so build the evidence:

- **One portfolio piece in an authoring tool.** A 10 to 15 minute module on a workplace task, built in
  the kind of tool corporate ads name most. Our guide to
  [instructional design portfolio examples](/guides/instructional-design-portfolio-examples/) shows what
  hiring managers look for.
- **Start the case study with the business problem.** Who needed to do what differently, and why it
  mattered, before any screens.
- **Translate your titles and duties.** "Unit coordinator" becomes project lead; "worked with faculty"
  becomes "worked with subject matter experts and stakeholders"; "LMS course build" becomes the
  deliverable and its result.
- **Read twenty current corporate ads in your market.** These studies are US-only and up to ten years
  old. Count what today's ads ask for, and trust that count over ours.

If you're coming from a classroom rather than a university, our guide to
[instructional design jobs for former teachers](/guides/instructional-design-jobs-for-former-teachers/)
goes deeper on which teaching skills transfer and how to name them.

## What to do next

Pick one course you've built in education and do the rewrite in the callout above. Then rebuild its
best ten minutes in an authoring tool as your first corporate portfolio piece.
