Free online course creation is still possible, but not where most guides send you. As of
20 November 2026, none of the four best-known course platforms, <PlatformLink id="thinkific" />,
<PlatformLink id="teachable" />, <PlatformLink id="kajabi" /> and <PlatformLink id="learnworlds" />,
has a free plan. Each offers a free trial of between 7 and 30 days, then charges a monthly fee.
Thinkific's own FAQ puts it plainly: "Thinkific does not have a free plan." Teachable's says the same
([Thinkific, 2026](https://www.thinkific.com/faq/); [Teachable, 2026](https://teachable.com/free)).

Many of the pages that rank for this search still list free Thinkific and Teachable plans, so it's
worth knowing the current position before you plan around one. The better news is that the parts of
a course the learning research backs most strongly, practice questions with feedback, are the
cheapest parts to build. This guide covers what "free" really means on each platform now, where free
course creation still works, and a six-step process for building a course that teaches without
paying for anything.

## What "free" means on the big four platforms now

Here is what each platform's own pricing pages said when we checked them on 20 November 2026. Prices
are in US dollars, and plans change often, so check the current page before you commit.

| Platform | Free plan? | Free trial | Cheapest plan listed | What the cheapest plan leaves out or adds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thinkific | No | 30 days | Start: US$109 a month, or US$82 a month billed annually | Includes quizzes, assignments and certificates; excludes bulk email to students, reporting and analytics, and removing Thinkific branding |
| Teachable | No | 7 days | Starter: US$39 a month, or US$29 a month billed annually | 7.5% transaction fee on Starter; the next plan, Builder (US$89, or US$69 billed annually), has 0% fees with Teachable's payments |
| Kajabi | No | 14 days | Basic: US$179 a month, or US$143 a month billed annually | Up to 5 products and 2,500 people on your list |
| LearnWorlds | No | 30 days | Starter: US$49 a month, or US$44 a month billed annually | US$5 fee per enrolment; no course certificates; free courses not included |

[Kajabi pricing](https://www.kajabi.com/pricing), [LearnWorlds pricing](https://www.learnworlds.com/pricing/).

Two details are worth knowing. Kajabi's help centre also lists a Kickstarter plan at US$89 a month
(US$852 a year), with one product and 250 contacts, which its main pricing page doesn't show
([Kajabi, 2026](https://help.kajabi.com/en/articles/18440112-kajabi-plans-and-pricing)). Although the
help centre identifies Kickstarter as a current plan, it remains unclear whether new customers can
select it through the standard signup process.

Teachable presents a similar discrepancy. Its help centre lists four current self-service plans:
Starter, Builder, Growth and Advanced. Its public pricing page, however, shows Starter, Builder, Growth
and Custom, omitting Advanced. The help centre lists Advanced at US$399 a month, or US$309 a month
billed annually ([Teachable, 2026](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682410-new-teachable-plans-in-june-2025)).
One useful clarification: multiple-choice quizzes with automatic grading are included on all four
self-service plans, including Starter (Teachable, 2026).

The pattern matters more than the prices. The entry plans keep their monthly fee low by taking a
share of each sale or each enrolment, or by leaving out features a course may need, such as
certificates. If you plan to give a course away, LearnWorlds' Starter plan is ruled out entirely,
because it doesn't include free courses.

## Where free course creation still works

Three routes cost nothing to start, and each suits a different kind of course.

**Google Classroom with Google Forms, for a group you already know.** Classroom works with a personal
Google Account outside a school, for settings such as a tutoring centre or homeschool, with some
limits: for example, students can't email classmates
([Google, 2026](https://support.google.com/edu/classroom/answer/7582372?hl=en)). Google says children
aged 13 and under should only use it with a Workspace for Education or Nonprofits account. Google Forms
supplies the practice: its quiz mode builds answer keys for multiple choice, checkboxes, dropdowns,
short answer and grid questions, lets you add written or YouTube video feedback to answers, and can
release scores immediately after each submission
([Google, 2026](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/7032287?hl=en)). It also summarises the most
frequently missed questions, which tells you which lesson needs work.

**Udemy, for reach.** You can publish a free course without paying for a subscription. To charge, you
must apply to become a premium instructor, and a course can only be switched from free to paid once.
Udemy also states that courses you charge for there can't be offered free anywhere else, including
your own site ([Udemy](https://support.udemy.com/hc/en-us/articles/229605668-Instructors-How-to-Set-The-Price-of-Your-Course)).
The trade-off is control: the course lives on Udemy's terms, not yours.

**Moodle, if you or a colleague can run a server.** Moodle describes itself as "free, open-source
software" ([Moodle, 2025](https://support.moodle.com/support/solutions/articles/80000825155-moodlecloud-and-moodle-hosting-options)).
The software costs nothing, but hosting, updates and backups take time or money. Moodle's own hosted
service, MoodleCloud, offers a time-limited free trial with up to 50 users and 1 GB of storage, not a
free plan.

A free trial on one of the big four is a fourth option, but treat it as a deadline. If you write
your course in your own documents first, a trial becomes a way to test a platform with a finished
course, not a race to build one.

## The parts that make a course work cost the least

Most of a course budget goes on production: cameras, editing, branded sites. Most of the learning
comes from somewhere else. In a large review of ten popular study techniques, Dunlosky and colleagues
rated only two as having high utility: practice testing (recalling or applying what you've learned,
through quizzes or exercises) and distributed practice (spreading that practice out over time)
([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)).

The same review gave re-reading and highlighting low utility ratings. It also notes something that
matters for anyone building on no budget: practice testing doesn't need special equipment or
expertise.

The review is about learners studying, mostly in school and university settings, not about online
course design as such. But the principle carries over directly: a course that asks learners to
recall and use what they've just learned will usually teach more than one they only watch. Our
[library entry on retrieval practice](/library/retrieval-practice/) covers the evidence in more depth,
and the [entry on effective feedback](/library/effective-feedback/) covers what to say when a learner
gets a question wrong.

## How to create an online course for free, in six steps

To make each step concrete, we'll follow one invented but typical example: a volunteer coordinator
at a community food bank who wants new volunteers to be able to run a Saturday sorting shift safely
before their first day.

### 1. Name the task learners should be able to do

Write one sentence describing what a learner will be able to do at the end, in terms you could watch.
"Understand food bank operations" is a topic. "Sort a donated crate into keep, check and discard, and
log it correctly" is a task. Our guide to
[writing learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/) covers this in detail.

**Our coordinator's version:** sort a mixed crate, spot the three things that must never go on the
shelf, and log the crate on the shift sheet.

### 2. Write the practice before the lessons

Draft the questions and exercises first, in a plain document you own. Each one should ask learners to
do a piece of the task, not recall a definition. Then write feedback for every wrong option: one
sentence on why it's wrong and what to look at again. Our guide to
[writing multiple-choice questions](/guides/writing-multiple-choice-questions/) shows how to make the
wrong options plausible.

**Our coordinator's version:** ten photos of real crate contents, each with the question "keep,
check or discard?", plus feedback that names the rule. Built in Google Forms, it costs nothing and
shows which photos most volunteers get wrong.

### 3. Plan short lessons that each end in practice

Break the content into short segments, each followed by a few of the questions you've already
written. Spacing a second round of questions a few days later, before the first shift, puts
distributed practice to work too. For segment length, see our guide to
[how long course videos should be](/guides/how-long-should-course-videos-be/).

### 4. Record with what you have

A phone held steady, in a quiet room with light facing you, is enough for most lessons. Plan each
segment in a short script first, so the recording is a reading rather than an improvisation, and
caption every video so learners can follow it without sound. What learners need to see clearly is the
task: the crate, the label, the shift sheet. Your face matters less than the close-up.

One example is the work I've done developing short courses and microcredentials at Avondale University. The *Writing and Producing for Podcasts* microcredential is a good illustration of how much you can achieve by starting with expertise already inside an organisation. We built a practical learning experience around existing academic knowledge, video content, activities and a small number of face-to-face sessions, rather than beginning with a large production budget.

But if I had money to spend at the beginning, I wouldn't necessarily spend it on making the course. I'd spend it on finding out whether people actually want it.

There's a great story about David Rogier, the founder of MasterClass. Before he had the courses, he built a website advertising classes that didn't exist. He bought advertising to drive traffic to the site and see which classes attracted interest. He was testing the market before investing in the product. In his [interview with IVP](https://medium.com/ivpvc/ivps-hypergrowth-podcast-masterclass-ceo-david-rogier-s-top-four-tips-for-hypergrowth-f902dbef267f), he describes building the fake website and using paid traffic to test demand.

That's a lesson I think the short-course industry could learn from. We often put enormous effort into designing, filming and building courses before we've properly established the audience, the problem we're solving or someone's willingness to pay.

If I had a small budget, my first investment would be a landing page, a well-defined offer and a targeted marketing campaign. I'd want to know who clicks, who registers interest, what they're prepared to pay and, ideally, who is willing to pre-purchase. I'd rather spend $2,000 discovering that nobody wants a course than $20,000 producing one that nobody buys.

Build the audience and validate the demand first. Then build the course.

### 5. Choose a free home that fits your learners

Match the route to the audience:

- **A known group** (staff, volunteers, a class): Google Classroom with Google Forms quizzes.
- **Strangers you want to reach**: a free course on Udemy, accepting its terms.
- **An organisation with technical help**: a self-hosted Moodle site.

**Our coordinator's choice:** Google Classroom, because the volunteers are a known group, the food
bank already uses Google accounts, and the quiz summaries show which rules need a better lesson.

### 6. Pilot with a few learners, then fix the weakest lesson

Ask three to five learners to take the course and do the task, and watch where they stumble. The
questions most people miss point to the lesson that needs rewriting. Our guide to
[piloting a course](/guides/how-to-pilot-an-online-course/) sets out a simple version.

## When paying for a platform makes sense

Free tools stop being enough when you need things they don't do: a checkout, certificates learners
can share, tracking across a whole course rather than one quiz at a time, or a site under your own
name. At that point, compare platforms on the learning features you'd use on the plan you'd actually
pay for, and on the fees each plan adds, not on headline prices. Our guide to
[creating an online course that sells](/guides/how-to-create-an-online-course-that-sells/) covers
that stage.

Because you wrote the lessons and questions in your own documents, moving to a paid platform means
copying them across, not starting again.

## Four mistakes to avoid

1. **Planning around a free plan that's gone.** Check the platform's own pricing page, not a
   comparison article, before you build.
2. **Building inside a trial with nothing written down.** If the trial ends before you decide, the
   work is stuck in a tool you're not paying for.
3. **Spending the effort on production instead of practice.** A plain video with good questions after
   it will usually teach more than a polished one with none.
4. **Choosing a home that doesn't fit the audience.** A course for your own volunteers doesn't need a
   marketplace, and a course for strangers rarely finds them in a private classroom.

## What to do next

Open a blank document and write the one task your course should teach, then five practice questions
that ask learners to do part of it, each with feedback on the wrong answers. That document is the
core of your course, and it's the part no platform can sell you.
