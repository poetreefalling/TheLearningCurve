To create a course on <PlatformLink id="teachable" />, you click **Create new product**, give the
course a title, author and thumbnail, choose a price (or skip that for now), and pick how to start
the outline: from scratch, generated with AI, by bulk-uploading files, or by copying an existing
course. Then you build sections and lessons, fill them with content blocks, and publish
([Teachable help centre](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682451-create-and-set-up-your-course)).

That's the part every tutorial covers. The part they skip is a default that decides whether your
quizzes do any work: **Teachable quizzes are non-graded unless you change them**. A non-graded
quiz's scores aren't tracked in your reports, and course compliance, the setting that can stop a
learner skipping ahead, doesn't apply to it
([Teachable help centre](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682455-add-lesson-content)).
Learners still get the practice, but you can't see who's struggling, and a pass mark can't be
required.

This guide walks through the build in eight steps, from Teachable's current help centre (we checked
every page on 27 November 2026), with the learning-design decision that belongs at each one. It's
written for a first course on Teachable, but the same order works on any platform.

## Before you open Teachable: write the practice first

Teachable will happily let you start with a blank outline and fill it with videos. Resist that.
Decide first what learners should be able to *do* at the end, then write the tasks or questions
that would show you they can, and only then plan the lessons that get them there. That order is
[backward design](/guides/backward-design/), and it stops a course drifting into everything the
expert knows.

In practice, that means two documents before you touch the platform: a short list of
[learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/) written as observable actions, and
a draft of every quiz question and practice task, one set per section. Those drafts become your
quiz blocks in step four.

<Callout>
  **Try this before you build:** for each section, write three quiz questions that a learner could
  only answer after the lesson, not before it. If you can't write them, the section doesn't have a
  clear job yet.
</Callout>

## Step 1: Create the course

You can start a course from three places: **Create new product** on the dashboard, the **+** next
to Products in the sidebar, or **New course** on the Courses page. Each opens the same wizard
([Teachable help centre](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682451-create-and-set-up-your-course)):

1. **Basic details.** A course title, an author, an optional description and a thumbnail. The
   thumbnail is 16:9; Teachable recommends 1024 × 576 pixels.
2. **Pricing (optional).** One-time purchase, payment plan, subscription or free. You can skip this
   and come back.
3. **Outline.** Generate with AI, start from scratch, bulk upload, or copy from another course.
   Bulk upload creates one lesson per file.

When you click **Finish**, Teachable opens a setup guide: update the title and thumbnail, preview
the curriculum, add pricing plans, choose a sales page (a course sales page, a product details page
or an external URL), set a thank-you page, then publish.

On the AI outline: treat it as a list of topics to argue with, not a course. An outline generator
can't know your learners or what they get wrong, which is what the analysis and practice drafts are
for. If you use it, check every section against your objectives and delete what doesn't serve one.

## Step 2: Build sections and lessons

A Teachable course is a set of **sections**, each holding **lessons**. There's no limit on how many
of either you create. Add a section with **New Section** at the bottom of the Curriculum tab, and a
lesson with **New lesson** inside a section. Drag the six-dot handle to reorder sections and lessons
or move a lesson to another section
([Teachable help centre](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682453-sections-and-lessons)).

Three behaviours worth knowing early:

- **New lessons start unpublished**, and unpublished lessons are hidden from students. A section
  whose lessons are all unpublished doesn't appear at all.
- **Bulk upload** turns each file into its own lesson, added in alphabetical order, so number your
  files before uploading. Teachable says it's available on paid plans.
- **Public preview** makes a lesson visible to anyone, and Teachable warns that preview content is
  searchable by search engines. Use it for a genuine sample lesson, not for something you'd rather
  keep behind the paywall.

The design decision here is the size of each lesson. Give every lesson one job: one idea, one
worked demonstration, or one practice task. If you're planning video, our guide to
[how long a course video should be](/guides/how-long-should-course-videos-be/) explains why short,
segmented videos help, and why the popular "six-minute rule" is weaker than it sounds. Splitting
material into small steps also keeps the load on working memory manageable, which is the core of
[cognitive load theory](/library/cognitive-load-theory/).

## Step 3: Add content blocks to each lesson

Open a lesson and click **Add Content**. Teachable's lesson editor builds a lesson from blocks:
text and images, video, PDF viewer, audio, banner image, downloadable resource, code example,
embedded media, quiz, custom code, and marketing blocks such as upsells
([Teachable help centre](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682455-add-lesson-content)). After
adding a block, preview it, then publish it. Files can be up to 20 GB.

The choice that matters most is **upload versus embed** for video. You can upload a video or embed
one from YouTube or Vimeo, but the two behave differently:

| | Uploaded video | Embedded video (YouTube, Vimeo, Loom) |
|---|---|---|
| Course compliance (must watch 90%) | Applies | Doesn't apply |
| Video stats and retention report | Included | Not included |

If you want to see where learners stop watching, or require them to watch before moving on, upload
the file. Teachable's AI captions are included on Growth and Advanced and cost extra on Starter and
Builder ([Teachable help centre](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682410-new-teachable-plans-in-june-2025)).

## Step 4: Add quizzes, and switch grading on

This is the step that makes the course teach rather than just present. The evidence for practice
quizzes is among the strongest in learning science. In a well-known experiment, students who
recalled a prose passage from memory remembered more of it two days and a week later than students
who spent the same time re-reading it, even though re-reading made them feel more confident
([Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)). The authors' own
conclusion is blunt:

Our library entry on [retrieval practice](/library/retrieval-practice/) covers the wider evidence
and its limits. For a Teachable course, the practical rule is: put a short quiz at the end of most
lessons, not one big test at the end of the course.

### How Teachable quizzes work

A quiz is a content block. In the lesson editor, choose **Quiz**, add a question and its answers,
mark the correct ones (more than one is allowed), write feedback for the answers, and add more
questions with **New question**. Press **Save quiz** before you leave
([Teachable help centre](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682455-add-lesson-content)).

The details that shape your design:

- **Question types:** multiple choice, fill in the blank, and matching pairs.
- **One quiz block per lesson.** If you want practice midway through and at the end, split the
  lesson in two.
- **Non-graded by default.** Turn grading on in the quiz settings. Graded quizzes report scores in
  the Reports tab and respect compliance settings; non-graded quizzes do neither.
- **AI-generated questions** are available through the Generate questions option. Edit every one
  before saving: a question has to test your objective, not whatever the lesson happened to
  mention.

### Write feedback for every answer

Feedback is the second half of a good practice quiz. Multiple-choice questions carry a known risk:
learners can come away remembering a wrong option they picked. In Butler and Roediger's experiment,
feedback after a multiple-choice test, immediate or delayed, raised correct answers on a later test
and reduced those wrong-answer intrusions compared with no feedback
([Butler & Roediger, 2008](https://doi.org/10.3758/MC.36.3.604)).

<ResearchCard n={1} />

Teachable's answer feedback field is where that happens. Don't settle for "Correct" and
"Incorrect": say why the right answer is right, and what the tempting wrong answer gets wrong. Our
guides to [writing multiple-choice questions](/guides/writing-multiple-choice-questions/) and
[effective feedback](/library/effective-feedback/) go further.

## Step 5: Decide on compliance and drip

### Course compliance

Course compliance makes learners complete the course in a set way. It has three settings
([Teachable help centre](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682463-course-compliance)):

- **Enforce lesson order:** learners move through lessons in sequence, marking each with **Complete
  and continue**. They can still revisit completed lessons.
- **Enforce video watching:** learners must watch at least 90% of an uploaded video before moving
  on.
- **Enforce quiz completion:** learners must complete a graded quiz to advance. You set a minimum
  score (zero means they only have to finish it) and up to 99 retake attempts. A learner who runs
  out of retakes is stuck until you reset their quiz scores from their progress report.

Turning on either of the last two automatically turns on lesson order. Teachable lists compliance
on all plans ([Teachable help centre](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682410-new-teachable-plans-in-june-2025)).

Use it with care. Enforced order suits a course where later lessons genuinely depend on earlier
ones, or where you need evidence of completion (compliance training, continuing education). For
experienced learners, forcing every video can slow them down without teaching them more; the
[expertise reversal effect](/library/expertise-reversal-effect/) is the research version of that
warning. A middle path: enforce quiz completion with a modest pass mark and generous retakes, so the
practice is required but the route through it isn't. If you lock quizzes, watch your inbox:
Teachable emails you when a learner fails out, and they stay stuck until you reset them.

### Drip content

Drip releases content over time instead of all at once. On Teachable it works on **sections, not
lessons**, either on a specific date (at least 48 hours ahead) or a set number of days after each
learner enrolled. Sections release at midnight UTC, and you can attach an email announcement to each
release ([Teachable help centre](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682465-drip-content)).

Drip is useful when you want learners to space their study, or to keep a cohort working together.
Because it releases whole sections, plan your sections around the pacing you want: a week's work,
including its practice quiz, per section. For Australian learners, remember that midnight UTC is
mid-morning on the east coast.

## Step 7: Preview, pilot, then publish

Before anyone pays, preview the course as a student would, then run a small pilot: three to five
people from your target audience working through one finished section while you watch where they
hesitate. Our guide to [piloting an online course](/guides/how-to-pilot-an-online-course/) explains
how to run one cheaply. Fixing a confusing quiz question after five testers costs minutes; after
launch, it costs refunds.

When you publish from the course's Information tab, Teachable asks whether to publish everything
or just the course pages
([Teachable help centre](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682451-create-and-set-up-your-course)):

- **Yes, publish all** publishes every page and lesson.
- **No, just publish course** publishes the pages but keeps unpublished lessons hidden, which is
  handy if you're launching with later sections still in progress.

A course needs at least one published lesson before students can access anything.

## Step 8: Read the reports after launch

Teachable's Reports tab has five reports: lesson completion, video stats, quiz scores, leaderboards
and open-ended answers. All except video stats export to CSV
([Teachable help centre](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682570-course-reporting-tools)).
Two caveats decide how much they can tell you:

- **Lesson completion** only counts a lesson when the learner clicks **Complete and continue**. A
  learner who watched everything but never clicked looks like a drop-out.
- **Quiz scores** only include graded quizzes, which is why step four matters. The report shows the
  average, minimum and maximum score and how many learners completed each quiz.

Look first for the question most learners get wrong and the video where retention drops. Those two
places are where your next revision should go.

## Five common mistakes

1. **Leaving quizzes ungraded by accident.** The quiz still works for learners, but you lose the
   data and can't require a pass mark.
2. **Embedding videos you wanted to track.** Compliance and video stats only cover uploaded videos.
3. **Publishing the AI outline as the course.** It lists topics; it doesn't contain your practice
   or your judgement.
4. **Locking everything.** Enforced order, enforced watching and a high pass mark together can stall
   capable learners. Enforce what the course genuinely needs.
5. **Choosing a plan before checking certificates.** Confirm the tier that includes them first.

## What to do next

Before you sign up or build anything, write the three practice questions for your first section,
with feedback for every answer. Then build that one section in Teachable, quiz switched to graded,
and give it to three people from your audience. What they get wrong tells you how to build the rest.
