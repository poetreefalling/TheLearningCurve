To test an online course before launch, run it past four kinds of check, in order. First a
**functional check**: every link, video, quiz, certificate and email, on the devices your learners
use. Then an **expert review** of the content. Then **think-aloud sessions**, where three to five
people from your audience work through one module while you watch. Finally a **pilot cohort**: a
small group who take the whole course, start to finish, before anyone else.

Most guides to eLearning testing stop at the first round. They're good on broken links and browser
quirks and say almost nothing about whether the course teaches. This guide covers all four rounds,
with what to look for in each, how many people you need, and how to turn what you find into fixes.

## Four questions, four kinds of test

Course testing gets muddled because one word covers several jobs. Separate them by the question
each one answers:

| Round | The question | Who does it | When | What you get |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Functional check | Does everything work? | You, plus one fresh pair of eyes | Each module as it's built, and again before launch | A bug list |
| Expert review | Is it right, and is it the right content? | A subject expert, ideally also a course designer | Outline, then first built module | Content corrections |
| Think-aloud sessions | Can learners follow it? | Three to five people from your audience | First built module | A list of the places people stall, misread or give up |
| Pilot cohort | Can learners do the task at the end? | Five to fifteen people from your audience | Whole course, before public launch | Final-task results, drop-off points, a revision list |

The last three are what instructional designers call formative evaluation (testing a course while
you can still change it, as opposed to judging it once it's finished). The classic sequence, from
Walter Dick and Lou Carey's model of instructional design, moves from one learner at a time to a
small group to a field test, with the materials closer to finished at each stage. As one study
summarising it puts it, "the one-to-one evaluation occurs in the earliest phase of product
development", while the small-group evaluation comes "when the materials are almost in the final
form" ([Chinien, 1990](https://cjlt.ca/index.php/cjlt/article/download/26785/20897)). That order is
the point: the earlier you watch a learner, the cheaper the fix.

To make each round concrete, we'll follow one example: an HR consultant building a short
self-paced course that teaches new managers to run a useful one-to-one meeting with each member of
their team. The final task is a plan for a real one-to-one, plus written responses to three
scenarios. It's an invented example, but typical of the courses independent creators build.

## Step 1: Decide what "ready" means

Before you test anything, write down what would make you launch, and what would make you stop.
Without that, every round produces a pile of opinions and no decision.

A useful set of launch criteria covers the four questions:

- **It works:** no broken links, videos or quizzes on the devices on your list; completion and
  certificates trigger correctly.
- **It's right:** the subject expert has signed off every module.
- **Learners can follow it:** no step where most think-aloud testers got stuck.
- **It teaches:** a set share of pilot learners complete the final task to the standard in your
  [learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/).

For the HR consultant, the last criterion might read: "At least two-thirds of pilot learners submit
a one-to-one plan that meets the rubric, without help from me." The exact share is a judgement
call. What matters is that it's written down before the results arrive.

## Step 2: Run the functional check

Treat the course as software, because it is. The functional check is the round most guides cover
well, so here it is as a short list rather than a long one:

- **Every path through the course**, as a learner would take it: enrol through the real checkout
  or invitation with a test account, not only the course builder's preview.
- **Every device and browser on your list.** At minimum a phone, a laptop and whatever your
  learners use at work. Play every video on mobile data as well as wifi.
- **Every quiz**, answered right and wrong. Check that scoring, feedback, retake rules and pass
  marks do what you intended.
- **Every automated message**: welcome email, reminders, completion and certificate.
- **The awkward behaviour**: skipping ahead, going back, leaving halfway and returning a week
  later, finishing on a different device.
- **Accessibility basics**: captions on every video, transcripts, readable contrast, and the whole
  course usable with a keyboard alone. The current web accessibility standard, WCAG 2.2, was
  published as a W3C Recommendation on 5 October 2023 and added criteria such as minimum target
  sizes and alternatives to dragging ([W3C, 2023](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/new-in-22/)),
  both of which matter for drag-and-drop activities on a phone.

Then hand the same list to someone who didn't build the course. Builders click where they know the
button is; a fresh tester finds the button isn't where they expected.

## Step 3: Get an expert review

A subject expert checks two things: that nothing is wrong, and that nothing important is missing or
out of date. If you are the expert, find another one. Your own blind spots are exactly what a
reviewer is for.

Give the reviewer the objectives and the final task as well as the content, and ask specific
questions: Would someone who passes this assessment be able to do the job? Is anything here
something a beginner doesn't need yet? What would a practitioner do differently?

For the HR consultant, a reviewer who manages a team might point out that the scenarios all involve
a cooperative employee, and that new managers most need practice with the one-to-one that goes
badly. That's a design fix, and it's far cheaper at this stage than after recording.

If you can, also ask someone who designs courses to look at the structure: whether each objective
has practice and an assessment, and whether the practice comes before the test. Our
[online course design checklist](/guides/online-course-design-checklist/) covers what they'd look for.

## Step 4: Watch three to five learners, thinking aloud

This is the round that finds the most, and the one most creators skip. Build one complete module,
including its practice and quiz, then sit with one learner at a time while they work through it.

The method is the think-aloud test from usability research: "you ask test participants to use the
system while continuously thinking out loud" ([Nielsen, 2012](https://nngroup.com/articles/thinking-aloud-the-1-usability-tool/)).
You hear where they're unsure, what they think an instruction means, and why they skipped a video,
at the moment it happens, rather than reconstructed afterwards in a survey.

How many people? Usability research suggests far fewer than most people assume. Jakob Nielsen's
analysis, based on a model he developed with Thomas Landauer, estimated that a first round of five
users finds about 85% of the usability problems in a design, and recommended spending a budget of
15 users on three rounds of five, fixing problems between each ([Nielsen, 2000](https://nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/)).
Two cautions apply. That figure is about usability, finding where people get stuck, not about
measuring learning. And it assumes testers are alike:

So if your course serves two quite different groups, such as first-time managers and experienced
managers new to the company, test a few of each rather than five of one.

### Running a session

1. **Recruit from your real audience**, not friends or colleagues who know the subject. For the HR
   consultant, that means people who have recently become managers.
2. **Explain the purpose**: you're testing the course, not them, and confusion is useful
   information. Ask permission before recording the screen or audio, and say how you'll use it.
3. **Give a realistic starting point**: "You've just enrolled. Work through module one as you
   would at home." Then stay quiet. Nielsen's advice is blunt, "Shut up and let the users do the
   talking", because interruptions "can very easily change user behavior".
4. **Prompt only to keep them talking** ("What are you thinking now?"), not to help. If someone is
   truly stuck, note it, help them past it, and treat it as the most important finding of the session.
5. **Debrief for five minutes**: what was hardest, what they'd skip, what they expected that wasn't
   there.

Note every stall, misreading, skipped item and wrong quiz answer, with the time it happened. After
three sessions, the same few problems usually keep appearing. Fix those, then test the revised
module with two or three new people.

  **Try this before you record anything:** run a think-aloud session on your module outline and
  quiz drafted as plain text. Testers can't fix production problems, but they'll show you which
  instructions they misread and which questions they can answer without the lesson. Both are
  cheaper to fix before filming.

## Step 5: Run a pilot cohort through the whole course

The pilot answers the question the earlier rounds can't: can learners get from enrolment to the
final task, at their own pace, without you beside them? Run it once the whole course is built, with
a group of five to fifteen people from your audience and a firm end date.

Collect three kinds of evidence:

- **Can they do the task?** Final-task results, marked against the same rubric you'll use after
  launch. This is the main result. For the HR consultant: how many plans meet the standard, and
  which scenarios trip people up?
- **Where do they slow down or stop?** Lessons where people drop out, and quiz questions most of
  the group gets wrong, point to specific fixes. Our guide to
  [online course completion rates](/guides/online-course-completion-rates/) covers what to look for
  in the data, and [formative assessment](/library/formative-assessment/) explains how low-stakes
  checks along the way show you where learners are before the final task does.
- **What do they say?** A short survey at the end with specific questions, and ideally a follow-up
  a few weeks later asking whether they've used what they learned.

### Why satisfaction isn't enough

It's tempting to treat a happy pilot group as a pass. The research suggests caution. In a
randomised crossover experiment with introductory physics students at Harvard, the same students
were taught some topics through active learning and others through lectures by experienced,
highly rated instructors, with identical handouts. Active instruction raised test scores by 0.46 standard deviations, but lowered
students' feeling of learning by 0.56 standard deviations
([Deslauriers et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1821936116)).

<ResearchCard n={0} />

The authors traced part of the gap to effort: the harder work of active learning felt, to students,
like learning less. That was a university classroom, not a self-paced course, but the lesson
carries over. A pilot module full of practice may get cooler feedback than a smooth video, and
still teach more. Read the survey alongside the final-task results, and when the two disagree,
trust the results. If you want learners to accept effortful practice, tell them why it's there.

## Step 6: Sort the findings and fix in the right place

A pilot produces more findings than you can act on before launch. Sort them by what they cost
learners:

| Severity | What it looks like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Blocks learning | Several testers can't complete a task, misread an instruction the same way, or fail the same question for reasons unrelated to the skill | Fix before launch |
| Slows learning | Testers get there, but slowly or after a detour | Fix before launch if cheap; otherwise list for the first revision |
| Cosmetic | Typos, a slightly awkward layout | Batch and fix once |
| Preference | One tester wants more video, another less | Note it; act only if a pattern appears |

Then trace each fix to where it belongs, as the [ADDIE model](/guides/addie-model/) suggests: a
confusing quiz question is a development fix; testers who already knew everything point back to
analysis. For quiz items that most testers miss, check the question before the lesson: our guide
to [writing multiple-choice questions](/guides/writing-multiple-choice-questions/) covers the
usual causes.

Retest anything you changed substantially. A rewritten scenario needs a new tester, not a note
that says "fixed".

## Common mistakes

1. **Testing only what's easy to test.** Links and videos are quick to check. Whether learners can
   do the task takes a pilot, and it's the result that matters.
2. **Recruiting the wrong testers.** Colleagues and friends know too much, or are too kind. Test
   with people who match the learners you'll sell to.
3. **Helping during sessions.** Every time you explain, you remove the finding you were looking for.
4. **Piloting too late to change anything.** If the whole course is recorded before anyone sees
   it, the pilot can only find problems you can't afford to fix. Test the first module first.
5. **No launch criteria.** Without a written standard, a pilot becomes a debate about how good is
   good enough.

## What to do next

Write your launch criteria today: one line each for "it works", "it's right", "learners can follow
it" and "it teaches". Then book three think-aloud sessions on your first module with people from
your audience, before you build the second.
