Instructional design contract work is a defined job for a client: you agree what you'll build, for
how much and by when, build it in stages the client signs off, and hand it over. Most of that is
project management. The part designers most often leave vague is the one that decides who owns the
result.

In the US, a course you're commissioned to build counts as the client's "work made for hire" only if
you both sign a written agreement that expressly says so
([US Copyright Office, 2024](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf)). Without one, copyright
starts with the author, and a later transfer has to be in writing and signed
([17 U.S.C. 201 and 204](https://copyright.gov/title17/92chap2.html)). Australia's default runs the
same way for most commissioned work
([Legal Services Commission of South Australia](https://lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch11s06.php)). That
applies to genuine contractors, not employees, and it's a default, not advice on your own contract.
But it means a handshake deal can leave a client paying for a course it doesn't own, and a designer
holding rights nobody meant them to have.

This guide walks through a contract project in six steps, with an example at each, and shows where
that ownership clause belongs. If you're still looking for the work itself, start with our guide to
[part-time instructional design jobs](/guides/part-time-instructional-design-jobs/), which covers
where contracts and freelance clients come from.

## 1. Check it's genuinely a contract

"Contractor" is a description of how the work happens, not a word you can put on the paperwork and
make true. In the US, the Internal Revenue Service looks at the whole relationship under the common
law, in three groups of evidence: behavioural control (whether the business has "a right to direct
and control what work is accomplished and how the work is done"), financial control (who pays
expenses, who supplies the tools, whether the worker can make a profit or loss), and the type of
relationship (benefits, permanence, and whether the work is a key part of the business)
([IRS, Topic 762](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc762)). If it's still unclear, either side can ask
the IRS to decide using Form SS-8, though the IRS warns it "may take at least six months" to get an
answer ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/independent-contractor-self-employed-or-employee)).

In Australia, the Fair Work Ombudsman puts it plainly: "It's illegal to represent to a worker that
they're a contractor when the business doesn't reasonably believe this"
([Fair Work Ombudsman](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/find-help-for/independent-contractors/sham-contracting)).
In March 2026 the Ombudsman and the Australian Taxation Office said they were "ramping up their focus
on sham contracting", with the ATO matching contractor payment reports against tax returns, ABN
records, super and payroll data
([FWO and ATO, 2026](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/newsroom/media-releases/2026-media-releases/march-2026/20260313-sham-contracting-media-release)).

This matters to designers for a second reason. The employee half of the US work-for-hire rule depends
on whether the work was made within the scope of employment, judged by the same kind of
common-law test ([US Copyright Office, 2024](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf)). How the
relationship really works affects tax, entitlements and who owns the course.

**Example:** a university offers a designer a "contract" to work 38 hours a week for a year, on its
laptop, to its timetable, reporting to a team lead. That looks more like fixed-term employment than a
contract project, whatever the document calls it. A project to rebuild four modules by March, using
her own equipment and schedule, for a set fee, looks like contract work.

## 2. Scope the project before you price it

Scope is the list of what you'll deliver, and it's where contract projects go wrong. In a 2020 ATD
survey of learning development professionals, limited resources (time, talent and money) were the
most common barrier to developing content faster, cited by 67 per cent of the 112 people who
answered every question. Scope creep, defined as "expanding project deliverables", was one of four
further barriers each cited by between 34 and 39 per cent
([Defelice, 2021](https://td.org/insights/how-long-does-it-take-to-develop-training-new-question-new-answers)).
The others were a lack of standard processes or templates, subject matter experts and stakeholders
who weren't available, and limited tools and systems. Two of those four are about the client's side
of the project, which a contract can address.

The same study also makes a point about estimating. It asked about the time to build whole units of
training, not an "hour of training", because, as the author put it, units "are rarely an hour long".
Estimate from the units you'll actually deliver, and from your own past projects, rather than a
generic ratio of development hours per hour of learning.

A useful scope names:

- **The deliverables and their format:** "six eLearning modules of 10 to 15 minutes, built in the
  client's authoring tool and published as SCORM packages for its LMS", not "an online course".
- **What's out of scope:** voice-over recording, translation, LMS set-up, accessibility remediation
  of the client's existing documents. Writing these down is how you price them later.
- **The client's commitments:** a named subject matter expert, how quickly they'll review drafts, and
  who has final sign-off.
- **The review rounds:** how many rounds of changes each deliverable includes.

**Example:** a training company asks for "a compliance course". The designer's scope document turns
that into a 30-minute module with a ten-question final quiz, two review rounds per stage, the
compliance manager as sole approver, and source content supplied by the client within a week.

## 3. Choose how to charge

There's no reliable public data on how instructional design contractors price their work, so this
section is our judgement rather than a finding. Three models cover most projects:

- **Hourly or daily rates** suit work whose size you can't see yet: fixing an existing course,
  open-ended support, or a placement inside the client's team. The client carries the risk of
  overruns; you need to log time.
- **A fixed price per project** suits a well-defined build. You carry the risk, so it only works when
  the scope from step 2 limits the deliverables and review rounds.
- **Staged pricing** splits the difference: a fixed fee for analysis and design (the learner brief,
  objectives, outline and storyboard), then a separate quote for the build once both sides can see
  it. It suits clients who can't yet say what they need.

Whichever you choose, your rate has to cover the hours you won't bill: quoting, admin, chasing
payment, software licences, insurance, tax and leave. Our guides to
[part-time instructional design jobs](/guides/part-time-instructional-design-jobs/) and
[instructional designer salary](/guides/instructional-designer-salary/) give survey figures on what
freelance and employed designers earn, as a starting point for your own sums.

**Example:** for the compliance module, the designer quotes a fixed fee for the analysis and
storyboard, payable on sign-off, and a second fixed fee for the build. When the client later asks
for a Spanish version, it's already listed as out of scope, so it becomes a new quote rather than an
argument.

## 4. Put it in writing, including who owns the course

The usual document is a contract plus a statement of work (SOW) that holds the project detail. Most
clauses are business terms: price, payment schedule, timeline, sign-off points, cancellation,
confidentiality. The one that's easy to skip, and hard to fix later, is ownership.

<ResearchCard n={0} />

Under US law, a commissioned work can only be a work made for hire if it falls into one of nine
categories, and one of them is "an instructional text": "a literary, pictorial, or graphic work
prepared for publication and intended for use in systematic instructional activities"
([US Copyright Office, 2024](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf)). Other parts of a course,
such as its video, may fit other categories, including "a part of a motion picture or other
audiovisual work". But all four of the circular's conditions have to be met: an eligible category, a
written agreement, express work-for-hire wording, and signatures from everyone. If any one fails,
the circular says, "it is not a work made for hire."

<ResearchCard n={1} />

When a work isn't made for hire, copyright starts with its author. A client can still own it by
assignment, but the Act says a transfer of ownership, "other than by operation of law, is not valid
unless" it is in writing and signed by the owner of the rights or their agent
([17 U.S.C. 204](https://copyright.gov/title17/92chap2.html)).

In Australia, the general rule is that the author owns copyright, and the employer owns work made in
the course of employment. For commissioned work, the Law Handbook notes exceptions for some
categories, such as commissioned private photographs, portraits, sound recordings and films; "In all
other cases of commissioned works however, the author owns copyright." Ownership can be changed by
agreement or assigned in writing
([Legal Services Commission of South Australia](https://lawhandbook.sa.gov.au/ch11s06.php)). The page
was last revised in 2016, so check the current Copyright Act or a lawyer before relying on it. If you
work elsewhere, check your own country's rules.

In practice, we'd make the ownership clause answer five questions:

1. Who owns the finished course: the client, by an assignment or a work-for-hire clause, or you,
   with a licence to the client?
2. When ownership passes: on signing, or on final payment? Tying it to payment protects you if an
   invoice goes unpaid.
3. Which files are included: the published course only, or the editable source files, storyboards
   and scripts too?
4. What you keep: your own templates, interaction patterns and tools you built before the project.
   Name them, so a client doesn't end up owning your starter kit.
5. Whether you can show the work in your portfolio, and with what removed.

Third-party material sits outside all five. Stock images, music, fonts and authoring tool assets come
with their own licences, and the client needs to hold or inherit the right licence to keep using
them after you leave.

This is general information, not legal advice. For a large project, or a template contract you'll use
for years, pay a lawyer in your jurisdiction to review it once.

Contracts are always easy at the beginning, and hard at the end of a job, so it's important to iron out all the understandings about IP ownership, partnership expectations and deliverables as early in the process as possible, and get it approved in writing between all parties. Even if all the project deliverables aren't understood at the beginning of a project, a Heads of Agreement (HOA) should be put together, with a clear date when that will be reviewed and put into a binding contract. When hiring through freelance platforms like Upwork, key milestones and deliverables are built into the job briefing, but if we're hiring from an ad placed on the University website, for instance, we make sure to put firm deliverables in place, with dates for when those deliverables will be needed for mission critical projects.

**Example:** a designer's standard SOW assigns copyright in the finished modules and their source
files to the client on payment of the final invoice, keeps her own interaction templates, and lets
her show two screens of the course in her portfolio with the client's name and content blurred.

## 5. Build in phases, with a sign-off at each

Run the build so that the client approves each stage before you start the next. The phases of the
[ADDIE model](/guides/addie-model/) map neatly onto contract milestones, and each one produces
something the client can sign:

- **Analysis:** a short brief on the learners, the problem and how success will be measured.
- **Design:** the objectives, the course outline and the assessment plan. Our guide to
  [writing learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/) covers the first.
- **Storyboard or script:** every screen or line, approved before you build. An
  [eLearning storyboard](/guides/elearning-storyboard/) is the cheapest place to make changes.
- **Build and review:** an alpha version for content review, then a beta for final checks, each
  counted against the agreed review rounds.
- **Pilot:** where the budget allows, a small test with real learners before launch. Our guide to
  [piloting a course](/guides/how-to-pilot-an-online-course/) shows how.

Sign-offs protect both sides. When a reviewer asks to restructure module two after the storyboard
was approved, the sign-off shows it's a change, and the SOW says how changes are priced.

**Example:** the designer sends the storyboard for the compliance module with a one-line approval
request. The compliance manager approves it with three small edits. When a second manager later asks
for a new scenario, the designer quotes it as a change, citing the signed storyboard.

## 6. Hand over and close the project

A clean handover is what earns repeat work and referrals. Agree what's in it before the last week:

- the published files (SCORM packages, videos, documents), tested in the client's LMS;
- the editable source files, if the contract includes them, with the authoring tool version noted;
- a list of third-party assets and their licences;
- a short maintenance note: where text lives, how to update a quiz question, what not to touch.

Then send the final invoice, confirm in writing that ownership has passed if the contract ties it to
payment, and ask for the two things a freelancer's next project depends on: permission to use the
work in your portfolio, and a referral or short reference. Our
[instructional design portfolio examples](/guides/instructional-design-portfolio-examples/) show how
to present client work once you have that permission.

**Example:** the designer delivers the packages and source files in a shared folder, walks the
client's LMS administrator through the maintenance note on a 20-minute call, invoices, and asks the
compliance manager whether she'd be willing to be a reference.

<Callout>
  Before you send your next quote, write a one-page statement of work with these headings: deliverables,
  out of scope, client commitments, review rounds, price and payment schedule, sign-off points, ownership
  and source files, portfolio rights. Any heading you can't fill is a question for the client.
</Callout>

## What to do next

Take the contract or statement of work from your last project, or the template you plan to use, and
find the ownership clause. If there isn't one, or it doesn't say who owns the source files and when
ownership passes, draft the five answers from step 4 and add them before you sign the next one.
