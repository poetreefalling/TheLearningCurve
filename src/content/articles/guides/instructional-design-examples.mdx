In a free psychology course on Coursera, students who did more of the short practice activities
learned more than students who watched more of the video lectures or read more of the pages. The
researchers estimated the benefit of extra doing at more than six times that of extra watching or
reading ([Koedinger et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1145/2724660.2724681)). That's one course, and
the data were correlational, but it's a better instructional design example than most you'll find
online, because it says what was designed, why, and what happened to learners.

That's the gap this guide fills. The pages that rank for "instructional design examples" mostly list
formats (a job aid, a microlearning module, scenario-based eLearning) or models (ADDIE, Bloom's
taxonomy, SAM). None of the four we read in full reported whether any of its examples worked, and two
presented invented scenarios without labelling them as invented. A format isn't an example of design. A decision is.

Below are four instructional design examples from real courses, each annotated the same way, then a
five-step method for annotating examples of your own.

## What counts as an instructional design example

Instructional design is the work of deciding how people will learn something: what they need to be
able to do, how they'll practise it, how you'll know they can, and what they'll see and read along
the way. So an instructional design example is one of those decisions, shown in context. A useful
one answers five questions:

1. **The problem.** What wasn't working, or what did learners need?
2. **The decision.** What did the designers do, specifically?
3. **The evidence.** What happened to learners, and how was it measured?
4. **The limits.** What can't the evidence tell you?
5. **What to copy.** What would we take into our own course?

Each example below follows that shape. We chose them because each was published with its method and
results, so you can check our reading against the paper.

| Example | The design decision | What was measured | Strength of the evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Psychology MOOC (Coursera) | Interactive practice with feedback alongside the videos | Quiz and final exam scores against what students used | Correlational, with causal inference methods |
| edX videos | Short, planned video segments | How long learners kept watching | Large but observational; engagement, not learning |
| Harvard Medical School | Case questions by email, repeated in spaced cycles | Physical examination knowledge scores | Randomised controlled trial, one school |
| Harvard physics | Small-group problem solving instead of lecture | Test scores and how much students felt they learned | Randomised crossover, two class meetings |

## Example 1: Practice activities instead of more video

**The problem.** Most massive open online courses (MOOCs) are built around video lectures. In
*Introduction to Psychology as a Science*, a 12-week course run by Georgia Tech and Carnegie Mellon
University on Coursera in 2013, the lectures ran 10 to 15 minutes each, with slides. The question
was whether learners got more from those videos or from doing something with the content.

**The decision.** The course embedded interactive activities from Carnegie Mellon's Open Learning
Initiative alongside the videos. There were two kinds: "Learn By Doing" activities, which gave
targeted feedback and hints, and "Did I Get This" self-checks, which didn't give hints. Of the 27,720
people who registered, 9,075 also registered for the activities.

**The evidence.** The researchers used the course logs to count how much each student watched,
read and did, and compared that with quiz and exam scores.

<ResearchCard n={0} />

In their causal model, a one standard deviation increase in doing activities went with a 0.44
standard deviation increase in quiz score. Watching video and reading pages each had coefficients of
about 0.065, so the link for doing was more than six times as strong
([Koedinger et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1145/2724660.2724681)). Watching and reading weren't
worthless: the authors note that students who skipped the activities did better if they at least read
or watched.

**The limits.** This wasn't an experiment. Students chose what to use, and the authors say the
students who signed up for the activities "may simply be better students". They used statistical
controls and a causal search method to reduce that problem, not remove it, and they flag that doing
activities may have taken more time than reading. It is one course, in one subject, in one run.

**What to copy.** When you're tempted to add another video, add a practice activity with feedback
instead. In a self-paced course, that might be three scenario questions after each lesson, each
with an explanation of why the answer is right or wrong. That is
[retrieval practice](/library/retrieval-practice/) (recalling information from memory rather than
re-reading it) with [feedback](/library/effective-feedback/), and both have strong evidence well
beyond this one course.

## Example 2: Short, planned videos

**The problem.** Video is expensive to make, and learners don't always watch it. Before recording,
course teams have to decide how long each video should be and how to film it.

**The decision.** Philip Guo, Juho Kim and Rob Rubin analysed 6.9 million video watching sessions
from four edX courses to see which production choices kept people watching
([Guo, Kim & Rubin, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2556325.2566239)). Their headline recommendation
was to plan lessons before filming and cut them into segments shorter than six minutes. Median
watching time was at most six minutes, however long the video was.

They also compared one course built from lessons planned for online delivery with one that reused
recorded classroom lectures, cut into pieces. The planned course held attention better. The authors
call this a natural experiment, and it rests on one pair of courses.

**The evidence and the limits together.** Here the limit matters more than the result. The study
measured engagement, mostly how long people kept watching, and the authors write that "it is
important not to draw any conclusions about student learning solely from our findings about video
engagement". Keeping people watching is necessary for learning from a video, not proof of it.

**What to copy.** Plan each video around one idea and script it before you film. Six minutes is a
useful ceiling for planning, not a rule. Our guide on
[how long course videos should be](/guides/how-long-should-course-videos-be/) goes through the
research behind that figure and what it leaves out. Then, as Example 1 suggests, put the effort you
save into the practice that follows each video.

## Example 3: Spaced questions by email

**The problem.** Medical students learn the physical examination early, then have to remember it for
years. Knowledge from a single course fades.

**The decision.** At Harvard Medical School, 120 second-year students were randomly assigned to two
groups. One group started a programme of interactive spaced education 12 weeks before the other,
which served as the control. The programme had 36 multiple-choice questions, each a short clinical
scenario with an explanation. Students got one question by email each day, Monday to Saturday, for
six weeks. They clicked through to answer, then saw the correct answer and why. The same questions
then came round again in a second and a third six-week cycle, so a question first seen in week one
returned in weeks seven and 13 ([Kerfoot, Armstrong & O'Sullivan, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-008-0533-0)).

**The evidence.** When the first group reached its third cycle, its mean score was 74.0, against
59.0 for the control group in its first cycle, an effect size of 1.43. Eighty-five per cent of
participants recommended the programme for the following year.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

**The limits.** Fewer students finished each cycle: 88 per cent completed the first, 76 per cent the
second and 71 per cent the third. It was one school and one group of medical students, and the authors
note the trial doesn't show which part did the work, the spacing or the number of cycles.

**What to copy.** Spacing (returning to the same material over days and weeks rather than in one
block) is one of the best-supported principles in the
[science of learning](/library/principles-of-learning-science/). You don't need a medical school to
use it: a weekly email with two questions from earlier modules does the same job in a self-paced
course. Plan for drop-off, and keep each prompt short enough to answer in a minute.

## Example 4: Active learning that feels worse

**The problem.** If learners rate a lesson highly, it's tempting to assume it worked. Course
evaluations, pilot feedback and star ratings all rest on that assumption.

**The decision.** In a calculus-based introductory physics course at Harvard, 149 students were
randomly assigned to two rooms for two class meetings in week 12. In one room, students worked on
problems in small groups before seeing the solutions. In the other, an instructor presented the same
handouts and solutions as a lecture. For the second topic the groups swapped, so every student had
both ([Deslauriers et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1821936116)).

**The evidence.** Test scores were 0.46 standard deviations higher after the active classes. But
students' feeling of learning was 0.56 standard deviations lower. In the authors' words, "students in
the active classroom learn more, but they feel like they learn less". They link the gap to how fluent
a polished lecture feels, to novices' difficulty judging their own learning, and to unfamiliarity
with active learning.

The design response was simple. In a separate course, the instructor gave a 20-minute presentation
near the start of term on active learning, the evidence for it, and why it can feel harder. More
than 65 per cent of students said their feelings about active learning had improved significantly
over the semester. That part is self-report.

**The limits.** This was a face-to-face classroom, two class meetings, at one university. It isn't a
study of online courses.

**What to copy.** Don't judge a design by how it feels to learners, or by how it feels to you when you
pilot it. Test what they can do. Where you ask learners to struggle, say why at the start: one short
paragraph in the welcome lesson explaining that the practice questions are meant to feel effortful.
Our guide to [piloting an online course](/guides/how-to-pilot-an-online-course/) covers how to
measure the task rather than the satisfaction.

## How to annotate an instructional design example of your own

The four examples share a pattern you can use on your own work, whether it's for a portfolio, a
client report or your own notes. Here's the method, using an invented example: a short self-paced
course teaching café staff to handle food allergy questions.

1. **Name the decision, not the format.** "A scenario-based module" is a format. "We replaced the
   allergen chart video with five customer conversations to answer" is a decision.
2. **State the problem it answered.** Write the evidence you had before you designed anything. For
   the café course: staff could recite the allergen list but froze when customers asked about
   ingredients not on the menu card.
3. **Say what you measured.** Choose a measure that matches the goal. Here, that's the share of staff
   who give a correct, safe answer in a role-play a week later, not how many finished the module or
   rated it highly.
4. **Write the limits.** One venue, twelve staff, no comparison group. Saying so makes the
   example more credible, not less, as each of the studies above shows.
5. **Record what you'd keep and change.** One line on what you'd do again, one on what you'd fix.
   This is the part that shows judgement, and the part most write-ups leave out.

### A real example, annotated: Teaching Students with a Disability

Here is the same method applied to a course Jim built, in his words.

**1. The decision.** When developing a course on Teaching Students with a Disability for Avondale
University, I replaced one of the prescribed readings with a practical classroom-planning exercise.
Rather than simply asking teachers to read about different disabilities, learning needs and inclusive
teaching approaches, I wanted them to apply that knowledge to the realities of their own classrooms.

The activity asked teachers to develop a practical plan for their own teaching environment, considering
the needs of learners with different abilities. They had to think about how they would adapt their
teaching strategies, learning activities and classroom environment to support participation and
learning for a diverse range of students.

**2. The problem it answered.** Teachers could engage with the theoretical material and understand the
principles of inclusive education without translating that understanding into practical teaching
decisions. Lectures and readings can build awareness, but they don't necessarily prepare someone for
the complexity of a real classroom. I wanted to close the gap between knowing what inclusive teaching
looks like in theory and being able to put it into practice.

**3. What I measured.** The primary learning outcome was the quality and practical relevance of the
classroom plan each teacher produced. The exercise let me assess whether participants could identify
diverse learning needs and turn their understanding into appropriate teaching strategies and
adjustments. The plan itself became an applied learning artefact, so I wasn't relying solely on
participation, course completion or a knowledge quiz as evidence of learning.

Note: If formal pre- and post-course assessment data were not collected, I would describe this as an
assessment of applied learning rather than claim demonstrated improvements in classroom practice.

**4. The limits.** The exercise showed teachers' ability to think through and plan for inclusive
classroom practice. But producing a plan is not the same as carrying it out. Without later classroom
observation, follow-up assessment or feedback on implementation, I couldn't establish how well the plans
translated into changes in teaching practice or student outcomes. The activity was a practical
demonstration of learning, not proof of long-term change in behaviour.

**5. What I'd keep and change.** I'd keep the exercise. Replacing a passive reading with an authentic
task gave teachers something tangible to take away and use in their practice. It also made the
learning more personally relevant, because participants were working with the students, teaching
contexts and challenges they actually faced.

What I'd improve is the follow-through. I'd build in a later chance for teachers to revisit their plans
after putting them into practice, reflect on what worked and what didn't, and adjust. That would
create a stronger link between the course and lasting change in the classroom.

## Three mistakes in most example lists

1. **Calling a format an example.** A microlearning module can be well or badly designed. Without
   the decision and the reason, it teaches the reader nothing.
2. **Inventing results.** "Errors dropped and managers got fewer calls" means little unless someone
   counted. If you didn't measure, say what you'd measure next time.
3. **Treating engagement or satisfaction as learning.** Example 2 measured watching time and Example
   4 showed that how learning feels can point the wrong way. Neither is a substitute for checking
   whether learners can do the task.

## What to do next

Take one design decision from your current course and write it up in five lines: the decision, the
problem, the measure, the limits, and what you'd change. If you can't fill in the measure, that's the
first thing to add before your next cohort.
