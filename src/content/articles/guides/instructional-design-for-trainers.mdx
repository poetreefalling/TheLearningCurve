Instructional design for trainers comes down to one shift. In a workshop, you are the course: you
explain, set the practice, watch people try it, answer the question nobody wrote down and change
direction when the room goes quiet. Online, none of that happens unless it was designed in
advance. So turning a workshop into a course isn't a matter of moving the slides. It's a matter of
moving the methods.

That isn't just a slogan. The largest comparison of web-based and classroom training, a
meta-analysis of 96 research reports covering 19,331 trainees in 168 courses, found that on average
web-based instruction was slightly more effective for teaching facts and about equally effective for
teaching procedures. When the researchers looked only at studies where both formats used the same
instructional methods, the difference all but vanished
([Sitzmann et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1744-6570.2006.00049.x)).

The guides that rank for this topic mostly offer a conversion checklist: chunk the content, add
interactivity, keep videos short. That's not wrong, but it skips the part trainers find hardest,
which is noticing everything the room was doing for them. This guide starts there, then walks
through seven steps to rebuild a workshop as an online course, using one example throughout.

## What the research says about moving training online

Traci Sitzmann and colleagues pooled studies that compared web-based instruction with classroom
instruction on the same content. Their headline results:

- **Knowledge of facts and concepts** (declarative knowledge): web-based instruction was 6% more
  effective on average.
- **Skills and procedures** (procedural knowledge): no meaningful difference (d = -.07).
- **Satisfaction:** trainees were equally satisfied with both.
- **Same methods:** when the two formats used the same instructional methods, the knowledge effect was
  near zero (d = .04). The authors read this as support for the long-standing argument that
  instructional methods, not delivery media, drive learning.

The authors noticed something else in the reports: the online courses "tended to incorporate more
instructional methods" than the classroom ones, and tended to require learners to be more active.
In other words, where online training won, it may have won because it asked learners to do more.

The study has limits that matter for trainers. In 67% of the research reports the trainees were
undergraduates; employees made up only 15% of courses. And the result depended on the research
design. When trainees chose their format, web-based instruction came out ahead (d = .18); when they
were randomly assigned, classroom instruction did better (d = -.26). The authors suggest motivated
learners may choose online courses when they can, and that onsite instructors may add value the
averages hide.

Their most useful finding for a trainer may be the one on blended learning. Web-based instruction
combined with classroom sessions was 13% more effective than classroom instruction alone for
knowledge and 20% more effective for procedures. Yet trainees reacted 6% more favourably to the
classroom on its own. The authors' advice to organisations follows from that:

A second line of research explains what the classroom contributes. Robert Bernard and colleagues
analysed 74 studies of distance education that compared courses with more and less designed
interaction. Courses that built in interaction produced higher achievement, with an overall effect of
0.38 ([Bernard et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654309333844)). In a later summary, the same
group reported the effect by type: +0.49 for interaction between students (10 studies), +0.32 for
interaction with the instructor (44 studies) and +0.46 for interaction with the content (20 studies)
([Abrami et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12528-011-9043-x)).

They also add a warning any trainer who has posted a discussion question will recognise: "just
because opportunities for interaction were offered to students does not mean that students availed
themselves of them." Interaction has to be designed so it's worth doing, not just made available.

**What this means in practice:** the evidence doesn't say online training is better or worse than a
workshop. It says the outcome depends on what learners do. Rebuild the practice, the discussion and
the feedback, and an online course can match the room. Record the talking and drop the rest, and it
probably won't.

## What the room was doing for you

Before you convert anything, list the work you do in a workshop that never appears on a slide. Most
trainers find it's a longer list than they expected.

| What happens in the room | Why it matters | What has to replace it online |
|---|---|---|
| You read faces and slow down when people look lost | Pacing matches the group | Short segments, a check question after each, and learner control over pace |
| People ask questions as they think of them | Misconceptions surface early | Questions anticipated in the content, plus a place to ask and a promised response time |
| Learners practise while you watch | You correct errors before they set in | Practice tasks with immediate, specific feedback built into each answer |
| Pairs and tables discuss a case | Learners explain ideas to each other | A discussion prompt with a concrete task and something to produce |
| You tell stories from your own work | Ideas attach to real situations | Your stories recorded or written as cases, placed where they illustrate a point |
| You notice who's disengaged | You can draw them back in | Progress data, reminders and a reason to come back (a task, a deadline, a cohort) |

This is the inventory the conversion checklists miss. Each row is a teaching method, and each
needs a deliberate online replacement, or a decision to keep it live.

## Turning a workshop into a course: seven steps

To make each step concrete, we'll follow one example: a trainer who runs a one-day workshop on
handling difficult customer conversations for retail team leaders, and wants a self-paced version
for new leaders across several stores. It's an invented example, but it's typical of the training
that moves online.

### 1. Decide whether it should be a course at all

Start with analysis: what should people be able to do after the training, and what stops them now?
If the answer is "they need to practise a hard conversation with someone watching", a fully
self-paced course may not be the right fix. If they mainly need a framework, examples and practice
recognising good and poor responses, most of it can move online. The [ADDIE model](/guides/addie-model/)
is a useful frame for this first pass.

For our trainer, the analysis shows new team leaders know the store policies but freeze when a
customer escalates. That points to a course built around realistic scenarios, not policy
explanation.

### 2. Write the workshop down, minute by minute

Take your run sheet and list every block of the day with its length. Then tag each block by what it
does: **tell** (you explain), **show** (you demonstrate), **practise** (learners do), **discuss**
(learners talk to each other) or **feedback** (you respond to what they did). Add the unwritten
blocks too: the debrief you always run after lunch, the story you tell when someone raises refunds.

Our trainer's day turns out to be about a third telling, a third role-play and a third discussion
and debrief. The slides only cover the first third, which makes the role play and discussion the most important thing to focus on when turning it into a course.

Forums can do some of the work, but we spend quite a lot of time preparing video prompts, lectures that capture the nuance of the discussion and point the learner in the direction of what they should be focused on. It's impossible to capture the collaborative energy of a room full of people in mid discussion, but if you realise your limitations, and build a multi-modal approach to the information into your course, it can work.

### 3. Rewrite the objectives as things learners do

Workshop objectives are often written loosely because the trainer can adjust on the day. A
self-paced course can't, so each objective needs to describe something learners will do that you
could check. "Understand de-escalation" becomes "choose the response that acknowledges the
customer's concern before explaining the policy, in a set of realistic scenarios". Our guide on
[how to write learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/) covers the method, and
[backward design](/guides/backward-design/) explains why the assessment comes next, before any
content.

### 4. Keep each method, change the vehicle

Go through your tagged inventory and choose an online version of each block:

- **Tell** becomes short videos or text, each ending with a question that makes learners use the
  idea. Our guide to [course video length](/guides/how-long-should-course-videos-be/) covers how
  long to make them.
- **Show** becomes a worked example: a recorded demonstration, or a transcript of a good
  conversation with your commentary on why each move works.
- **Practise** becomes scenario questions with feedback on every option, so a wrong answer
  explains what would happen and why. This is [retrieval practice](/library/retrieval-practice/)
  plus [feedback that improves performance](/library/effective-feedback/).
- **Discuss** becomes a structured task (step 5).
- **Feedback** on complex work becomes a submitted task that you or a peer reviews against a short
  checklist.

For our trainer, the morning role-plays become branching scenarios: learners read a customer's
complaint, choose a response, and see how the customer reacts and why. The method, practice with
feedback, survives. Only the vehicle changes.

### 5. Design the interaction on purpose

Bernard and colleagues' results suggest all three kinds of interaction help, but Abrami and
colleagues' warning applies: offering a discussion board isn't the same as getting people to use it.
Give each interaction a reason to exist.

- **With other learners:** instead of "share your thoughts", ask each person to post how they'd open
  a specific difficult conversation, then reply to one other post with a suggested improvement.
- **With you:** schedule feedback on a submitted piece of work, such as a short recorded or written
  response to a scenario, and say when learners will hear back.
- **With the content:** practice questions, worked examples and a short task at the end of every
  segment.

### 6. Decide what stays live

The blended result in Sitzmann and colleagues' meta-analysis is a strong argument for not converting
everything. Keep live time for the blocks where learners need to perform in front of someone, or
where discussion does the teaching.

Our trainer keeps one 60-minute live online session per cohort for role-plays in pairs, after
learners have finished the self-paced scenarios. The explaining and first practice happen online, at
each learner's pace; the live hour is spent entirely on practice and feedback, which is the part of
the workshop that mattered most.

### 7. Pilot one module before converting the rest

Build one complete module, with its practice and feedback, and put it in front of four or five real
learners before you build anything else. Watch where they stall, which feedback they skip and which
questions they get wrong for the wrong reasons. Our guide on
[how to pilot a course before launch](/guides/how-to-pilot-an-online-course/) walks through it.

## Five mistakes trainers make when they go online

1. **Recording the workshop and calling it a course.** A recording keeps the telling and drops the
   practice, discussion and feedback, which is usually where the learning happened.
2. **Keeping lecture-length segments.** In the room you broke up a long explanation with questions
   and activities without thinking about it. Online, build those breaks in.
3. **Testing recall when the job needs judgement.** If the workshop practised decisions, the course
   should too. A scenario question tests more than a definition.
4. **Posting discussion questions with no task.** Learners skip optional discussion. Give them
   something specific to produce and someone to respond to.
5. **Leaving your stories behind.** The examples you tell from experience often carry the
   workshop. Write them down and place them where they illustrate a point.

## What to do next

Take the run sheet for your most-requested workshop and tag every block as tell, show, practise,
discuss or feedback, including the blocks that aren't written down. That one page shows you what
the course has to replace, and which parts deserve to stay live.
