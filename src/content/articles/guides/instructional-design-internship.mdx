An instructional design internship is a short placement, usually one academic term or less, where
you design real learning materials under the guidance of a working designer. Most are tied to a
graduate programme and count for credit; some are summer roles in company learning and development
(L&D) teams. They are hard to find because few are advertised, and the one feature that most
predicts a good experience isn't the employer's name. In a survey of interns at five US colleges,
what supervisors did was significantly associated with how satisfied interns were and how much
they felt they developed ([Hora et al., 2023](https://ccwt.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Designing-Effective-Internships_Research-Report_Jan-2023-1.pdf)).
That study covered interns in every field, not instructional design alone, but its lesson
transfers: choose the supervisor and the project, not the logo.

Search for instructional design internships and you'll mostly find job boards, a university
vacancy or two and a Facebook group of students asking where the placements are. None of them
explain what interns actually do, how to tell a useful placement from free labour, or when an
unpaid one is even legal. This guide covers all three, using the two published accounts of
instructional design internship programmes we could find, the wider research on internships, and
the labour rules in the US and Australia.

## What instructional design interns actually do

Two universities have written up their instructional design internship programmes in peer-reviewed
journals. Both are small and both are described by the people who run them, so read them as
worked examples, not evidence of outcomes. They agree on the shape of a good placement.

**Bath Spa University's virtual internship programme** in the UK took graduate students from
learning design programmes, mostly in the US, for 8 to 12 weeks, capped at 90 hours for up to three
graduate credits ([Ruggiero & Boehm, 2016](https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v17i4.2385)). Each
intern was paired with a learning technologist as mentor and an academic as client, and redesigned
an existing face-to-face module for online or blended delivery. The deliverables were a storyboard,
the built module, usability tests with real users and a five-minute walkthrough video.

**The University of Pittsburgh's Instructional Design Internship Program**, run with Indiana
University of Pennsylvania, hosts graduate students in a virtual placement that spans 15 weeks,
including the steps before and after ([JAID, 2025](https://edtechbooks.org/jaid_14_4/agiksdcmvn)).
Interns work on projects that grow in complexity over the semester: needs assessments, designing
and building materials, multimedia assets such as an interactive quiz or an infographic, and course
outlines, with weekly one-on-one check-ins with their supervisor. The programme typically caps intake at
two interns a semester.

Put those together and a good instructional design internship looks like this:

| Element | What the documented programmes did |
|---|---|
| A real project | Redesign a module, or build materials for a live course, for a real client |
| A defined output | A storyboard, a built module or lesson, test results and a short walkthrough |
| A named mentor | One person who meets you regularly, often weekly |
| A client or subject expert | Someone whose content you're designing, so you practise working with experts |
| A fixed length | One term or less, scoped so the project can finish |

That last row matters more than it looks. A finished module you can show beats three half-built
ones, and it gives you the portfolio piece most entry-level ads are really asking for (more on
that in our guide to [entry-level instructional design jobs](/guides/entry-level-instructional-design-jobs/)).

## Does an internship help you get hired?

The direct evidence for instructional design is thin. The best general evidence comes from a
résumé experiment: Nunley and colleagues sent about 9,400 fictitious applications to entry-level
business jobs in seven US cities in 2013, randomly giving a quarter of the applicants a three-month
internship ([Nunley et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.labeco.2015.11.002)).

<ResearchCard n={1} />

In plain numbers, about 18.4 per cent of applications with an internship got an interview request,
against 16.1 per cent without. The authors think the internship mostly works as a signal to
employers rather than proof of skill, and they note the outcome was an interview request, not a
job. The jobs were in banking, finance, insurance, management, marketing and sales, not learning
design.

The Pittsburgh programme reports that 75 per cent of its graduated interns have found positions in
instructional design or adjacent fields, but it doesn't say how many interns that is (it had hosted
five by the time of writing) and there's no comparison group. Treat it as encouraging, not as an
effect size.

What we can say with more confidence is that instructional design employers want to see design
work. In [job-ad studies](/guides/entry-level-instructional-design-jobs/), almost every posting mentions experience, and hiring leaders expect it to
be shown during hiring. An internship is one structured way to get that work. It isn't the only
one.

## Where to find an instructional design internship

Because few placements are advertised, treat the search as a set of channels, roughly in order of
how often they lead somewhere.

### 1. Your own programme

If you're studying instructional design, learning design or educational technology, start with
your programme's internship or practicum coordinator. Both documented programmes ran through degree
programmes, with credit attached, and that's typical: the host gets a supervised student, and you
get a structure that protects your learning. If your course has no internship option, ask whether
an independent project for an outside client can count.

### 2. University teaching and learning centres

Universities have teams that build online courses: centres for teaching and learning, digital or
online learning units, and faculty-level online learning offices like the one in the Pittsburgh
example. They often have more projects than staff, and they understand supervision. In Australian
and New Zealand universities, look for teams that employ educational designers or learning
designers.

### 3. Company L&D teams

Corporate placements exist, often as summer roles, but they're advertised under varied titles.
Search for "instructional design intern", "learning experience design intern", "eLearning intern",
"learning and development intern", "training intern" and "curriculum intern". Read the duties, not
the title: some roles are mostly scheduling and admin.

### 4. Not-for-profits and community organisations

Charities, health services and community groups often need volunteer training or onboarding
modules and rarely have a designer. A scoped project here can give you the same evidence as a
formal internship. The pay rules differ for these organisations (see below).

### 5. Propose your own

If nothing is advertised, write a one-page proposal. Name the problem (an induction that's all
slides, a workshop that needs an online version), the output (a storyboard and one built module),
the time you'll give, and the support you'd need: one person to meet you weekly. A modest, specific
offer is easier to say yes to than a request for "experience".

## How to judge an internship offer

The internship research gives us a short list of what to look for. Hora and colleagues surveyed
students at five US colleges and universities who had done internships in any field
([Hora et al., 2023](https://ccwt.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/Designing-Effective-Internships_Research-Report_Jan-2023-1.pdf)).
Supervisor support stood out.

<ResearchCard n={0} />

The same report notes that an intern's tasks aren't guaranteed to relate to their studies, and
that students in internships unrelated to their majors have more negative experiences. The Bath
Spa team reached a similar conclusion from the other side of the table: clear, explicit
communication between clients, mentors and interns led to completed projects, and they found the
first connection between mentor and intern crucial ([Ruggiero & Boehm, 2016](https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v17i4.2385)).
They also added a client interview at the start, because clients didn't always give interns a clear remit.

In practice, we'd ask these questions before accepting:

| Ask | A good answer | A warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Who will supervise me? | A named designer or L&D lead | "The team" or no one in particular |
| How often will we meet? | Weekly, at a set time | "Whenever you need us" |
| What will I have built at the end? | A specific module, storyboard or course | General support for whatever comes up |
| Who is the client or subject expert? | A named person with time set aside | Unclear, or "you'll pick it up" |
| How does it fit my studies? | Linked to credit or a learning plan | Unrelated tasks, mainly admin |
| Can I show the work afterwards? | Yes, or a version with client details removed | No, with no alternative offered |

The last question is easy to forget. Ask early whether you can include the work in a portfolio,
and if the content is confidential, agree on a redacted version or a case study that describes
your process. Our guide to [instructional design portfolio examples](/guides/instructional-design-portfolio-examples/)
shows what a strong case study includes.

## Paid or unpaid: what the rules say

Unpaid internships are common: in Hora's College Internship Study, across five US colleges and universities, 34 per cent of student
interns worked without pay ([Hora, 2020](https://wcer.wisc.edu/news/detail/5-obstacles-that-stop-many-students-from-taking-an-internship)).
They are also the main reason many students miss out. Of the non-interns in that study, 64 per
cent had hoped to take an internship but couldn't, and 60 per cent of those cited the need to keep
working a paid job. If you can't afford an unpaid placement, you're not unusual, and a smaller,
part-time project may be the better plan.

The law limits unpaid internships too. This is general information, not legal advice; check with
your local regulator.

- **United States.** Courts use a "primary beneficiary test" to decide whether an intern at a
  for-profit employer is really an employee entitled to minimum wage. The seven factors include
  whether the internship is tied to a formal education programme by coursework or academic credit,
  whether it fits the academic calendar, and whether the intern's work complements rather than
  displaces paid staff. Unpaid internships at public-sector and non-profit charitable organisations,
  where the intern volunteers without expecting pay, are generally permissible
  ([US Department of Labor, 2018](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/71-flsa-internships)).
- **Australia.** The Fair Work Ombudsman notes that education and training institutions run
  internship programmes to help students meet course requirements, often called student or
  vocational placements. Otherwise, an intern who does activities that look like work for a
  business may be an employee who must be paid, even if both sides agreed there'd be no pay. Its indicators include how long the arrangement lasts, how
  important the work is to the business, whether the person mainly observes and learns, and who
  gets the main benefit ([Fair Work Ombudsman](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/starting-employment/unpaid-work/work-experience-and-internships)).

The practical rule in both countries is the same: the more an unpaid placement looks like a junior
job, building courses the business sells or relies on, with no structured learning, the more
likely it should be paid. A placement linked to your course, with a mentor and a learning plan, is
on firmer ground.

<Callout>
  Before you start, write a one-page learning plan with your supervisor: the project, the output,
  the meeting rhythm and two or three skills you want to practise (a needs analysis, a storyboard,
  a usability test). It protects your learning, makes the placement easier to defend if pay is
  questioned, and gives you a checklist for the final review.
</Callout>

## Making the internship count

An internship is worth what you can show and say about it afterwards.

1. **Keep a design journal.** Both documented programmes asked interns to reflect as they went.
   A few lines a week on decisions and why you made them become the backbone of a portfolio case
   study.
2. **Test with real learners.** The Bath Spa interns ran usability tests on their modules. Even
   two testers give you evidence most beginner portfolios lack. Our guide to
   [piloting a course](/guides/how-to-pilot-an-online-course/) shows how to run a small test.
3. **Finish something.** Scope down rather than leave work half built. One complete module with a
   storyboard, a quiz and test notes is a stronger story than a large project you didn't finish.
4. **Ask for a specific reference.** At the end, ask your supervisor to describe one thing you
   designed and how you handled feedback. That's what a hiring manager wants to hear.

If you're coming from teaching, much of this will feel familiar; our guide to
[instructional design jobs for former teachers](/guides/instructional-design-jobs-for-former-teachers/)
covers which of your skills transfer.

## What to do next

Write a one-page internship proposal this week: the problem you'd solve, the module or storyboard
you'd deliver, the hours you can give and the one thing you need, a supervisor who will meet you
weekly. Send it to your programme coordinator or one university teaching and learning centre, and
use the questions above to judge any reply.
