An instructional design internship is a short, supervised placement where you design or build
real learning materials for an organisation: a university's online learning team, a company's
training department, a not-for-profit. Many are graduate practicums done for course credit; others
are paid jobs with "intern" in the title. If you have a choice, the evidence points one way. In US
graduate surveys run by the National Association of Colleges and Employers (NACE), paid interns
consistently receive more job offers than unpaid ones, and in the 2024 survey unpaid interns
averaged fewer offers than students who had no internship at all. Those surveys cover graduates of
every major, not designers alone, so read them as a guide to how employers treat internships in
general.

Search for "instructional design internship" and you'll find job boards and a few forum threads:
lists of openings with no advice on which ones are worth taking. This guide covers the three kinds
of internship you'll come across, what the pay data says, when unpaid placements are legal, what
interns in the published accounts actually did, and a six-step plan for finding one.

## Three kinds of instructional design internship

The word "internship" covers arrangements that work very differently. This is our reading of how
they divide; check the details of any you're offered.

| Kind | Who it's for | What it usually looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Graduate practicum or internship for credit | Students in an instructional design master's or certificate | A set number of hours with an approved host, a faculty supervisor and a portfolio or report at the end. Often unpaid or with a small stipend |
| Paid internship | Students and recent graduates | A fixed-term role, often over a summer, in a company's learning team or a university's online learning unit, advertised like a job |
| Self-arranged placement | Career changers, teachers moving across, anyone without a programme | A defined project for a not-for-profit, a community group or your own employer's training team, set up with a brief and a reviewer |

The University of Massachusetts Boston's handbook shows what a credit-bearing internship involves
([UMass Boston](https://sites.google.com/umb.edu/idgradhandbook/internships)). Students can
enrol only after eight courses, must complete at least 120 to 130 hours in their placement, be
available eight to ten hours a week, and hand in a reflective journal, an hours log, work samples
with explanations and an evaluation from their field supervisor. The handbook describes the
position as "primarily a volunteer position", with at most a nominal stipend. That pattern of set
hours, a supervisor on each side and assessed reflection is common in graduate programmes, but
the numbers vary, so read your own programme's rules.

## Paid or unpaid: what the job-offer data shows

NACE surveys US graduating students every year. Its most detailed breakdown by pay comes from the
Class of 2015 survey, which focused on 9,184 bachelor's students
([NACE, 2016](https://www.naceweb.org/job-market/internships/paid-interns-co-ops-see-greater-offer-rates-and-salary-offers-than-their-unpaid-classmates/)).
Among students who applied for jobs, 72.2 per cent of those with a paid internship at a for-profit
company received an offer. For unpaid interns at for-profit companies it was 43.9 per cent, and for
students with no internship, 36.5 per cent. The gap between paid and unpaid held for every type of
employer.

<Chart id="nace-2016-intern-offer-rates" />

Starting pay followed the same pattern. The median offer for paid interns at for-profit companies
was 53,521 USD, against 34,375 USD for unpaid interns there and 38,572 USD for students with no
internship.

The newer data agrees on the direction. The 2024 survey drew 2,281 graduating seniors at bachelor's
level from 253 colleges and universities
([NACE, 2024](https://naceweb.org/docs/default-source/default-document-library/2024/publication/executive-summary/2024-nace-student-survey-executive-summary-four-year.pdf)).

<ResearchCard n={0} />

Paid interns averaged 1.01 job offers, unpaid interns 0.66 and students with no internship 0.74.
Average starting salaries were 68,041 USD, 53,125 USD and 55,924 USD.

Read these numbers carefully. They're self-reported surveys, they compare different groups of
students rather than testing internships, and they cover every field. NACE itself says the most
likely reason unpaid interns did worse than non-interns in 2024 is their majors: about a third of
the unpaid interns studied public service, one of the lowest-paid majors. So the data doesn't prove
that working for free hurts you. What it does show is that, across employers, paid internships
are the ones most often followed by a job.

In practice, we'd treat pay as a signal. An employer who pays an intern usually has real work
waiting, a budget for the role and a reason to keep you. An unpaid placement can still be worth
doing, especially one your programme sets up and supervises, but it has to earn its place with a
real project and a real mentor.

## When is an unpaid internship legal?

The rules depend on where you are and who you work for. This is general information, not legal
advice: check your local regulator before you agree to work unpaid.

**In the US**, the Department of Labor says courts use a "primary beneficiary test" to decide
whether an intern at a for-profit employer is really an employee who must be paid
([US Department of Labor, 2018](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/71-flsa-internships)).
The seven factors include whether the internship is tied to your formal education through
coursework or credit, fits around the academic calendar, lasts only as long as it's teaching you
something, and complements the work of paid staff rather than replacing it. Unpaid internships with
public-sector and non-profit charitable organisations, where the intern volunteers without
expecting pay, are generally permissible.

**In Australia**, the Fair Work Ombudsman says unpaid work experience, job placements and
internships that are not vocational placements are unlawful if the person is in an employment
relationship with the business
([Fair Work Ombudsman](https://www.fairwork.gov.au/pay/unpaid-work/work-experience-and-internships)).
A placement your course requires is the main exception. Otherwise, the indicators include how long
the arrangement runs, whether the work is something paid staff would normally do, and who gets the
main benefit. The Ombudsman's position is that the person doing the work should get the main
benefit.

The practical test in both countries comes down to one question: if the organisation would
otherwise pay someone to build this course, you should probably be paid too.

## What instructional design interns actually do

Published research on design internships is small and mostly descriptive, but it is consistent
about what helps.

The largest study we found followed 44 graduate instructional design students through internship
seminars at California State University, Los Angeles, from 2011 to 2012
([Chen, 2012](https://doi.org/10.18785/jetde.0501.02)). Each completed 60 hours with a host, kept
a weekly journal and wrote up a case study. Thirty-six (82 per cent) said the internship met or
exceeded their expectations, and they reported learning project and time management, planning,
research and building real products; one student wrote about learning to build an online course in Moodle.
The study also found the limits of learning by doing.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

It found that moving from the edge of a team to doing central work was a particular challenge for
interns, and that they needed a balance between doing the work and reflecting on it. This is one
programme at one university, with self-reported outcomes, so treat it as a description rather than
proof. But it matches what the research on [scaffolding](/library/scaffolding-in-education/) says
about novices in any field: they learn faster when someone shows them how and then steps back.

Two smaller accounts show what the work looks like up close. At Bloomsburg University's library, two
instructional technology master's students each worked about 20 hours a week towards a 240-hour
requirement, building video tutorials through task analysis, storyboards, drafts and revisions
([Yelinek & Coffta, 2013](https://crln.acrl.org/index.php/crlnews/article/view/9038/9864)). The
tutorials went onto the library's website. The interns said the placement built independent work
and time management, and they were frustrated by how much ran through email.

The second is a design case written by an early-career designer about a tutorial built for a
university department during an online graduate practicum
([Lunga & Howard, 2022](https://doi.org/10.14434/ijdl.v13i2.32356)). Looking back, she wrote that
she "neglected essential analysis" and jumped to a solution too quickly, and that she talked more
often with her instructor and classmates than with her client. Both mistakes are easy to make in a
placement where the client is busy and the instructor is the person grading you.

## How to find an instructional design internship: a six-step plan

To keep this concrete, we'll follow one invented but typical example: a primary school teacher
halfway through a part-time graduate certificate in instructional design, who wants to move into
corporate learning.

### 1. Decide which kind you need

If your programme offers a credit-bearing internship, start there: it comes with a supervisor, it
counts towards your qualification, and in Australia a required placement is the clearest case of
lawful unpaid work. If you're not enrolled anywhere, you need a paid internship or a self-arranged
placement.

Our teacher's certificate includes an optional practicum. She takes it, because it gives her a
faculty supervisor and a reason for an employer to say yes.

### 2. Search beyond the word "intern"

Job boards list a few roles titled instructional design intern, learning experience design intern
or eLearning intern. Many more placements are never advertised. Look at university centres for
teaching and learning, online learning units, hospital and health-service education teams,
professional associations that run training, and not-for-profits with volunteers to train. Our
guide to [instructional design jobs](/guides/instructional-design-jobs/) covers where design work
sits in each sector.

Our teacher writes to the learning team at a regional insurance company and to a not-for-profit
that trains volunteer tutors. The not-for-profit replies first.

### 3. Check the pay and the legal position before you start

Ask whether the role is paid, and if not, why. A placement your course requires, or volunteering
for a charity, is a different arrangement from an unpaid role at a business that would otherwise
hire a contractor. If you're unsure, read your regulator's guidance, as above.

The not-for-profit can't pay, but the placement is part of her certificate and the organisation is
a charity, so she's comfortable it's set up properly.

### 4. Propose one defined project

Hosts say yes more readily to a clear offer than to an open-ended "can I help?". Write a one-page
proposal: the learners, the problem, what you'll make, how long it will take and who will review it.
One finished project beats three half-done ones.

Our teacher proposes a short online induction module for new volunteer tutors, replacing a
45-minute talk that new volunteers forget. She'll write [learning
objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/) first, then build two lessons and a short
check for understanding.

<Callout>
  Agree three things in writing before day one: the project and what "done" looks like, who your
  mentor is, and when you'll meet. Chen's interns struggled without modelling and coaching, and the
  published accounts all lost time to slow or patchy communication.
</Callout>

### 5. Ask for a mentor and a weekly check-in

The research is clear that a placement teaches you most when someone experienced shows you how
the work is done and gives you [feedback you can act on](/library/effective-feedback/). If no one
at the host does design work, ask your programme or a designer you know to review your drafts.
Make the check-in weekly and by video or in person, not just email, especially if you're remote.

Our teacher's host has no designer, so her faculty supervisor reviews the storyboard, and the
volunteer coordinator acts as the subject expert who checks the content is right.

### 6. Document the whole process for your portfolio

Keep the brief, the objectives, the storyboard, drafts, the feedback you got, the finished module
and what happened when people used it. Hiring managers want to see your thinking, not only the
screens. Our guide to [instructional design portfolio
examples](/guides/instructional-design-portfolio-examples/) shows how to present a project like
this.

Our teacher pilots the module with the next intake of volunteers, notes where they stall, and
revises one lesson. That revision, with the reasons for it, becomes the centre of her portfolio
piece and her first interview answers. If you're also moving from teaching, our guide to
[instructional design jobs for former
teachers](/guides/instructional-design-jobs-for-former-teachers/) covers how classroom skills
carry across.

## Four signs an internship isn't worth it

1. **No defined project.** "Help the team" usually means admin. Ask what you'll have made by the end.
2. **No one to learn from.** Without modelling and feedback, you're working alone for free.
3. **Unpaid work a business would otherwise pay for.** That's the case regulators in the US and
   Australia look at most closely.
4. **Nothing you can show.** If the host won't let you describe or show the work afterwards, even
   with names removed, it won't help your portfolio.

## What to do next

Write a one-page proposal for one project you could finish in 60 to 120 hours, with the learners,
the problem, the output and a named reviewer. Then send it to two organisations this week: one from
your programme's list or your current workplace, and one you'd like to work for.
