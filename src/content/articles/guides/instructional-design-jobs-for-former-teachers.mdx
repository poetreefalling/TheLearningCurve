Instructional design jobs suit former teachers better than the job titles suggest, but not for the
reason most career-change advice gives. Your teaching experience matters less as *teaching* and more
as evidence that you can analyse learners, write objectives, design assessment and sequence content.
The gaps that stop teachers getting hired are rarely about learning. They're about working through
other people's expertise, running projects, and building in the tools employers name.

Search for this topic and you'll mostly find job boards, forum threads and course sales pages. The
job boards list vacancies without saying what they want. The threads are honest but anecdotal. The
sales pages promise a smooth path into a well-paid role. What's missing is what employers actually
put in their ads, which teaching skills map onto them, and a plan that closes the real gaps. That's
what this guide covers.

## What instructional design job ads ask for

The best evidence on this comes from a study that read the ads themselves. Wang, Chen, Ritzhaupt and
Martin collected instructional design job announcements from Glassdoor, Indeed and Monster between
17 September and 16 October 2019, removed duplicates and incomplete listings, and coded the 1,030
that remained for 185 kinds of knowledge, skill and ability
([Wang et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijtd.12209)).

Two findings matter most for a teacher thinking about the switch. The first is about qualifications.

The second is about what employers want you to be able to do. The single most frequent skill was
collaboration, and the most frequent group of abilities was working with stakeholders.

Other abilities that came up often include developing course materials (81 per cent of ads),
advising and consulting with subject matter experts, usually called SMEs (57 per cent), needs
analysis (41 per cent) and evaluating learning products and programmes (41 per cent). On the
knowledge side, instructional design models and principles (the study's examples are ADDIE and
Dick and Carey) were named in 59 per cent of ads, and educational authoring software such as
Articulate or Lectora in 33 per cent.

What the ads rarely ask for is classroom teaching. Teaching face-to-face appeared in 10 per cent of
announcements and teaching in virtual learning environments in 4 per cent. Delivering training to
learners appeared in 30 per cent.

Two limits are worth keeping in mind. The data is from US ads posted over one month in 2019, before
generative AI tools arrived in most design teams, and LinkedIn listings weren't included. The
ratios are a fair guide to what employers value; the tool names will have moved on.

## Which teaching skills transfer, and how to name them

The good news in those ads is how much of the core work teachers already do. The problem is
vocabulary: a hiring manager in a corporate learning team may not recognise "unit planning" as
course design. Translate your experience into the employer's terms and back each claim with
something you can show.

| What you did as a teacher | What an instructional designer calls it | What to show |
|---|---|---|
| Planned units and sequences of lessons | Course mapping and sequencing | A course map for one unit, rebuilt for adult learners |
| Wrote learning intentions and success criteria | Measurable learning objectives | Five objectives, each with the task that tests it |
| Designed tests, tasks and rubrics | Assessment design | One assessment and its rubric, with a sample answer |
| Differentiated for a mixed class | Learner analysis | A one-page learner brief |
| Gave written and verbal feedback | Feedback design | Quiz feedback that explains each wrong answer |
| Changed a lesson when it wasn't working | Formative evaluation and iteration | A short revision log from a pilot |

Every row in that table corresponds to an ability the job ads ask for: needs analysis, developing
course materials, developing assessments and evaluating learning programmes. If the language is new,
the [ADDIE model](/guides/addie-model/) is the framework most employers will expect you to know,
and each phase maps onto work you've already done.

## Where teachers find the gaps

The research on people who've made the move is small and qualitative, but it points in the same
direction as the job ads.

Xia and Brown interviewed seven graduates of an instructional design programme in their first
design roles; four had been teachers. Four themes emerged: learning new tools and practices on the
job, communication barriers, mismatches between what participants hoped the work would be and what
employers expected, and different norms from one profession to the next
([Xia & Brown, 2025](https://edtechbooks.org/jaid_14_2/cdipdhnptm)). The communication
problems included unclear client needs and feedback loops that closed too early. Participants also stressed
the value of having "a rationale behind design" so they could give "theory-supported, evidence-based
reasons" for their choices.

<ResearchCard n={2} />

A 2026 master's thesis from the University of Victoria interviewed eight former K–12 teachers in
Canada and found that the move was as much a change of professional identity as of job. Participants
drew on their teaching expertise while building new skills in technology, collaboration and project
management ([Adante, 2026](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/items/5dfffa11-1213-4065-83d3-816dfb039bd2)).
Both studies are small, so treat them as a description of common experiences rather than a measure
of how often they happen.

Putting the job ads and the interviews together, the gaps most teachers need to close are these:

1. **Working through someone else's expertise.** In a classroom you're the content expert and the
   person who delivers. In most design roles you're neither: an SME owns the content and someone
   else runs the course. Your job is to ask the questions that turn their knowledge into something
   learners can practise.
2. **Projects with deadlines, budgets and sign-off.** Project management appeared in 30 per cent of
   ads and managing multiple projects in 26 per cent. A school term has a rhythm; a design project
   has stakeholders who change their minds.
3. **Authoring and media tools.** Authoring software, video and presentation tools each appeared in
   roughly a third of ads. You don't need all of them. You need one well enough to build a sample.
4. **Explaining your decisions.** Teachers make good design decisions by instinct. Designers are
   asked to defend them to people who don't share that instinct, which means naming the principle
   behind each choice.

## How to make the switch: a six-step plan

To keep this concrete, follow one invented but typical example: a secondary science teacher with
eight years' experience who wants to move into a corporate learning team.

### 1. Choose a sector before you build anything

Instructional design work sits in very different places: corporate learning and development, higher
education, schools and edtech companies, government and not-for-profits. Each values different
things. Higher education and edtech may count your teaching more; corporate teams care more about
business outcomes and turnaround times.

Pick one sector for your first applications and read twenty of its job ads. List the duties and
tools that recur. For our science teacher, corporate ads in her city keep naming compliance
training, SME interviews and one particular authoring tool, so that's what she builds towards.

### 2. Rewrite your CV in the employer's language

Turn each teaching duty into a design outcome with evidence. "Taught Year 10 chemistry" says little.
"Redesigned a ten-week chemistry unit around weekly low-stakes quizzes and a practical assessment
with a published rubric" says you can sequence content, design practice and write assessment. Use
the table above as your translation guide, and cut anything that's only about classroom management.

### 3. Build one portfolio piece that shows your whole process

Hiring managers see plenty of polished sample screens. What they see less often is a candidate who
can show the thinking: the learner brief, the objectives, the assessment, one built module, what
happened when real people tried it, and what changed as a result. A portfolio piece with all six
parts does more than three that only show the finished product.

Pick a topic outside school, ideally one relevant to your chosen sector. Our science teacher builds
a short module on laboratory chemical safety for new staff at a research facility: close to her
expertise, but aimed at adults at work. Our [online course design checklist](/guides/online-course-design-checklist/)
covers what to check at each stage.

<Callout>
  Write a one-paragraph design rationale for every major choice in your portfolio piece: why this
  sequence, why this assessment, why this much practice. It's the part that answers the question
  employers ask most often in interviews, and the part that turns a teacher's instinct into a
  designer's argument.
</Callout>

### 4. Learn one authoring tool well enough to build your sample

Choose the tool that recurs in the ads you read in step 1 and learn it by building your portfolio
piece in it, rather than by working through a course on every feature. Depth in one tool, shown in
real work, reads better than a list of six tools you've opened once.

### 5. Get real experience working with an SME

This is the gap a portfolio alone can't close. Find a small project where someone else owns the
content: induction training for a local not-for-profit, a short course for a professional
association, or a staff training module at your current school built for adults rather than
students. Run it like a design project, with a kick-off meeting, a written brief, a review round and
sign-off. Then you can describe the experience truthfully in an interview.

### 6. Apply where your background counts most

For a first role, look where teaching experience is a plus rather than a translation exercise:
education-focused companies, university learning design teams, and training roles in sectors close
to your subject. In Australia and New Zealand, universities often call the role an educational or
learning designer, and the career path into it is known to be varied
([Nichols & Meuleman, 2017](https://doi.org/10.61468/jofdl.v21i2.311)). A second move into a
corporate team is easier once you have a design job on your CV.

## What the pay data says, and doesn't

There is no reliable public pay figure for "instructional designer" as such. The US Bureau of Labor
Statistics doesn't treat it as a separate occupation. Its two closest categories are:

| BLS occupation | Median annual pay (May 2025) | Typical entry-level education | Projected growth 2025–35 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Training and development specialists | 69,280 USD | Bachelor's degree | 11% |
| Instructional coordinators | 77,440 USD | Master's degree | 2% |

Training and development specialists design and run training in organisations; the BLS notes that
some positions ask for experience in areas such as instructional design, and lists education among
the common degree fields ([BLS, training and development
specialists](https://www.bls.gov/OOH/business-and-financial/training-and-development-specialists.htm)).
Instructional coordinators work mostly in schools and education systems, and the BLS says most need
several years of related work experience as a teacher or an instructional leader ([BLS,
instructional coordinators](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/education-training-and-library/instructional-coordinators.htm)).

These are medians across broad categories, so treat them as a rough guide rather than a salary
offer. If you're outside the US, check your own country's labour statistics and current job ads.

## Four mistakes that slow the switch

1. **Leading with years of teaching.** Employers read "twelve years in the classroom" as delivery
   experience. Lead with what you designed.
2. **Building a portfolio of school lessons.** A Year 7 lesson reminds hiring managers of the gap.
   Build for adults at work, even if the topic is close to your subject.
3. **Collecting tools instead of finishing work.** One finished, piloted module beats certificates
   in five tools.
4. **Treating the SME as an obstacle.** In design roles, getting good material out of busy experts
   is the job. Show that you can do it.

## What to do next

Open twenty job ads in the sector you want, copy every duty and tool into a list, and mark each one
you could already show evidence for. The unmarked items are your plan. For more on the learning
principles behind the work you'll be asked to explain, browse [our research library](/library/).
