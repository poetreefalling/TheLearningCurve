Search for instructional design jobs and you'll get job boards: long lists of postings, few of which
explain where the work actually is or what it takes to be hired. The research on job ads gives a clearer
picture, and the first surprise is the sector. In a study of 130 full-time US instructional design job
ads collected in May 2023, 60 per cent were corporate and 2.3 per cent were in K-12 schools
([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606)). It's a US sample from one job site, but a
larger study found the same tilt.

This guide covers where the roles are, how they differ by sector, what job ads ask for, and how to build
the portfolio that gets you through the door.

## Where the instructional design jobs are

<Chart id="weber-2024-id-jobs-by-sector" />

Weber, McCarthy, Campbell and Bauer collected 130 full-time US instructional designer and
instructional technologist postings from Indeed in May 2023, sorted them by sector, and interviewed 12
leaders who hire designers, three each from schools, universities, corporate and non-profit settings. Corporate roles,
which in their study include healthcare employers, made up 60 per cent. Higher education was next at 23.8
per cent, then military, government and research at 10.8 per cent, non-profits at 3.1 per cent and schools
at 2.3 per cent ([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606)).

An earlier and larger study points the same way. Wang, Chen, Ritzhaupt and Martin analysed 1,030 US job
announcements from 2019 and coded each by industry. The biggest group, 37.4 per cent, was professional,
scientific and technical services, which includes consultancies, agencies and training providers that
build learning for other organisations. Educational services followed at 19.0 per cent, then health care
(10.0 per cent), information (8.5 per cent) and finance and insurance (8.2 per cent)
([Wang et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijtd.12209)).

The practical point: if you're aiming for a school, there are few instructional design roles there. If
you're coming from teaching, the bigger markets are workplaces and universities, and our guide to
[instructional design jobs for former teachers](/guides/instructional-design-jobs-for-former-teachers/)
covers how to make that move.

### Corporate and workplace learning

This is the largest market: learning and development teams inside companies, plus the agencies and
consultancies they hire. The work is usually short, practical courses tied to performance: onboarding,
compliance, product knowledge, systems training, leadership programmes. Deadlines are tight and the
subject expert is often a busy manager rather than an academic.

In Weber's interviews with hiring leaders, one corporate leader described her organisation moving away
from formal degree requirements towards experience and skills, and working in specialised teams with a
senior learning architect keeping a programme consistent across courses
([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606)). In Wang's larger sample, health care and
finance and insurance each accounted for about one in ten ads, which makes regulated industries a market
worth targeting on their own.

### Higher education

Universities hire instructional designers (often called learning designers or educational designers in
Australia and New Zealand) to help academics build online and blended courses. The job is as much
consulting as building: you work alongside faculty who own the content and may not have asked for help.

Degree expectations are higher here. A higher education leader in Weber's study said his hiring usually
required a master's in instructional design or a related field, partly because of institutional
bureaucracy, and another stressed experience with learning management systems and working with faculty
([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606)). Our
[instructional designer salary guide](/guides/instructional-designer-salary/) explains why universities
tend to pay less than corporate roles.

### Government, military, non-profit and schools

Government, military and research roles made up about one in ten ads in Weber's sample; read the
eligibility section of each ad first, as public-sector roles can carry requirements private employers
don't. Non-profits and schools together made up about one in twenty. In schools the design work often
sits under other titles: the school leaders Weber interviewed had titles such as technology integration
specialist and instructional technology coordinator.

### Contract and freelance work

Neither study counted contract roles separately, but contract work is a real route into the field:
agencies and consultancies take on course builds for clients and bring in designers per project, and
staffing firms place contractors with corporate teams. Contract roles tend to want someone who can start
producing quickly, which makes a strong portfolio even more important. If you're weighing freelance work,
treat it as a business: you'll need your own tools, contracts and a way of finding clients.

## What instructional design job ads actually ask for

Across sectors, the pattern in the ads is consistent. In Weber's 130 postings:

- **Degree:** 73.8 per cent set a bachelor's as the minimum, and 57.7 per cent accepted a degree in any
  field. Only 12.3 per cent required a master's, though 43.1 per cent preferred one.
- **Skills:** written communication was named in 71.5 per cent of ads and oral communication in 66.2 per
  cent, ahead of collaboration (45.4 per cent), organisation, problem-solving and project management.
- **Technology:** the most named was a learning management system (29.2 per cent), then video (20.8 per
  cent), general learning technologies, Microsoft Office, Articulate (16.2 per cent) and Adobe Creative
  Suite (15.4 per cent).

([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606))

Wang's larger 2019 sample found a similar degree pattern: 62.5 per cent of ads set a bachelor's as the
minimum and 7.4 per cent a master's, with 27.3 per cent not stating a degree at all
([Wang et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijtd.12209)).

Two things stand out. Tools matter less than people assume: no single tool appeared in even a third of
ads, while communication appeared in most. And the field of your degree matters less than the evidence
that you can do the work. Our guide to
[entry-level instructional design jobs](/guides/entry-level-instructional-design-jobs/) goes deeper into
experience requirements and the titles that work as first roles.

## Job titles to search

Employers name these roles to suit their own culture, as Weber and colleagues note, so a search for
"instructional designer" alone misses a lot. Search several titles and read the duties rather than the
label:

- **Instructional designer**, the most common title across sectors
- **Learning designer** or **educational designer**, common in Australian and New Zealand universities
- **Learning experience designer**, common in corporate and agency roles
- **eLearning developer**, usually more building than design
- **Instructional technologist** or **learning technologist**, often more platform and tools
- **Curriculum developer** or **training developer**, common in workplace and government roles

## How to build a portfolio that gets you hired

The job-ad studies don't count portfolio requests, but the hiring leaders Weber interviewed were clear
that experience has to be shown during hiring, not just listed.

<ResearchCard n={0} />

A portfolio is how you show it. Here's what we'd put in one.

### 1. Two or three projects, not ten

Pick projects that match the sector you're applying to: a short compliance or onboarding module for
corporate roles, a redesigned unit of an online course for university roles. Depth beats volume.

### 2. Show the process, not just the product

For each project, show the problem, the learners, your objectives, how you assessed them, one or two
screens or storyboard pages, and what happened. Hiring managers want to see your decisions. Our guides
to [writing learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/) and
[storyboarding an eLearning module](/guides/elearning-storyboard/) cover the pieces worth showing.

### 3. Work with someone else's content

At least one project should involve a subject expert who isn't you. Working with experts is much of the
job in every sector, and a project where you had to draw content out of someone else is more convincing
than one where you were the expert.

### 4. Include a writing sample

Communication tops the job ads, so make it visible: a short script, a scenario or a set of quiz questions
with feedback. Our guide to [writing multiple-choice questions](/guides/writing-multiple-choice-questions/)
shows what good assessment writing looks like.

### 5. Build it with the tools the ads name

Use one LMS or authoring tool well enough to build your sample, rather than collecting certificates in
several. Since LMS experience is the most-named technology, a project that shows a course running in a
real platform carries weight.

## Is the field growing?

There's no official US count of instructional designers. The closest government category, training and
development specialists, had 472,500 jobs in 2025, is projected to grow 11 per cent between 2025 and
2035, and is expected to have about 46,000 openings a year. The category is broader than instructional
design, and its biggest employers include healthcare, professional services, education and finance
([US Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/Business-and-financial/Training-and-development-specialists.htm)).
Treat it as a sign of the wider field's health, not a count of design roles.
