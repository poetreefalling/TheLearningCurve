Instructional design models are maps of how to plan and build instruction. The ones you'll meet most
often are ADDIE, the Dick and Carey model, the Morrison, Ross and Kemp model, SAM (the Successive
Approximation Model) and, for classroom teaching, ASSURE. Most comparison pages stop there and list
the steps. The more useful fact is the one the field's own reference work states plainly: almost none
of these models has been rigorously tested.

That comes from Kent Gustafson and Robert Branch's *Survey of Instructional Development Models*, the
standard catalogue of the models, which has run through several editions
([Gustafson & Branch, 2002](https://people.potsdam.edu/betrusak/621/survey_of_intructional_development_models.pdf)).


<ResearchCard n={0} />

Instead, they write, models with wide distribution "gain their credibility by being found useful by
practitioners, who frequently adapt and modify them to match specific conditions." In their
conclusion they go further: it appears many models "have never actually been applied, never mind
rigorously evaluated."

<ResearchCard n={1} />

That changes how to choose one. If no model is proven better than the others, the question isn't
which is best. It's which fits what you're building. This guide compares the main models, separates
them from the frameworks often listed alongside them, and gives a four-step way to pick.

## Process models and design frameworks are different things

Search for instructional design models and you'll find lists that mix two kinds of thing:

- **Process models** describe how to run a design project from start to finish: ADDIE, Dick and Carey,
  Morrison, Ross and Kemp, SAM, ASSURE.
- **Design frameworks** describe what good instruction contains, whatever process you use: Merrill's
  first principles of instruction, backward design, action mapping. Some lists even include
  [Bloom's taxonomy](/guides/blooms-taxonomy-examples/), which classifies learning objectives and
  isn't a model at all.

You don't choose between the two kinds. You run a process model and use frameworks inside its
phases: backward design while you plan assessments, Merrill's principles while you design each lesson.

Most process models are also versions of the same work. Gustafson and Branch describe "five core
elements—analyze, design, develop, implement, and evaluate (ADDIE)" that the models share, and the
textbook *Foundations of Learning and Instructional Design Technology* calls ADDIE a process and each
model "a specific instance of a process that can be imitated or emulated"
([Dousay, 2018](https://edtechbooks.org/lidtfoundations/instructional_design_models)). So learning one model
well teaches you most of the others.

## The main models at a glance

| Model | Kind | Shape | Fits best | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ADDIE | Generic process | Five phases, meant to loop | Any project, as a checklist of the work | Being run as a one-way waterfall |
| Dick and Carey | Systems process | Detailed sequence of steps | Whole courses where analysis and evaluation matter | Heavy for a small project |
| Morrison, Ross and Kemp | Systems process | Nine elements in a cycle; start anywhere | Curriculum planning with revision throughout | Less guidance on order for beginners |
| SAM and rapid prototyping | Iterative process | Short rounds of prototype and review | Uncertain briefs, eLearning built with a client | Prototypes that harden into the final product |
| ASSURE | Classroom process | Six steps | A teacher planning lessons with existing media | Assumes you select materials more than build them |
| Backward design | Framework | Three stages | Aligning objectives, assessment and lessons | Says little about running the project |
| Action mapping | Framework | Goal, actions, practice, minimum information | Workplace training tied to a business problem | Less suited to open-ended education |
| Merrill's first principles | Framework | Five principles | Designing lessons around real tasks | Not a project process on its own |

## The process models

### ADDIE: the generic process

ADDIE's five letters, analysis, design, development, implementation and evaluation, name the work
every course needs. It has no single author, and the strictly linear version critics attack is a
reading of it rather than the model itself. We've covered it in depth in our
[guide to the ADDIE model](/guides/addie-model/), including what each phase should produce.

### Dick and Carey: the detailed systems model

Gustafson and Branch call the Dick, Carey and Carey model "the standard" against which other models
are compared. It starts with a needs assessment to set instructional goals, runs instructional
analysis and learner and context analysis side by side, then moves through performance objectives,
assessment instruments, instructional strategy, materials, and formative and summative evaluation.
The survey classes it as a systems model and notes how detailed it is in analysis and evaluation.

Its strength is that order: assessments are written before materials, and the course is tried out and
revised before release. Its cost is effort. For a three-lesson course it's more procedure than you need.

### Morrison, Ross and Kemp: the flexible cycle

Jerrold Kemp's model, later developed with Gary Morrison and Steven Ross, is organised around nine
elements, from identifying the instructional problem and learner characteristics to analysing
content, writing objectives and choosing resources. Revision runs alongside every element, and the
survey notes that a designer "can start anywhere and proceed in any order." It suits experienced
designers planning a curriculum who want a checklist rather than a sequence.

### SAM and rapid prototyping: build early, test often

Rapid prototyping, which the survey traces to Tripp and Bichelmeyer (1990), "emphasizes early
development of a simple and incomplete prototype" that evolves as the client's needs become clearer.
SAM, developed by Michael Allen's company Allen Interactions, is the best-known version for
eLearning. It runs in three phases: preparation, iterative design and iterative development.
Early prototypes are meant to be thrown away: "the less investment in initial prototypes, the better"
([Manning, 2016](https://blog.alleninteractions.com/using-an-iterative-process-to-bake-successful-e-learning-results)).

Gustafson and Branch describe rapid prototyping as most useful when the client isn't sure what they
want or the project needs a creative solution. That's most eLearning briefs.

### ASSURE: the classroom model

ASSURE, from Heinich, Molenda, Russell and Smaldino, has six steps: analyse learners, state objectives,
select media and materials, use them, require learner participation, and evaluate and revise. It's
built for a teacher planning their own lessons, who typically selects and adapts existing materials
rather than producing new ones. The survey praises it as practical and easy for teachers to apply.

## The frameworks you use inside a model

### Backward design

Backward design, from Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe, runs in three stages: identify the results you
want, decide what evidence will show learners have reached them, then plan the lessons. It's the
best habit to bring into any model's design phase. Our [guide to backward design](/guides/backward-design/)
works through it on an online course.

### Action mapping

Cathy Moore's action mapping starts from a measurable business goal, lists what people need to do on
the job, and checks whether training is even the answer before designing anything
([Moore, 2014](https://blog.cathy-moore.com/online-learning-conference-anti-handout/)). Where it is, learners practise realistic decisions
and get only the information each activity needs. Her advice to "resist the temptation to present and
then quiz" is worth taping to the monitor.

### Merrill's first principles: the one with some evidence

David Merrill reviewed instructional design theories to find the principles they share
([Merrill, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02505024)). He arrived at five, each stated as
"Learning is promoted when…": learners are engaged in solving real-world problems, existing knowledge
is activated, new knowledge is demonstrated, learners apply it, and they integrate it into their own
world. Merrill called the paper "a preliminary report."

Unlike the process models, the principles have been studied in real courses. Frick and colleagues
surveyed 464 students in 12 undergraduate courses at one US university, then had instructors rate each
student's mastery of the course objectives about a month later
([Frick et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11423-009-9131-z)). Students who agreed that both the
first principles and their own successful engagement with tasks had occurred were about five times as
likely to be rated high masters as students who did not. That's some evidence, not proof:
the study was correlational, used student ratings of the teaching, and covered face-to-face courses
at a single university. The principles also overlap with better-tested findings, such as
[retrieval practice](/library/retrieval-practice/) for the application step.

## How to choose a model: four questions

### 1. What are you building?

Gustafson and Branch sort models by what they produce:

| | Classroom | Product | System |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical output | One or a few hours of instruction | A self-instructional product | A course or whole curriculum |
| Who does it | Usually one person | A team | A team |
| Front-end analysis | Low | Low to medium | Very high |
| Tryout and revision | Low to medium | Very high | Medium to high |

A teacher planning a unit is in the classroom column: ASSURE, or a light ADDIE pass. A creator
building a self-paced course that strangers will take alone is in the product column, where testing
with real learners matters most. An L&D team redesigning a company's whole onboarding programme is
building a system: Dick and Carey or Morrison, Ross and Kemp earn their extra procedure there.

### 2. How certain is the brief?

If you know exactly what learners need to do, a sequential model is efficient. If the client keeps
saying "I'll know it when I see it", or nobody has built this kind of course before, start
prototyping. For example, a small team asked to build a customer-service simulation would mock up one
scenario in a day, put it in front of a few staff and the manager, and revise before designing the
rest. That's SAM in practice, and it's also what our
[guide to piloting a course](/guides/how-to-pilot-an-online-course/) recommends for any build.

### 3. Is training the answer?

Before any model's design phase, ask what problem the course is meant to fix. If sales staff aren't
logging calls because the software is slow, a course won't help. Action mapping's flowchart is the
quickest check, and it fits inside ADDIE's analysis phase or SAM's preparation phase.

### 4. Which framework will shape the lessons?

The process model gets the course built. What makes each lesson work comes from frameworks with
evidence behind them: design the assessment first with backward design, build lessons around real
tasks with Merrill's principles, and keep each screen within what learners can process
([cognitive load theory](/library/cognitive-load-theory/)).

In higher education the unit outline and learning outcomes usually arrive already approved, so I start from them and work backwards: our learning model sits in the background as a checklist, but where I’m building a new unit in Canvas and showing it to the academic beforehand, we do reports on what has changed, and assemble student feedback or additional research, if possible, to justify changes and help us all save time in the sign off process.

## What the comparisons miss

Most pages comparing instructional design models present them as rival products, each with pros and
cons, as if picking the right one guarantees a good course. The evidence doesn't support that, because
it barely exists. Gustafson and Branch advise designers to "acquire a working knowledge of several
models", one from each of their three categories, and to choose for the job in front of them.

In practice, we'd keep it simple. Use ADDIE as the checklist of work, run it in short loops with a
prototype early, and put your effort into the parts with the best evidence: clear objectives,
assessments that match them, and plenty of practice with feedback.

## What to do next

Before your next project starts, answer the first question in one line: are you building a lesson, a
product or a system? Write it at the top of your plan, and choose the model that fits that column.
