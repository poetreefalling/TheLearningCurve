The best instructional design portfolio examples aren't the prettiest modules. They're the ones that
show a real problem being solved. In a survey of 101 people who hire instructional designers, run in
2023 by the course provider Devlin Peck, "ability to solve real-world problems with instructional
design" was ranked first more often than any other portfolio quality, ahead of user experience
([Peck, 2023](https://www.devlinpeck.com/content/id-hiring-manager-report)). It's a self-selected
sample, mostly corporate and mostly American, so treat it as a strong hint rather than a census. But
it points the same way as older research on what employers expect of new designers.

This guide gives six annotated portfolio examples, each built to show problem-solving rather than
polish, and a template for writing each one up. The examples are illustrative, not real people's
portfolios: they describe what to build and what to say about it.

## What hiring managers look at

Portfolios matter more in instructional design than in most fields. In a 2022 survey of hiring
representatives at New Jersey businesses across industries, only a third said they encourage
applicants to submit a portfolio, though three-quarters thought one could help
([Holtzman et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1108/JWAM-01-2021-0001)). Among instructional design
hiring managers, the picture is different.

<ResearchCard n={0} />

In the same survey, 25.7 per cent required a portfolio, a further 38.6 per cent said it plays a
significant role in hiring even when not required, and 72.3 per cent named it among their top three
considerations. The respondents' comments explain what they're looking for. One wrote: "I want to know
what design they did, rather than what they built." Another looks for "design statements that accompany
portfolio work: the 'why' behind design decisions." A third said the consulting work with a subject
expert or client "is more critical to me than looking at a pretty finished product"
([Peck, 2023](https://www.devlinpeck.com/content/id-hiring-manager-report)).

Two cautions. The survey was run by someone who sells instructional design training, it was shared on
LinkedIn, YouTube and his mailing list, and its author says 101 responses are likely not enough to
represent hiring managers worldwide. And it measures what managers say, not who they hired.

Still, it matches peer-reviewed work. When Villachica and colleagues surveyed 185 members of a
professional association, more than half expected new designers to perform 22 common tasks across all
the phases of the ADDIE model, and most said new hires fell short of that without substantial help
([Villachica et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/piq.20067)). A portfolio of finished screens shows
one phase, development. The examples below are built to show the others too.

## Six portfolio examples, annotated

Each example below names the problem, what to show, and the weak version hiring managers see most
often. Pick the two to four that best match the jobs you're applying for, so the set shows "the
ability to design different types of solutions based on the need", as one survey respondent put it.

### 1. A branching scenario for a workplace skill

**The problem:** new retail supervisors handle customer complaints badly in their first month, and the
existing slide course hasn't helped.

**What to show:** a short branching scenario (a practice conversation where each choice leads to a
different outcome) in which the learner handles an escalating complaint. Show the decision map, not just
the finished screens, with a sentence on why each wrong option is a mistake supervisors actually make.
Explain how you found those mistakes: an interview with a store manager is enough.

**The weak version:** a click-to-reveal module about complaint handling, with a quiz on the policy at the
end. It looks finished, but it tests recall of a policy when the job is a conversation.

### 2. A redesigned module from a university course

**The problem:** students in an online first-year unit drop off in week three, where a single 40-minute
recorded lecture covers the hardest topic.

**What to show:** a before-and-after. The original structure, your analysis of where students struggled,
and the redesign: the lecture split into short segments, each followed by practice, with the reasoning.
Naming the principle behind a choice, such as [cognitive load](/library/cognitive-load-theory/) when you
split dense material, turns instinct into an argument a reader can follow.

**The weak version:** the new module on its own, with no original to compare it to and no account of why
it changed.

### 3. A job aid, and the analysis that chose it

**The problem:** a manager asks for a course on a new expense system. Your conversations with staff show
they understand the policy; they forget the steps because they file expenses once a month.

**What to show:** the short analysis that led you away from a course, and the one-page job aid (a
checklist used at the moment of the task) you built instead. This piece shows the judgement many
portfolios lack: knowing when training isn't the answer.

**The weak version:** a 20-minute module on the expense system, built because that's what was asked for.

### 4. An assessment set with an objective map

**The problem:** a compliance course's final quiz tests definitions, while the real risk is staff
misjudging situations.

**What to show:** three or four [measurable objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/), a set
of scenario-based questions mapped to them in a small table, and the feedback for each wrong option. Our
guide to [writing multiple-choice questions](/guides/writing-multiple-choice-questions/) covers what
good items look like. This doubles as a writing sample, which matters because written communication is
named in most job ads, as our [instructional design jobs guide](/guides/instructional-design-jobs/) shows.

**The weak version:** a quiz built in an authoring tool, with no objectives, no rationale and feedback
that only says "Incorrect".

### 5. A short instructional video, with its script

**The problem:** field technicians need to learn a five-step equipment check they'll do on site, often
with a phone in one hand.

**What to show:** a two- or three-minute video, its script and storyboard, and why you made the choices
you did: why video rather than text, why the steps appear on screen, why it's that length. Include
captions; prerecorded video needs captions to meet Level A of the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines
([W3C, 2023](https://www.w3.org/TR/2023/REC-WCAG22-20231005/)).

**The weak version:** a polished talking-head video with no script, no captions and no explanation of
what learners were meant to be able to do afterwards.

### 6. A design document or storyboard

**The problem:** you don't yet have a built project, or your best work belongs to a client.

**What to show:** the planning documents for a course: the learner brief, objectives, course map and a
[storyboard](/guides/elearning-storyboard/) for one module. Design documents are the work most hiring
managers never see in a portfolio, and they show analysis and design directly. Pair one with a small
built sample if you can.

**The weak version:** a storyboard template filled in with placeholder text.

## How to write up each example

Learning design has its own genre for this. The *International Journal of Designs for Learning* has
published design cases, accounts of a real design and the decisions behind it, since 2010; Elizabeth
Boling's opening editorial describes the design case as "a specialized and critical form of design
knowledge" ([Boling, 2010](https://doi.org/10.14434/ijdl.v1i1.919)). In 2025 the journal added a
short-form version, Quick Hit design cases, for authors without time for a full one
([Boling et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.14434/ijdl.v16i1.41130)). A portfolio write-up is a quick hit
for a hiring manager. Here's the shape we'd use, in roughly 300 to 500 words per project:

| Section | What it answers | Example line |
|---|---|---|
| The problem | What wasn't working, and for whom? | "New supervisors were escalating complaints their teams could have resolved." Add a figure only if you have a real one and may share it. |
| Your role | What did you do, and what did others do? | "I ran the analysis and wrote the scenario; a developer built it in Storyline." |
| Learners and constraints | Who were they, and what limited the solution? | "Supervisors learn on the shop floor, on a shared tablet, in 15-minute gaps." |
| Key decisions | What did you choose, and why? | "A branching scenario, because the skill is a conversation, not a policy." |
| The sample | What can the reader try? | A link that opens in one click, or screenshots and the storyboard |
| What happened | What did you test, and what changed? | "Three supervisors tried it; two missed the same branch, so I rewrote it." |

The last row is where honesty matters most. If you don't have outcome data, don't invent it: describe
what you tested and what you changed as a result. Our guide to
[piloting a course](/guides/how-to-pilot-an-online-course/) shows how to run a small test that gives you
something true to report.

## When your best work is confidential

Much real design work sits under a non-disclosure agreement. Putting it behind a password doesn't change
what the agreement allows. In our view there are two honest options. Check what you're permitted to share
and get written permission if you're unsure. Or rebuild the capability, not the deliverable: design a new
scenario on an invented topic that uses the same approach, label it as a recreated sample, and describe
the original project only at the level you're allowed to (the kind of client, the problem, your role).

## Five mistakes that make a portfolio forgettable

1. **Ten projects with no write-ups.** A gallery shows output. Three cases show judgement.
2. **Every piece built in the same template.** One respondent in the Peck survey said a portfolio full of
   Rise projects makes skill hard to judge. Vary the solution, not just the topic.
3. **Hiding your role.** If others built, wrote or approved parts of a project, say so. Managers will ask.
4. **Samples that don't open.** Broken links, downloads, logins or files that only run on one browser.
   Test every link on a laptop you don't own before you apply.
5. **Ignoring accessibility.** User experience ranked second in the survey. Captions, readable contrast
   (at least 4.5:1 for normal text at Level AA) and keyboard access are part of it
   ([W3C, 2023](https://www.w3.org/TR/2023/REC-WCAG22-20231005/)).

## What to do next

Take the project you're proudest of and write its case in the six sections above, in under 500 words,
before you touch the built sample again. If the "key decisions" row is hard to fill, that's the part
of the project to rework first.
