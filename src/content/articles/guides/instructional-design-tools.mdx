Instructional design tools are the software you use to plan, write, build, record and deliver a
course. Most lists of them run to dozens of products. You need far fewer: something to write and plan
in, something to storyboard in, one tool to build interactive content when a project calls for it, a
way to record and edit video, and the learning management system (LMS) or course platform the course
will live in. Much of that you probably already own.

The best evidence on which tools matter comes from studies of what employers actually ask for. The
most popular "instructional design software" articles don't cite any. This guide starts with that
evidence, then works through the toolkit stage by stage, with what you can skip.

## What employers actually ask for

Two peer-reviewed studies have counted the tools named in instructional design job ads.

The larger one analysed 1,030 US job announcements collected in September and October 2019
([Wang et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijtd.12209)). The researchers coded the knowledge,
skills and abilities each ad asked for, including knowledge of software. Their summary of the technology findings is
plain.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

Presentation software was named in 38 per cent of ads, an LMS in 37 per cent, and word processing
and spreadsheets in about 35 per cent each. Educational authoring software such as Articulate and
Lectora appeared in 33.4 per cent, and authoring tools such as Captivate in 32.9 per cent. Further
down, image editing appeared in 23.6 per cent, screen recording in 20.6 per cent and project
management software in 15.6 per cent.

<Chart id="wang-2021-id-job-tools" />

The smaller, more recent study reached the same conclusion from a different angle. Weber, McCarthy,
Campbell and Bauer analysed US instructional designer and learning technologist postings from May 2023
and interviewed hiring leaders across sectors ([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606)).
The most-named technology was an LMS, in 29.2 per cent of postings, followed by video (20.8 per cent),
Microsoft's office suite (17.7 per cent), Articulate (16.2 per cent) and Adobe Creative Suite
(15.4 per cent). HTML appeared in 4.6 per cent. The authors note that hiring managers "did not always
require skills with particular technologies", and one higher education leader put it directly.

<ResearchCard n={0} />

Another interviewee said that what makes good content is "not your ability to
just hit buttons on Captivate" (p. 12).

Two cautions. Both are US samples, one from 2019, and Weber's is small; Australian and UK ads may
differ. And an ad that doesn't name a tool may still expect you to use whatever the team uses. But
the pattern is consistent: no single tool appears in most ads, while communication and collaboration
appear in far more. Our guide to [instructional design jobs](/guides/instructional-design-jobs/)
covers the skills side.

## The toolkit, stage by stage

Sort tools by the job they do, not by brand. Here's a small toolkit for each stage of a course
build, using one running example: a short compliance course for a hospitality group, teaching new
staff how to handle a food allergy question from a customer.

### 1. Plan and write: use what you have

Analysis, objectives, outlines and scripts are writing. A word processor and a spreadsheet do the job:
the spreadsheet for a content map (each objective, the lesson that teaches it, the activity that
practises it, the question that assesses it), the document for scripts. This is the "productivity
software" that topped Wang's list, and every designer uses it daily.

For the allergy course, that's a one-page analysis, three objectives (see our guide to
[writing learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/)) and a content map with a
row for each. Shared documents in whatever suite your client or team already uses beat a specialist
tool, because reviewers can comment without learning anything new.

### 2. Storyboard: slides are enough

A storyboard sets out each screen: what's shown, what's said, what the learner does. Presentation
software is the most common tool for it, and for good reason: one slide per screen, with notes for the
narration. Some teams storyboard in a document table instead. Either works. Our
[eLearning storyboard guide](/guides/elearning-storyboard/) walks through the format.

### 3. Build interactive content: one authoring tool, if you need it

An authoring tool builds interactive modules (scenarios, drag-and-drop activities, branching
questions, software simulations) and packages them in a standard such as SCORM or xAPI so an LMS can
launch them and record results. This is where most of the money goes, so ask first whether you need
one at all.

You need an authoring tool when a client or employer delivers through an LMS that expects SCORM or
xAPI packages, or when you want interactions your platform can't build. You may not need one if you
sell courses on a course platform with its own lesson and quiz builder, or if your content is mostly
video and reading with quizzes.

The common options fall into two groups that overlap but solve different problems. **Course
authoring tools** (Articulate 360, Adobe Captivate, iSpring Suite) build whole modules and package
them for an LMS. **Interactive content tools** (H5P, Genially) make individual activities, such as an
interactive video or a branching scenario, that you embed in a page, a course platform or an LMS.

Prices and plans change often, so check the vendor's current pricing page before you buy. All three
paid course authoring tools offer a free trial. Articulate 360's lasts 14 days with no credit card,
but Rise 360 exports carry a watermark during the trial
([Articulate](https://www.articulatesupport.com/article/Articulate-360-FAQs-Free-Trials)). Adobe
Captivate offers a 30-day trial ([Adobe](https://adobe.com/au/products/captivate/download-trial/try.html)),
and iSpring Suite a 14-day trial of the full version, also with no credit card
([iSpring](https://ispringsolutions.com/ispring-suite/trial)).

If you're starting out, pick one and learn it well enough to build a portfolio piece. For corporate
roles, the job-ad evidence points to Articulate as the safest first choice. If you can't justify the
subscription yet, H5P lets you build the same kinds of interactions for free, and the design thinking
transfers. For the allergy course, a short branching scenario (a customer asks whether a dish contains
nuts; the learner chooses what to say) is the one interaction worth building. Everything else can be
video and a quiz.

### 4. Record and edit video: start simple

Video appeared in about a fifth to a third of ads in both studies, and Weber's interviewees said
expectations for video are rising. For screen recordings and narrated slides, a screen recorder with
basic editing is enough; [Camtasia](/discover/camtasia/) is the long-standing choice in workplace
learning, and Wang's ads named Camtasia-type software in 20.6 per cent. For talking-head or scenario
footage, any mainstream editor will do. Spend on a decent microphone before you spend on software:
learners forgive plain visuals faster than bad audio.

Our guide to [how long course videos should be](/guides/how-long-should-course-videos-be/) covers the
design side.

### 5. Deliver: the platform decides a lot

An LMS appeared at or near the top of both studies' lists, but employers mostly want experience with
an LMS, not a particular one, since every organisation runs its own. If you build courses to sell, the
equivalent is a course platform such as <PlatformLink id="thinkific" />,
<PlatformLink id="learnworlds" />, <PlatformLink id="teachable" /> or <PlatformLink id="kajabi" />.

Choose or confirm the delivery platform before the authoring tool. Many platforms include quiz
builders, assignments and certificates, which may cover what you were going to buy an authoring tool
for.

If you plan to build in Articulate, Captivate or iSpring and upload the result, check whether your
platform imports SCORM packages, and whether that is included on the plan you'd use. The four big
course platforms differ a lot:

- **Thinkific:** SCORM is available only on its Plus plan, which can track completion, pass or fail
  and scores ([Thinkific](https://www.thinkific.com/pricing/comparison/)).
- **LearnWorlds:** SCORM and HTML5 packages are available from the Pro Trainer plan up, not on
  Starter, and graded SCORM activities from Pro Trainer too
  ([LearnWorlds](https://support.learnworlds.com/support/solutions/articles/12000052986)).
- **Kajabi:** "Kajabi does not support LMS formats like SCORM or HTML5 files"
  ([Kajabi](https://help.kajabi.com/en/articles/17174537-is-kajabi-scorm-compliant)).
- **Teachable:** its help centre lists video, audio, PDFs and images as the supported file types, and
  doesn't mention SCORM ([Teachable](https://support.teachable.com/en/articles/11682497-content-file-types)).

Check not just whether a platform can launch your content, but whether it records completion,
scores and pass or fail results. Embedding an interactive HTML5 lesson in a page is not the same as
running a SCORM package that reports back.

### 6. Images and graphics: free is fine

Image editing appeared in about a quarter of ads in the 2019 study. You need to crop screenshots,
annotate them and make simple diagrams. Browser-based design tools and the image features in your
presentation software cover most of this. Professional image suites are worth it if you also do
graphic design; otherwise they're a cost you can defer.

### 7. AI assistants: use them for drafts, check everything

Most authoring tools now include AI features. Articulate's AI Assistant, for example, is described by
the vendor as drafting outlines, lesson content and knowledge checks inside Rise and Storyline. These
can save time on first drafts and question banks. They can't tell you whether a question tests the
objective or whether a scenario matches what staff actually face, which is the instructional design
part. Treat AI output as a draft from a fast junior writer: useful, and in need of checking.

At Avondale University, we've used Articulate 360 in the past, and iSpring to build SCORM files. But Avondale uses Canvas (by Instructure) as its LMS, so we've had our own learning curve working out how to use HTML and Canvas's built-in tools to create immersive, personalised learning journeys. Our single biggest ingredient is documentary-quality video production, which makes the experience immersive and memorable for learners.

## What you can skip

- **Coding, at least at first.** HTML appeared in 4.6 per cent of ads in the 2024 study, markup
  languages in 9.9 per cent and JavaScript in 6.9 per cent in the 2019 sample.
- **A second authoring tool.** Learn one properly. The concepts (triggers, states, variables,
  branching) transfer when a job needs another.
- **Specialist project management software.** It appeared in 15.6 per cent of 2019 ads. A shared
  spreadsheet or the task tool your team already uses is enough for most projects.
- **Long tool lists.** The pages that rank for this topic list up to 60 products, mixing in books,
  courses and general office apps. Some are published by companies that sell instructional design
  training or software. Use them to discover options, not to decide what's essential.

<Callout>
  Before you buy anything, list your next course's stages and write the tool you'll use for each.
  If a stage says "nothing yet", that's the one to fill. If it already has a tool you own, you
  don't need another.
</Callout>

## What to do next

Write down where your next course will be delivered and what format it expects. If it's an LMS that
wants SCORM, start a trial of one authoring tool and build a single five-minute interaction from your
storyboard. If it's a course platform, build the same lesson with the platform's own tools first, and
only look at authoring tools if you hit a limit. Then add it to your
[portfolio](/guides/instructional-design-portfolio-examples/).
