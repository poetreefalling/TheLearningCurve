There is no official instructional designer salary. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), the source
behind most government pay figures, doesn't count instructional designers as an occupation of their own.
The two closest occupations had median wages of **US$69,280** (training and development specialists) and
**US$77,440** (instructional coordinators) in May 2025 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/OOH/business-and-financial/training-and-development-specialists.htm);
[BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/education-training-and-library/instructional-coordinators.htm)). A 2023 survey
of instructional designers themselves put the average for US full-time employees at **US$83,347**
([Peck, 2023](https://www.devlinpeck.com/content/instructional-designer-salary)).

Those three numbers don't contradict each other. They measure different groups in different ways, and
the range around each one is wide. This guide charts what each source shows, where the money differs
(sector and experience, mostly), and how to use the data to work out what to ask for.

## Why there's no single instructional designer salary

Most "instructional designer salary" figures online come from salary websites that collect anonymous,
self-reported pay. The pages ranking for this search mostly quote one of those averages with no date, no
sample size and no explanation of what was counted. That's not useless, but it's not a benchmark.

Government data is more rigorous but less precise about the job. The BLS surveys employers about the pay
of every occupation in its classification, and instructional designers fall inside two of them:

- **Training and development specialists** design and run training programmes, mostly inside
  organisations. This is where most corporate instructional designers, eLearning developers and
  L&D specialists are counted. It also covers training roles in industries such as accommodation and
  food services, many of which are likely to involve delivering training rather than designing courses.
- **Instructional coordinators** develop curricula and teaching materials and assess how well they
  work, mostly in schools, colleges and government. Many work in school systems, and the BLS lists a
  master's degree as the typical entry qualification.

So the government figures are reliable about a group that is broader than instructional design. The
survey figures are specific to instructional designers but self-reported by people who chose to answer.
Read them together.

## What the government data shows: a wide range

The median is only the middle. For job seekers, the spread is more useful, because it shows how far
pay can sit above or below the middle for the same occupation.

  <Step view="both">Instructional coordinators sat a little higher in the middle, at US$77,440, with a narrower range: US$47,980 at the 10th percentile and US$121,670 at the 90th.</Step>

These are employee wages, not total pay: they leave out bonuses and benefits, and they exclude the
self-employed, so freelancers aren't in them at all.

## Industry moves pay more than the job title

Within training and development specialists, the industry you work in makes a large difference. The
BLS reports medians for the five industries that employ the most of them.

<DataChart id="bls-td-industries" />

The bottom of that chart is worth reading carefully. Accommodation and food services has a median of
US$35,330, probably because its training roles are mostly front-line and on the job, not course design.
Those roles pull the occupation's overall median down, which is one reason the BLS median is lower than
the survey average.

Instructional coordinators show a different pattern, because they work mostly in education and
government.

<DataChart id="bls-ic-industries" />

State colleges and universities had the lowest median of the five, US$61,810, against US$89,920 in
government roles outside education.

## What instructional designers themselves report

Devlin Peck surveyed people in the field between July and September
2023 and published the results as a salary report. After removing managers, people who wanted to enter the
field and incomplete answers, 1,075 responses remained, 518 of them from US full-time employees
([Peck, 2023](https://www.devlinpeck.com/content/instructional-designer-salary)).

It's the most useful practitioner dataset we found, with limits worth stating plainly. Respondents chose to
take part and were recruited mainly through LinkedIn and the author's own channels, which the report
itself says may skew results slightly high. It reports averages, which large salaries pull upwards, rather
than medians. Some groups are small.

<DataChart id="id-survey-sector" />

Corporate instructional designers averaged US$87,384 and those in higher education US$68,474, about
US$19,000 less. Government, healthcare and non-profit roles sat in between.

<DataChart id="id-survey-experience" />

Pay rises with experience, from US$77,856 in the first three years to US$93,046 at 9 to 15 years. Beyond
that the groups are too small (14, 8 and 6 people) to say much, including about the dip at 26 years and
over.

## The gap every source agrees on: universities pay less

The strongest pattern in the data is the gap between corporate and higher education roles. It shows up
in two independent sources measured in different ways: the practitioner survey (corporate US$87,384,
higher education US$68,474) and the BLS figures for instructional coordinators (state colleges and
universities the lowest of their top five industries, at US$61,810).

That matters for the many people moving into instructional design from teaching, who often look first at
university roles. University jobs can offer other things (stability, leave, the work itself), but on
pay alone, the evidence points to corporate learning and development. Our guide to
[instructional design jobs for former teachers](/guides/instructional-design-jobs-for-former-teachers/)
covers how teaching skills transfer to both.

## How to work out what to ask for

1. **Match your role to the right comparison.** A corporate eLearning developer compares with training
   and development specialists in professional services or finance; a university learning designer
   compares with higher education figures. *Example:* someone applying for a learning designer role at a
   state university should expect pay nearer the US$61,810–68,474 end of the data than the corporate
   figures.
2. **Read the range, not the middle.** Place yourself on the spread by experience and responsibility.
   *Example:* a designer with two years' experience and a portfolio is unlikely to start at the 90th
   percentile, and shouldn't price themselves at the 10th either.
3. **Check current job ads.** Where ads include a salary range, those ranges are the closest benchmark
   you'll get for a job like the one you want. Collect ten and note the middle of each range.
4. **Price the skills that move pay.** Authoring tools, video, learning analytics and the ability to run
   a project from analysis to evaluation separate a senior role from a junior one. The
   [ADDIE model](/guides/addie-model/) is a good map of the full range of that work.
5. **Show the work.** In our view, a portfolio with real outcomes (what learners could do afterwards,
   not just how the course looked) is the strongest evidence for the upper end of a range.

## Freelance and contract rates

There's no reliable public dataset of freelance instructional design rates. The BLS figures exclude the
self-employed, and the survey reports annual self-employed earnings rather than hourly or daily rates.

## In practice

If you're weighing a move into instructional design, the data supports three plain conclusions. The pay
range is wide, so the job you take matters more than the job title. Corporate roles pay more than
universities in every source we found. And the jump into management is the largest single step in pay,
so the skills that lead there (planning programmes, managing projects and people, showing results) are
worth building early. If you're starting out, our guide to
[entry-level instructional design jobs](/guides/entry-level-instructional-design-jobs/) covers what
employers ask for.

## What to do next

Find ten current job ads for the role you want, in your sector and city, and write down the middle of
each advertised range. Compare the spread with the government data above for the same sector, and set
your ask from where your experience puts you in that range.
