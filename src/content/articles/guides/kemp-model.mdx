The Kemp model is an instructional design model built from nine elements: the decisions you make
when planning instruction, from defining the problem and the learners to writing objectives,
choosing strategies, designing and building the instruction, and writing evaluation instruments. What sets it apart is
the shape. The elements sit in a cycle rather than a line, revision and evaluation run around all of
them, and the authors say a designer can start with whichever element the project needs.

That freedom is the model's selling point, and it comes with a catch. When Kenneth Gustafson and
Robert Branch surveyed instructional design models for the ERIC Clearinghouse, they noted that
although the model indicates you can start anywhere, the narrative of the authors' own textbook
suggests beginning with task analysis ([Gustafson & Branch, 2002](https://people.potsdam.edu/betrusak/621/survey_of_intructional_development_models.pdf), p. 29).
The diagram is flexible; the book behind it has a preferred route in.

This guide explains the nine elements, walks through them on one example course, and covers when
starting in the middle helps and when it quietly leaves gaps.

## Where the Kemp model came from

The model was created by Jerrold Kemp and later adapted with Gary Morrison and Steven Ross, which is
why it's also called the Morrison, Ross and Kemp model, or MRK. Gustafson and Branch describe it as a
model "with a focus on curriculum planning" that approaches instruction "from the perspective of the
learner rather than from the content" (p. 26).

It lives in the authors' textbook, *Designing Effective Instruction*, and it has kept changing with
each edition.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

Two details show how far that change has gone. Gustafson and Branch placed the model among the
*classroom-oriented* models, those aimed at teachers planning their own instruction. Reviewing the
sixth edition in 2011, Yavuz Akbulut found that it "looks more product-oriented than
classroom-oriented", noted an emphasis on business settings, and described it as applicable in
higher education, school classrooms, and military and medical settings
([Akbulut, 2011](https://www.cedtech.net/download/book-review-designing-effective-instruction-6063.pdf)).
And the eighth edition, published by Wiley in 2019, lists Gary Morrison, Steven Ross, Jennifer
Morrison and Howard Kalman as authors; Kemp's name is no longer on the cover
([Morrison et al., 2019](https://digitalcommons.odu.edu/stemps_books/2/)). The model still carries
it.

## The nine elements at a glance

The pages that rank for this topic give at least three different lists of the nine elements, with
different names and, in some cases, different elements. Part of the reason is that the list has
changed between editions. The table below uses the names in the current, eighth edition, which
confirms that the model still has nine interrelated elements (p. 13, illustrated in Figure 1.1 on
p. 14) and that they overlap and needn't be completed in a fixed sequence (p. 18)
([Morrison et al., 2019](https://digitalcommons.odu.edu/stemps_books/2/)).

| # | Element | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Instructional problems | What's the problem, and is instruction the right fix? What are the goals? |
| 2 | Learner and context | Who are the learners, where will they learn, and what about either will shape your decisions? |
| 3 | Task analysis | What content and tasks does the goal involve? |
| 4 | Instructional objectives | What, precisely, will learners be able to do? |
| 5 | Content sequencing | In what order should the content come within each unit? |
| 6 | Instructional strategies | How will each learner master the objectives? |
| 7 | Designing the message | How will each lesson be presented? |
| 8 | Development of the instruction | How will the materials actually be built? |
| 9 | Evaluation instruments | How will you know learners met each objective? |

The older version is worth knowing, because many web pages still copy it. In the 2001 edition, as
Gustafson and Branch quote it (p. 26–28), the seventh element was to "plan the instructional message
and develop the instruction", the eighth was evaluation instruments, and the ninth was to "select
resources to support instruction and learning activities". The 2019 edition splits message design
and development into two elements and no longer names resource selection as one of the nine:
resources and specialist support are discussed under the ongoing process of support services.

Around the nine sit the processes that never stop. Gustafson and Branch describe the model as
communicating the authors' belief that design is "a continuous cycle with revision as an on-going"
activity, and note that the then-current version placed greater emphasis on formative and summative
evaluation as continuous processes (p. 28–29). Akbulut's review of the sixth edition describes the
same structure: a "continuous cycle of nine elements" supported by "ongoing processes related to
planning, implementation and evaluation".

## Working through the nine elements: an example

To make each element concrete, we'll follow one example: a carpentry teacher at a TAFE college
moving a classroom unit on reading building plans into a blended course, with online lessons
between workshop sessions. It's an invented example, but it's typical of the projects this model
was built for.

### 1. Identify the instructional problem

Start by asking what's actually wrong and whether teaching will fix it. The teacher's problem
statement: apprentices pass the plan-reading quiz but still mis-measure on the job, and workshop time
is too short to give each of them enough practice.

That framing matters. If the real cause were missing tools or a supervisor who never checks
measurements, a better course wouldn't help. Here, the cause is too little practice reading real
plans, so instruction is a reasonable fix. **Goal:** apprentices can read a residential floor plan
and take off accurate measurements without help.

### 2. Learner and context

List what about the learners and their setting will change your decisions, not everything you know about them. For
this group: first-year apprentices, mostly aged under 25, confident on phones but rarely on laptops,
working full-time on site, with patchy maths confidence and an hour or two a week for online study.

Each item points somewhere. Phone-first means lessons that work on a small screen. Patchy maths
confidence means worked examples before independent practice. Limited time means short lessons.

### 3. Identify content and analyse the task

Break the goal into what an expert actually does. Reading a floor plan involves finding the scale,
reading dimension lines, interpreting symbols for doors, windows and wall types, and converting
measurements. The task analysis also turns up a step the old unit never taught: checking a
calculated dimension against the drawing's running totals.

This is the element the textbook's narrative tends to start with, and you can see why: it shows what
the course must contain.

### 4. Specify the instructional objectives

Turn the task analysis into objectives you could watch someone meet. "Understand floor plans"
doesn't pass. "Given a residential floor plan at 1:100, calculate the internal length of each
room to within 10 mm" does. Our guide to [writing learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/)
covers how to make them observable.

### 5. Sequence the content

Decide the order within each unit. Here, the order follows the task: scale first, then dimension
lines, then symbols, then full take-offs. Simple plans come before complex ones, and the new
checking step comes straight after learners first calculate a dimension, so it becomes a habit
rather than an afterthought.

### 6. Design the instructional strategies

Choose how learners will get from not knowing to doing. For apprentices with patchy maths
confidence, that means worked examples first, then partly completed problems, then independent
practice, which keeps the load manageable while they learn (see [cognitive load theory](/library/cognitive-load-theory/)).
Workshop sessions are kept for measuring real frames against the plan.

### 7. Design the message

Decide how each lesson is presented. For phone-first learners, that might mean short screen
recordings that zoom into one part of a plan at a time, with the narrator pointing at the detail
being discussed, and practice questions that use cropped plan sections rather than whole sheets that
need pinching and scrolling.

### 8. Develop the instruction

Build it. The teacher records the screen walkthroughs, builds the practice questions in the college's
learning platform and writes the workshop task sheets. Older editions folded this into the previous
element; the 2019 edition gives it its own, which matches how much of a project's time it takes.

### 9. Develop the evaluation instruments

Write the tests that match each objective. The final assessment is a take-off from an unseen plan,
checked against a marking key, plus a workshop check where apprentices measure a set-out against the
drawing. Short practice quizzes in each online lesson double as formative evaluation: they show the
teacher which step is causing trouble.

Many designers prefer to draft these straight after the objectives, which the model allows. That's
[backward design](/guides/backward-design/) inside a Kemp project.

### Around the cycle: support, revision and evaluation

Resources now sit in the ring around the nine elements, under support services, rather than in the
cycle itself. For this unit that means real plans from past jobs (with permission), scale rulers,
a printable symbol sheet, help from the college's learning technology staff, and the teacher's time
to answer questions between sessions.

That ring is also where the model earns its shape. After a pilot with one class,
the practice quizzes show most apprentices stumbling on wall-type symbols. That sends the teacher
back to element 5 (move symbols earlier) and elements 7 and 8 (design and build a lesson on them), without redoing the
rest. A [small pilot](/guides/how-to-pilot-an-online-course/) is the cheapest way to start this loop.

When I was working on the lifestyle medicine course, the lift project we started from a previous incarnation, which was pitched towards a younger demographic. Because we were revamping the course for more mature audience, we had to rethink the approach rework all our learning objectives and start from the ground up. A lot of the science and research was the same, but the delivery was completely different. As a result we expanded our base of experts. enriched science with more practical interactive exercises and broadened the scope. Even so, we had to keep going back to the original research to make sure that we were true to the findings.

## Where should you start?

The model's best-known feature is that you don't have to start at element 1.

<ResearchCard n={0} />

Akbulut's 2007 comparison makes the same point: the elements "are not required to be considered in
an orderly way", and an experienced designer can start the design process from whichever step the
context requires. He reports Morrison's view that not all nine elements are required on every
project ([Akbulut, 2007](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/tojde/article/176576), p. 3–4).

Starting in the middle is useful when the project already has a middle:

- **Revising an existing course.** Start at evaluation (element 9) and the results learners got,
  then work back to whichever element caused the problem.
- **A fixed platform or content library.** If the organisation has already bought them, starting
  with message design (element 7) and what those tools can present is honest about the constraint.
- **Content handed to you.** When a subject expert arrives with slides, task analysis (element 3) is
  the natural way in, which is exactly where the textbook's narrative tends to begin.

The risk is that starting anywhere becomes skipping something. Gustafson and Branch's point about
the narrative is a useful warning: even the authors walk readers in through task analysis, because
objectives, sequence and tests all depend on knowing what the task is. Akbulut adds that novice
designers might favour the Dick and Carey model, precisely because it prescribes the order of the
steps.

In practice, we'd treat the cycle as permission to enter where the project is, not as permission to
leave elements out. Wherever you start, check that elements 1 and 2, the problem and the learners,
have been answered before you build anything.

## Kemp, ADDIE and Dick and Carey compared

| | Kemp (Morrison, Ross and Kemp) | ADDIE | Dick and Carey |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Nine elements in a cycle | Five phases, usually drawn as a sequence or loop | Ten components in a set order |
| Where you start | Anywhere the project needs | Analysis | The first component, then in order |
| How revision works | Runs around every element | Evaluation feeds back to earlier phases | Within the fixed sequence |
| Best fit | Experienced designers; revising or planning courses and curricula | Any project, as a checklist of kinds of work | Systematic builds where a set order helps, and newer designers |

Akbulut calls Dick and Carey "a rectilinear ID model which suggests a lockstep approach" and the
Kemp model "a curvilinear ID model" (p. 3). The difference from [ADDIE](/guides/addie-model/) is
mostly one of grain: ADDIE's design phase covers what Kemp splits into objectives, sequencing,
strategies, message design, development and evaluation instruments. If you already run ADDIE in loops, Kemp's nine
elements make a useful checklist for what the design phase should settle. Our comparison of
[instructional design models](/guides/instructional-design-models/) covers the rest of the field.

## Does the Kemp model work?

There's no good evidence either way, and that's true of almost every design model. Gustafson and
Branch's survey opens with the observation that these models are rarely tested "in the sense of
rigorous assessment of their application and the resulting instruction" (p. 1). Akbulut's
comparison is a conceptual paper; it presents no data showing that courses designed with the Kemp
model teach better.

So use the model for what it reliably offers: a complete list of the decisions instruction needs,
and a reminder that revision never stops. The evidence about what makes instruction work, worked
examples, practice and feedback, comes from learning research, not from the model.

## Four common mistakes

1. **Treating "start anywhere" as "skip anything".** Entering at element 7 is fine. Never answering
   element 1 means you might build a good course for the wrong problem.
2. **Listing learner characteristics that change nothing.** Every item in element 2 should point to
   a decision, as phone-first did in the example.
3. **Writing evaluation instruments last.** The model lets you draft them early. Do it, so the
   lessons are built to what you'll test.
4. **Ignoring the outer ring.** If nothing feeds back from evaluation to the earlier elements, you're
   running a line with a circular diagram.

## In practice

For a first project with the Kemp model, work through the nine elements roughly in the order shown,
and keep notes on each. On the second project, or on a revision, enter where the work actually is:
the evaluation results, the content you've been handed, or the platform you're stuck with. The
model's value is that it doesn't pretend every project starts from a blank page.

## What to do next

Take one course you already run and write a one-line answer for each of the nine elements. The
element you can't answer is where your next revision should start.
