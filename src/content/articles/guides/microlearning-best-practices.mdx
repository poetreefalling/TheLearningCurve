The two-minute video is the face of microlearning, and it's the part the evidence supports least.
Microlearning is teaching in short, focused pieces, usually a few minutes each, built around one
task or idea. It works when those pieces carry the things the research supports: **spaced
practice** (coming back to an idea over days and weeks), **recall** (answering from memory rather
than re-reading) and **feedback**. Cutting a long course into short videos, on its own, does
little. Design each piece around a question, space the pieces out, and judge the result by what
people do differently.

That's the short version. Most of the guides ranking for "microlearning" are written by companies
that sell microlearning software, and they lean on engagement figures that rarely link to a study.
This guide starts from the research and the latest industry benchmarks, then turns them into seven
design steps with examples. For the definition and the history of the term, see our library entry,
[Microlearning defined](/library/microlearning/).

## Why microlearning is everywhere now

The simplest reason is time. The Association for Talent Development (ATD) surveys hundreds of
organisations each year about their training. In its benchmarks, the formal learning hours each
employee used fell year after year, then turned.

<Scrolly chart="atd-learning-hours">
  <Step view="fall">**Formal learning hours fell by more than half.** Organisations in ATD's surveys reported 35 hours of formal learning per employee in 2020 and 13.7 hours in 2024. Formal learning means scheduled courses and programmes; on-the-job and informal learning aren't counted.</Step>
  <Step view="rebound">**Then they edged up.** In ATD's 2026 report, covering 2025, hours rose to 16.7 per employee, "for the first time in five years". Over the same year, direct learning spend per employee fell to USD 846, so the extra hours came with less money behind them.</Step>
</Scrolly>

Each year is a different group of organisations, so treat the line as a direction rather than a
precise trend. The direction matches what employees say. In TalentLMS's 2026 benchmark of 1,000 US
employees, 70% said they multitask during training, a three-year high, and 53% said high workloads
leave little room for it ([TalentLMS, 2026](https://www.talentlms.com/research/learning-development-report-2026)).
We looked at that squeeze in more detail in [the workplace learning paradox](/trends/learning-paradox-workplace-learning-time/).

Microlearning is the industry's answer, and it's already mainstream:

<DataChart id="atd-learning-methods" />

Two in three organisations used microlearning in 2024, and another 22% were considering it. The
fastest movement is in AI: 40% already embed it in learning and 36% more are considering it. That
matters for microlearning because AI tools can now generate short quizzes, flashcards and scenario
questions in minutes, which makes it cheap to produce many small pieces. Cheap to produce isn't the
same as good to learn from, which is where the evidence comes in.

## What the research supports, and what it doesn't

There's less direct research on "microlearning" than the market suggests, and the best of it points
to a clear conclusion: the shape helps because of what it makes easy, not because short is better.

**Spacing has the strongest support.** Spaced learning means returning to the same material in
several sessions with gaps between them, instead of covering it all at once (massed learning).
A 2024 meta-analysis pooled 23 randomised and quasi-randomised trials of spaced digital education
for doctors, nurses and other health professionals ([Martinengo et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.2196/57760)).
Spacing came out ahead on knowledge, skills and what clinicians did in practice:

<Chart id="martinengo-2024-spaced-digital" />

<ResearchCard n={0} />

The effect on knowledge was small, the effects on skills and on what clinicians did in practice
were larger, and the confidence intervals are wide where few studies exist. The authors rated most
studies (19 of 23) at unclear or high risk of bias, so this is moderate evidence, not proof. What's
useful for designers is how the spaced programmes were built: in 15 of the 17 online studies, each
piece was a case or small learning unit followed by one or more multiple-choice questions, most often
delivered by email. That's microlearning, but microlearning with a question in every piece.

**The gap between sessions matters, and the right gap grows with how long people need to remember.**
A synthesis of 317 experiments on spaced practice found exactly that ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)):

<ResearchCard n={1} />

In plain terms (ISI is the gap between study sessions): if people need something next week, review it
over a few days; if they need it next year, spread the reviews over weeks and months.

**Recall beats re-reading.** A meta-analysis of the testing effect found that practising recall
produced better retention than restudying the same material, with larger benefits from recall
questions than from recognition ones ([Rowland, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559)). Short
pieces make it easy to put a question in front of learners often.

**Shortness alone has weak support.** A scoping review of 17 microlearning studies in health
professions education found positive effects on knowledge and confidence, but none of the studies
defined microlearning, and few looked beyond the test ([De Gagne et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.2196/13997)):

<ResearchCard n={2} />

None of the 17 measured results for patients or organisations. The popular six-minute video rule has
a similar problem: it came from a study of how long people watched, and its authors warned against
drawing conclusions about learning from it. Our guide to [how long course videos should be](/guides/how-long-should-course-videos-be/)
covers that study in detail.

## How short is short?

Microlearning is often sold as two-minute videos. Viewers don't seem to want that for instruction.
TechSmith, which makes video software, asked 929 people in six countries, Australia among them, how
long they prefer instructional videos to be.

<Scrolly chart="techsmith-instructional-length">
  <Step view="all">**Preferences spread across a wide range.** Most people prefer instructional videos somewhere between three and 19 minutes.</Step>
  <Step view="short">**Very short is a minority taste.** Only 8% preferred instructional videos under three minutes, and almost nobody chose under 30 seconds.</Step>
  <Step view="peak">**The single most popular choice was 10 to 19 minutes,** picked by 26%. At the other end, 60% of viewers said they would not watch a video of 20 minutes or longer.</Step>
</Scrolly>

This is a survey of preferences, not a test of learning, and a vendor's survey at that. But it's a
useful corrective. "Micro" should describe the scope of a piece, one task or idea, not a target
running time. A recall question takes 30 seconds; a worked example of a tricky procedure might take
eight minutes. Both can be microlearning.

## How to design microlearning that works: seven steps

### 1. Start from a task, not a topic

Write down what people need to do differently, in observable terms. "Understand our returns policy"
is a topic. "Process a return for a damaged item in under three minutes without calling a supervisor"
is a task. Microlearning suits tasks that recur, have clear right answers or common mistakes, and
are easy to forget between uses.

*Example:* a retail team keeps miscoding damaged returns. The microlearning goal is the coding
decision, not "returns training".

### 2. Give each piece one objective

Each piece should do one job: teach one step, practise one decision, correct one misconception. If
you can't name the piece's single objective in a sentence, split it. Our guide to
[writing learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/) covers how to word them.

*Example:* "Choose the right return code for a damaged item" is one piece. "Explain the returns
process" is five.

### 3. Make most pieces ask a question

The spaced programmes in the 2024 meta-analysis almost all paired a small case with questions. Aim
for most pieces to ask learners to recall, decide or apply, with explanation only where it's needed
to answer. Scenario questions ("A customer hands you this. What do you do?") beat fact questions for
workplace skills, because they practise the decision itself.

*Example:* a 60-second piece shows a photo of a damaged item and the receipt, then asks which code
to use.

### 4. Space the pieces out

Schedule pieces across days and weeks, and bring back earlier ideas instead of moving steadily
forward. Following Cepeda and colleagues' finding, set the gaps by how long people need to
remember: days for something needed next week, widening to weeks for something needed all year.
Adaptive tools that bring back the questions a learner got wrong sooner, and the ones they got right
later, apply the same idea automatically.

*Example:* the return-code question comes back on day 2, day 7 and day 21, with a fresh photo each
time so learners can't memorise the picture.

### 5. Give feedback that explains

After each answer, say whether it was right and why, briefly. Feedback that explains the reasoning
helps learners more than a tick or a cross. Our library entry on [effective feedback](/library/effective-feedback/)
summarises the evidence.

*Example:* "Code D2: the damage happened in transit, so it's the carrier's claim, not a store write-off."

### 6. Keep the pieces connected to the whole

Short pieces are easy to lose in. Show learners where each one fits (a simple map or progress view),
link related pieces, and keep longer formats for what needs sustained attention: a new mental model,
a complex procedure, a discussion. Microlearning is often best as the practice and review layer
around a longer course, not as a replacement for it.

Even though short videos are often touted as the holy grail in learners with dopamine systems primed by social media, there is a tendency for students to skip shorter videos, or think of them as less consequential. Surprisingly, we find the most engagement and the highest retention (assessed through quizzes and reflection activites afterwards) with videos that sit in the 8-10 minute mark. That might be because a video 8 or 10 minutes long is seen as something a learner shouldn't miss.

*Example:* a 40-minute workshop introduces the returns system; microlearning handles the practice
for the next month.

### 7. Measure behaviour, not views

Completion rates and opens tell you people saw the pieces. Decide before launch what change you
expect to see in the work, and check it: fewer miscoded returns, faster handling, fewer escalations.
Track question accuracy over time too. If people keep getting the same question wrong on its third
return, the piece needs rewriting, not repeating.

*Example:* the team tracks the share of returns coded correctly in the month before and the two
months after.

<Callout>
**Claims to ask for a source.** Microlearning marketing often quotes precise gains in engagement,
retention or speed. If a figure doesn't link to a study you can open, with who was studied and what
was measured, treat it as marketing. Ask: compared with what, measured how, and after how long?
</Callout>

## Microlearning examples, by format

| Format | Example | What makes it work |
|---|---|---|
| Spaced scenario questions | A short case by email or app every two days, with feedback after each answer | Spacing, recall and feedback in one piece |
| Flashcards | A spaced set of key terms or drug doses, with the ones you miss coming back sooner | Recall on a schedule that adapts |
| Demo plus practice | A 90-second screen recording of one task, then a task to try straight away | One objective, immediate application |
| Worked example | A short walk-through of one solved problem, then a similar problem to solve | Shows the thinking before asking for it |
| Job aid with self-check | A one-page checklist used on the job, with three questions at the end | Support at the moment of need |
| Reflection prompt | A question after a real task: what went well, what would you change? | Connects learning to the work |

## When microlearning is the wrong tool

Microlearning is a poor fit when learners need to build a new, connected understanding from scratch,
when a skill has many interdependent steps that only make sense together, or when the goal is
discussion and practice with other people. It also can't fix a lack of time to apply what's learned:
if workloads leave no room for practice, shorter lessons won't create it. In those cases, use a
longer lesson or a live session for the core, and microlearning for the spaced practice afterwards.

## What to do next

Pick one task your learners keep getting wrong. Write three scenario questions about it, each with
a short explanation of the right answer, and schedule them to reach learners two, seven and 21 days
after the main lesson. Compare their answers on day 21 with day 2, and check the task itself a month
later.
