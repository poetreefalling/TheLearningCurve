Most online course completion rates you'll see quoted, 5%, 10%, "85% never finish", come from one of
two places: research on free MOOCs (massive open online courses), or blog posts that don't say where
the number came from. The MOOC research is real, and it shows a median completion rate of about 13% of
enrolled students ([Jordan, 2015](https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v16i3.2112)). But it counts everyone
who clicked enrol, including the half who never opened the course. Your own rate depends on how you
define completion, who you count, and a few design choices you control: how the course starts, how long
it is, and whether the right people bought it.

This guide covers what the research actually measured, how to work out a rate for your own course that
means something, and which changes have evidence behind them. It also says where the evidence is thin,
because some of the most popular fixes have the weakest support.

## Where the famous numbers come from

The best-documented completion data is from MOOCs, because universities and platforms published it and
researchers analysed it.

Katy Jordan of The Open University collected enrolment and completion data for 221 MOOCs, with
completion figures available for 129 of them. Completion rates, which she defines as the percentage of
enrolled students who completed the course, ranged from 0.7% to 52.1%, with a median of 12.6%
([Jordan, 2015](https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v16i3.2112)).

Justin Reich and José Ruipérez-Valiente looked at six years of HarvardX and MITx courses. Of those who
registered for a course, 52% never entered the courseware at all. Completion among all participants sat
between about 3% and 6% each year, and the authors were blunt about the trend
([Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aav7958)).

The same paper shows how much the answer depends on who you count. Learners who paid to enter the
verified certificate track completed at rates between 44% and 56%, around ten times the rate for all
participants. Learners who said in a pre-course survey that they intended to finish sat in between.

<Chart id="reich-2019-mooc-completion-by-cohort" />

That gap is not proof that charging money makes people finish. People who pay are already more
committed, so the comparison mixes the effect of paying with the kind of person who pays. What it does
show is that "completion rate" without a denominator is close to meaningless.

### What about paid courses on creator platforms?

Many of the pages ranking for this topic quote figures for paid, self-paced courses on platforms such as
Thinkific, Teachable or Kajabi: 10–15%, 15–40%, "above 30% is good". We couldn't find a published,
independently collected dataset behind any of them. Some cite each other; most cite nothing. Treat them
as anecdotes, and set your own baseline instead.

## 1. Define completion before you measure it

A completion rate has three parts, and changing any one changes the number:

- **The rule.** What counts as complete? Every lesson marked done, a share of lessons (say 80%), a passed
  final assessment, or a certificate issued. A rule based on a passed assessment says more about learning
  than one based on clicking "mark complete".
- **The starting group.** Everyone who enrolled, everyone who opened the first lesson, or everyone who
  said they meant to finish. Free enrolments that never start will drag down a rate based on everyone.
- **The window.** Self-paced learners finish at different speeds. Pick a fixed window, such as 90 days
  from enrolment, so this month's rate is comparable with last month's.

**Example.** A bookkeeper (an invented example, but a typical one) sells a six-module course on monthly reconciliation. She defines completion as
passing the final practice reconciliation, counts everyone who opened lesson one, and uses a 60-day
window. Her rate is now a number she can track and improve, and it doesn't include the people who bought
on impulse and never logged in. She tracks that group separately, because it's a sales and onboarding
problem rather than a course design problem.

Write the definition down where you'll see it each time you check the number. If you use a platform
report, check what it counts: whether it divides by everyone enrolled or only active learners, and
whether lessons marked complete without being watched still count.

## 2. Find where learners stop

A single rate tells you that people leave. It doesn't tell you where. The research is consistent on one
point: most leave early.

In Jordan's analysis of MOOCs with weekly data, the first two weeks appeared to be critical in gaining
student engagement; after the third week, the drop from week to week was small for almost all courses
([Jordan, 2015](https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v16i3.2112)). Reich's study of nine HarvardX courses
found the same pattern regardless of what learners had said they intended: attrition was highest in the
early part of a course ([Reich, 2014](https://er.educause.edu/articles/2014/12/mooc-completion-and-retention-in-the-context-of-student-intent)).

**Example.** The bookkeeper exports lesson-by-lesson progress and sees that a third of learners never get
past lesson two, a 25-minute video on accounting principles. After that, the drop between lessons is
small. One lesson is doing most of the damage.

Look for the lesson where the curve falls most steeply, and for quiz questions most people get wrong.
Both point to a specific fix rather than a general "boost engagement" project.

## 3. Fix the start of the course

If most people leave early, the opening of a course deserves more design attention than any other part.
The research above tells us where attrition happens. It doesn't test particular fixes for the opening,
so what follows is our judgement from course design practice rather than a tested finding:

- **Let learners do something useful in the first lesson.** A short task they can use straight away
  beats a long introduction to the course and the instructor.
- **Move background theory later**, or cut it to what the first task needs. Theory makes more sense once
  learners have a problem it solves.
- **Say what's ahead.** How many modules, roughly how long each takes, and what they'll be able to do at
  the end.

**Example.** The bookkeeper replaces the 25-minute principles video with a 6-minute lesson in which
learners match ten real bank transactions, then moves the principles into short explanations at the
points where they're needed.

This also keeps the load of the first lesson manageable. Our library entry on
[cognitive load](/library/cognitive-load-theory/) covers why new learners are easily overwhelmed by
too much at once.

## 4. Keep the course as short as the goal allows

Jordan found that longer MOOCs had lower completion rates, and that courses using peer grading had lower
completion than those using automatic grading only
([Jordan, 2015](https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v16i3.2112)). Both findings are correlations across
different courses, not experiments, so a long course may differ from a short one in many other ways. But
they fit what designers see: every extra week is another chance for life to intervene.

That doesn't mean cutting what learners need. It means cutting what they don't:

- Remove material that doesn't serve an objective. If you can't name the objective a lesson supports,
  it probably belongs in a bonus resource. Our guide to
  [writing learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/) helps with that test.
- Split one long course into two shorter ones if the goals separate cleanly.
- Think hard before adding steps that depend on other learners, such as peer review, to a self-paced
  course, where there may be no one at the same point to review with.

Short lessons help too, within limits. Our entry on [microlearning](/library/microlearning/) covers what
the evidence says about how short is useful.

## 5. Use reminders and planning prompts, but expect small effects

Behavioural nudges are the most common advice on this topic, and the evidence here is the most mixed.

In a field experiment in three HarvardX courses, open-ended prompts asking learners to plan how they'd
complete the course raised completion by 29% (a relative increase) and certificate payments by 40%
([Yeomans & Reich, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1145/3027385.3027416)). That looked promising.

Then the same research group and colleagues scaled up. Across 247 courses from Harvard, MIT and Stanford,
with about a quarter of a million students over two and a half years, self-regulation interventions such
as plan-making raised early engagement but not final completion. The effects were far smaller than the
researchers had expected from earlier studies
([Kizilcec et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1921417117)).

**In practice, we'd** still send a short welcome sequence, ask learners when they plan to study, and nudge
people who stall early, because those things are cheap and help people start. But we wouldn't expect
reminder emails to rescue a course that's too long, starts badly or was sold to the wrong people.

## 6. Sell the course to the people it's for

Intent matters. Among HarvardX learners who said they intended to finish, about 22% earned a
certificate, against about 6% of those who said they meant to browse
([Reich, 2014](https://er.educause.edu/articles/2014/12/mooc-completion-and-retention-in-the-context-of-student-intent)).

<ResearchCard n={0} />

The same study warns that intentions change: many people who didn't plan to finish did, and most who
planned to finish didn't. So intent explains part of the gap, not all of it.

For a paid course, the practical lever is the sales page. Be specific about who the course is for, who it
isn't for, how much time it takes each week and what learners will be able to do at the end. Fewer
buyers who are a poor fit means fewer people who were never going to finish. Our guide to
[creating an online course that sells](/guides/how-to-create-an-online-course-that-sells/) covers the
sales page in more detail.

## 7. Measure learning, not just completion

Completion is a means to an end. A learner who clicks through every lesson and can't do the task counts as
a completion and a failure at the same time. A learner who gets what they needed from three modules and
leaves may count as a dropout and a success.

So track completion alongside two other measures:

- **Can learners do the task?** Results on a final assessment that asks them to do it. Our guide to
  [writing multiple-choice questions](/guides/writing-multiple-choice-questions/) covers how to make a
  quiz test understanding rather than recall of wording.
- **Where do they struggle?** The questions most people get wrong, and the lessons where they stop.

The evaluation phase of the [ADDIE model](/guides/addie-model/) puts these measures in a cycle, so what
you learn feeds back into the design.

## Common mistakes

1. **Comparing your rate with a benchmark that has no source.** Compare with your own last cohort, using
   the same definition.
2. **Counting everyone who enrolled in a free course.** Track never-started learners separately; they're
   an onboarding problem.
3. **Adding more content to fix low completion.** Length is associated with lower completion, not higher.
4. **Treating nudges as the main fix.** They help people start. At scale they rarely changed whether people
   finished.
5. **Optimising completion at the expense of learning.** Making it easier to click "complete" raises the
   number and lowers its meaning.

## In practice

If we were looking at a self-paced course with a disappointing completion rate, we'd spend an hour on the
definition and the lesson-by-lesson drop-off before changing anything. Most of the time the data points at
one or two early lessons, and fixing those does more than any number of reminder emails. Then we'd change
one thing at a time and compare cohorts using the same definition, so we'd know what actually helped.

For the wider set of checks that make a course work, see our
[online course design checklist](/guides/online-course-design-checklist/).

## What to do next

Write a one-line definition of completion for your course, with the rule, the starting group and the
window, then pull the lesson-by-lesson progress for your last 90 days of learners and find the single
lesson where the most people stop. That lesson is your first fix.
