Search for part-time instructional design jobs and you'll get job boards. What you won't get is much
evidence, because the research on the field mostly leaves part-time work out. The best-known recent
study of instructional design job ads filtered its search to full-time postings only
([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606)), and the largest practitioner survey
had no part-time category at all ([Peck, 2024](https://www.devlinpeck.com/content/instructional-designer-salary)).

What the survey does show is side work. Of 1,075 instructional designers who answered it in 2023,
10.5 per cent did client work on top of a full-time job, and another 10 per cent freelanced full time.
It's a self-selected online sample, mostly reached through LinkedIn and design communities, so treat
the shares as a rough picture rather than a census. But it points to how most part-time design work
actually happens: as projects, contracts and side work, more than as part-time jobs on a payroll.

This guide covers what "part-time" means in this field, the four forms the work takes, where to look
for each, and what you'll need to win it.

## What the data shows, and doesn't

<ResearchCard n={0} />

Weber, McCarthy, Campbell and Bauer searched Indeed in May 2023 for US instructional designer and
instructional technologist postings. One of their filters was full-time only, which left 130 ads
([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606)). That's a reasonable choice for a study
of what employers expect, but it means the most-cited picture of the job market says nothing about
part-time roles.

The US government's outlook for the nearest occupation is just as clear. The Bureau of Labor
Statistics says most training and development specialists "work full time during regular business
hours" ([US Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/Business-and-financial/Training-and-development-specialists.htm)).
That category is broader than instructional design, but it sets expectations: part-time employee roles
exist, and they're the exception.

<Chart id="peck-2024-id-employment-type" />

Devlin Peck's survey sorted respondents into three groups: full-time employees (79.4 per cent), people
with a full-time job who also did client work (10.5 per cent), and full-time freelancers or contractors
(10 per cent). An earlier round in 2021, with 615 valid responses, found a similar split: 75 per cent,
12.8 per cent and 12.2 per cent
([Peck, 2021](https://www.devlinpeck.com/content/instructional-designer-report-2021)). In both,
roughly one designer in five was earning at least some of their income from clients rather than an
employer.

## What "part-time" means depends on who's hiring

Before comparing ads, know that the label carries less information than it seems.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

In the US, then, a part-time instructional designer role could be 10 hours a week or 30
([US Department of Labor](https://webapps.dol.gov/elaws/faq/esa/flsa/014.htm)). In Australia,
part-time and casual are separate kinds of employment with different entitlements, and the Fair Work
Ombudsman explains the difference; check the equivalent rules wherever you work. Freelance and
contract work sits outside both: you're paid per project or per hour, and you supply your own
equipment, insurance and leave.

So read every ad for three things the title won't tell you: the weekly hours, how long the
arrangement lasts, and whether you'd be an employee or a contractor.

## The four forms of part-time instructional design work

### 1. A reduced-hours employee role

This is what most people picture: a permanent or fixed-term job at fewer than full-time hours, with
an employer's systems, colleagues and (depending on where you are) leave. Look first at employers
that need some design capacity but not a full-time designer: a university faculty with a few courses
to rebuild, a small learning and development team, a non-profit or professional association.

**How to search:** use the job titles from our guide to
[instructional design jobs](/guides/instructional-design-jobs/), not just "instructional designer":
learning designer, educational designer, eLearning developer, curriculum developer. Add "part time",
and in Australian and New Zealand universities also try fraction terms such as "0.6 FTE". Set
alerts on university careers pages directly, in case a role never reaches the big boards.

**Example:** a former teacher who wants three days a week sets alerts for "learning designer" and
"educational designer" at the four universities within commuting distance, rather than scrolling
national boards for the word "part-time".

### 2. A fixed-term or hourly contract

Contract roles fill a gap: a course build with a deadline, a platform migration, or cover while a
team member is on leave. They may be full time for a few months or a set number of hours a week.

**How to search:** add "contract" and "contractor" to the job titles, register with recruitment
agencies that place learning and development staff in your area, and approach learning consultancies
that build courses for clients and bring in designers per project. Expect to be judged
on how quickly you can start producing.

**Example:** a designer between permanent roles takes a 12-week contract converting a company's
classroom compliance training to eLearning, working 25 hours a week from home.

### 3. Freelance client work

Freelancing is running a small business: you find clients, scope and price projects, and deliver
them. It's how many designers make part-time hours work, because you choose how much to take on.

The data on how freelancers find clients is thin. In a 2022 survey of 61 freelance instructional
designers run by the eLearning software company iSpring, LinkedIn was the most-cited source of
clients and gigs (45 per cent), ahead of Upwork, professional communities on Facebook, Slack and
WhatsApp, existing contacts and referrals. Finding clients was also the most common challenge
respondents named (24 per cent)
([iSpring, 2022](https://www.ispringsolutions.com/upload/iblock/358/iuhzqxwif7d0jcn5pa6xm7w6ydgs0x91/the_field_of_freelance_instructional_design_in_figures.pdf)).
It's a small, specialised international sample from a vendor, not a picture of all freelancers, so
read it as a hint: your professional network does more of the work than marketplaces.

**Example:** a designer who left a corporate L&D team tells former colleagues and two agencies she
worked with that she's taking projects, and posts short case studies of her portfolio work on
LinkedIn. Her first two jobs come from people who already knew her work.

### 4. Side work alongside a full-time job

This is the most common form in the survey data: 10.5 per cent of Peck's 2023 respondents did client
work on top of a full-time role ([Peck, 2024](https://www.devlinpeck.com/content/instructional-designer-salary)).
It's also how many designers test freelancing before relying on it.

Read your employment contract first. Look for clauses on outside work, conflicts of interest, and
who owns what you create, and keep client work off your employer's laptop and hours. A client in the
same industry as your employer is the case most likely to cause trouble.

**Example:** a university learning designer builds one short course a year for a professional
association in an unrelated field, after checking her contract's outside-work clause with HR.

## What part-time work pays

No survey we found reports pay for part-time instructional designers, so work from hourly figures.
The median wage for US training and development specialists was $33.31 an hour (USD) in May 2025
([US Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/Business-and-financial/Training-and-development-specialists.htm)),
a reasonable floor for comparing an employee role's hourly rate.

Freelancing changes the maths. In Peck's 2023 survey, self-employed designers in the US averaged
$84,796 a year against $83,347 for full-time employees, with much more spread: worldwide, freelance
earnings ranged from $10,000 to $325,000 a year
([Peck, 2024](https://www.devlinpeck.com/content/instructional-designer-salary)). In the 2021 round,
the average reported side income was $27,449, from 53 respondents
([Peck, 2021](https://www.devlinpeck.com/content/instructional-designer-report-2021)). Remember that a
freelance rate has to cover the hours you spend finding work, quoting and invoicing, plus your own
tools, tax and leave, so it needs to sit well above an employee's hourly rate to come out even. Our
[instructional designer salary guide](/guides/instructional-designer-salary/) covers full-time pay by
sector.

## What wins part-time work

Whatever the arrangement, the people hiring want proof you can do the job, and they have less time to
train you than a full-time employer does. The hiring leaders Weber's team interviewed said much the
same about full-time roles; one put it simply: "really having done the work would be a real critical
piece" ([Weber et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.30935/cedtech/14606)).

That makes a focused portfolio the main tool:

- **Two or three projects** that match the work you want: a short compliance module for corporate
  contracts, a redesigned online unit for university roles. Our
  [instructional design portfolio examples](/guides/instructional-design-portfolio-examples/) show
  what to include.
- **Proof you can work alone.** Part-time and contract designers are often the only designer on a
  project. Show how you scoped it, worked with the subject expert and handled feedback.
- **The tools the client uses.** You won't get time to learn a new authoring tool on a short contract.
  Our guide to [instructional design tools](/guides/instructional-design-tools/) covers which ones job
  ads mention most.

When I'm looking for freelancers or part-time staff, the first thing I want to know is that you
understand the LMS we use. It sounds basic, but listing your proficiencies and showing your knowledge
of a particular area is a good way to get on the shortlist; in our case, that's the Australian
Qualifications Framework (AQF) and TEQSA standards for higher education. Beyond that, I look for
enthusiasm for designing learning that sticks, and the ability to collaborate.

If you're new to the field, part-time work can be a way in, but it rarely skips the experience
question. Our guides to [entry-level instructional design jobs](/guides/entry-level-instructional-design-jobs/)
and [instructional design internships](/guides/instructional-design-internship/) cover building that
first evidence.

<Callout>
  Before you search, decide which of the four forms you want, and write down three numbers: the weekly
  hours you can give, the hourly rate you need, and how many months you want it to last. Then read
  every ad or enquiry against those three numbers before you read anything else.
</Callout>

## What to do next

Pick one form of part-time work and set up its channel this week: alerts on three or four employers'
careers pages for a reduced-hours role, registration with two agencies for contracts, or a message to
ten former colleagues saying you're taking freelance projects. One well-chosen channel will do more
than a daily scroll through national job boards.
