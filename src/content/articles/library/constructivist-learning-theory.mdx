Constructivist learning theory is usually taught with a piece of classroom advice attached: let
learners explore, step back, be a guide on the side. Yet when researchers pooled 164 studies of
discovery learning, learners who worked things out with little or no help did worse than learners who
were taught directly, and the learners who did best were given guided tasks
([Alfieri et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017)). The theory itself, that people build their
own understanding, is well supported. The advice doesn't follow from it.

This entry separates the two. It explains what the theory says, what decades of studies on discovery
learning found, and how to design a course that takes constructivism seriously without leaving
learners to work everything out alone.

## What the research says

### Constructivism is a theory of learning

Constructivism describes how understanding forms. People don't take in new information like files
copied onto a drive. They interpret it in the light of what they already know, connect it to existing
ideas, and sometimes have to rebuild those ideas when the new information doesn't fit. Its roots are
usually traced to Jean Piaget's work on how children's thinking develops and to Lev Vygotsky's
emphasis on the social side of learning, which also inspired the idea of
[scaffolding](/library/scaffolding-in-education/).

Few learning researchers dispute this account. Richard Mayer, whose work underpins much of what we know
about multimedia learning, describes his own position as "a constructivist theory of learning in which
the learner is cognitively active"
([Mayer, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.59.1.14)). The disagreement starts when the theory is
turned into a teaching method.

### The constructivist teaching fallacy

The tempting step is from "learners build their own understanding" to "so teachers should let them
build it unaided". Mayer gave that step a name.

<ResearchCard n={0} />

His point is that the activity that matters is mental. A learner who watches a clear explanation and
then explains it back in their own words is constructing knowledge, even though nothing about the lesson
looks like discovery. A learner clicking around a simulation with no idea what to look for may be very
busy and learn little. In Mayer's words, "the kind of activity that really promotes meaningful learning
is cognitive activity"
([Mayer, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.59.1.14)).

Clark, Kirschner and Sweller make the same distinction for teachers: many educators, they write,
"confuse 'constructivism,' which is a theory of how one learns and sees the world, with a prescription
for how to teach"
([Clark, Kirschner & Sweller, 2012](https://www.aft.org/sites/default/files/GuidedInstruction.pdf)).

### Three strikes against pure discovery

Mayer reviewed three waves of research, each comparing pure discovery (learners work out the rule or
strategy themselves) with guided methods: discovery of problem-solving rules, which peaked in the 1960s;
discovery of conservation strategies in children, in the 1970s; and discovery of programming concepts
with the LOGO language, in the 1980s. In each, guided methods came out ahead. His conclusion was not to
abandon discovery but to guide it: "guided discovery appears to offer the best method for promoting
constructivist learning"
([Mayer, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.59.1.14)).

In 2006, Kirschner, Sweller and Clark went further. They grouped discovery, problem-based, inquiry,
experiential and constructivist teaching under one heading, minimally guided instruction, and argued
that for novices it is "less effective and less efficient" than guided approaches, because it ignores
the limits of working memory that [cognitive load theory](/library/cognitive-load-theory/) describes.
Guidance, they argued, "can be relaxed only with increased expertise"
([Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006](https://www.davidlewisphd.com/courses/EDD8121/readings/2006-Kirschner_et_al.pdf)),
the same pattern now known as [the expertise reversal effect](/library/expertise-reversal-effect/).

### What the meta-analysis found

The most useful single study is a pair of meta-analyses by Alfieri, Brooks, Aldrich and Tenenbaum, who
pooled 164 studies ([Alfieri et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021017)). They split discovery
learning into two kinds:

- **Unassisted discovery**, where learners work with little or no help. Across 108 studies (580
  comparisons), it did worse than explicit instruction, d = −0.38.
- **Enhanced discovery**, where learners still do the work but with support: guided tasks, scaffolding,
  or being asked to explain their reasoning. Across 56 studies (360 comparisons), it did better than
  other methods, including explicit instruction, d = 0.30.

<Chart id="alfieri-2011-discovery-learning" />

Within enhanced discovery, guided discovery had the largest advantage (d = 0.50), followed by asking
learners to explain their thinking (d = 0.36). Simply asking learners to generate rules or ideas, with
no further support, did slightly worse than the comparison methods (d = −0.15). In the unassisted
analysis, the disadvantage of discovery was larger for adolescents (d = −0.53) than for adults
(d = −0.26), though adults still did better with explicit teaching.

### The counter-argument, and where it landed

Kirschner and colleagues' paper drew sharp replies. Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and Chinn argued that
problem-based and inquiry learning had been wrongly lumped in with pure discovery: done well, they wrote,
these approaches are "highly scaffolded", with structure and expert guidance throughout
([Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520701263368)). The argument is less
a defence of minimal guidance than a claim that good inquiry teaching was never minimal. On that, both
sides largely agree: what fails is leaving novices alone with a problem, not giving them a problem.

### When struggling first helps

There's one finding that complicates a simple "explain first" rule. Research on productive failure asks
learners to attempt a problem before they're taught how to solve it, then teaches the concept, building
on their attempts. A meta-analysis of 53 studies (166 comparisons) found problem solving followed by
instruction beat instruction followed by problem solving, with a moderate effect (Hedges' g = 0.36), and
larger effects when the design closely followed productive-failure principles. The pattern reversed for
younger primary-school children and for domain-general skills
([Sinha & Kapur, 2021](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543211019105)).

The important detail is the second half. In these studies the struggle is followed by explicit teaching
that uses learners' attempts. It is a sequence, not a substitute for instruction, which fits Mayer's
argument rather than contradicting it.

### How strong is the evidence?

We rate it **strong** for the claim that matters in practice: that novices learn more from guided forms
of construction than from unguided discovery. It rests on a meta-analysis of 164 studies, decades of
experiments summarised by Mayer, and agreement on the central point from both sides of the 2006 debate.

Two limits. First, "discovery" and "guidance" cover very different methods, so pooled effects tell you
the direction, not the size you'll get in your course. Second, productive failure shows that the order
of struggle and instruction is a live question, especially for conceptual understanding with older
learners. Constructivism as a description of learning isn't something these studies test directly; it's
the shared starting point.

## How to apply it in a course

The practical version of constructivism is to make learners generate something, then make sure what they
generate is right. Chi and Wylie's ICAP framework is a useful lens. It ranks learning activities by what
learners do: passive (receiving information), active (manipulating it, such as highlighting), constructive
(producing something beyond what they were given, such as an explanation or a concept map) and interactive
(building ideas together in dialogue), and hypothesises that learning increases "more or less in the
direction of I&gt;C&gt;A&gt;P"
([Chi & Wylie, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2014.965823)).

Here's how we'd apply that in a self-paced course, using an invented example: a short course teaching
new managers how to give feedback to their team.

### 1. Explain the core idea clearly first

Start with a short, clear explanation and a demonstration: a two-minute video showing one feedback
conversation, with the structure pointed out as it happens. For novices this is the guided part of
guided discovery, and it isn't a betrayal of constructivism. It gives learners something accurate to
build on.

### 2. Follow every explanation with a constructive task

Straight after the video, ask learners to produce something:

- **Explain:** "In two sentences, why did the manager describe the behaviour before its effect?" Our entry
  on [elaborative interrogation](/library/elaborative-interrogation/) covers why prompts like this work.
- **Compare:** show a second, weaker conversation and ask what differs.
- **Predict:** before a clip of the employee's reply, ask how they'll respond and why.

Each is constructive in ICAP's sense: learners go beyond what they were shown.

### 3. Give feedback on what they build

Learners left to construct alone can build misconceptions as easily as understanding. After each task,
show a model answer and what it gets right, or use multiple-choice versions with an explanation for each
wrong option. [Effective feedback](/library/effective-feedback/) is what turns construction into accurate
understanding.

### 4. Try problem-first, with instruction straight after

For a concept rather than a procedure, try the productive-failure sequence. Before teaching the feedback
structure, give learners a scenario and ask them to draft what they'd say. Then teach the structure,
using common weak drafts as examples, and ask them to revise their own. The instruction that follows the
attempt is not optional: it's the part the research shows makes the struggle pay off.

### 5. Reduce the support as learners improve

Move from guided tasks to open ones: from choosing the better of two feedback openings, to completing a
half-written script, to writing a full conversation for a situation from their own team. That's
[scaffolding](/library/scaffolding-in-education/) in practice, and it ends where constructivism wants it
to: with learners building the whole thing themselves.

## How the platforms support it

Constructive activities need a few platform features: ways for learners to produce something, feedback on
it, and control over sequence. We haven't yet published our reviews of
<PlatformLink id="thinkific" />, <PlatformLink id="learnworlds" />, <PlatformLink id="teachable" /> and
<PlatformLink id="kajabi" />, and features change often, so check these in your platform's current
documentation, on the plan you're paying for:

- **Open-response questions.** Can learners type an explanation inside a lesson, not just pick an answer?
- **Feedback on responses.** Can a model answer appear after submission, and can quiz options each carry
  their own explanation?
- **Interactive video.** Can you pause a video for a question or prediction before revealing the next part?
- **Assignments with review.** Can an instructor or peer comment on a draft before the learner revises it?
- **Discussion.** Is there a place inside the lesson for learners to compare answers with each other?

If your platform only offers multiple choice, you can still be constructive: ask learners to write their
answer on paper or in a notes app before revealing the options.
