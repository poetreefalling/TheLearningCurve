Most online courses already use words and pictures together. There's a narrated video, a slide
deck, a diagram in the workbook. So it's easy to assume dual coding is something you're doing
by default. Usually you're doing half of it, and the other half goes wrong in one of three ways:
the picture is decoration rather than content, the script is printed on screen under it, or the
words and the picture arrive at different moments.

Dual coding is the idea that people learn better when an explanation comes in two forms at once:
words, and a picture that shows the same thing. The research behind it is decades old and
reasonably solid. What the popular versions leave out is the part that matters most when you
record a lesson: which pictures count, what to do with on-screen text, and when the words should
arrive. This entry covers what the evidence says, how to apply it to course video, and what to
check in your platform.

## What the research says

### Two systems, linked

Dual coding theory comes from the Canadian psychologist Allan Paivio. Its most useful statement for
educators is a long review he wrote with James Clark, which describes "nonverbal and verbal mental
systems that are specialized for the processing of imagery and linguistic information"
([Clark & Paivio, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01320076)). The verbal system handles language in
any form: heard, read, spoken. The non-verbal system handles images and other representations that
resemble the things they stand for.

The two systems are separate but connected. A word can call up an image, and an image can call up a
word. The educational claim follows from that connection. Clark and Paivio summarise the
explanation for imagery's effect on memory as "the additive effect of imagery and verbal codes is
better than a verbal code alone", and report that concrete phrases, sentences and paragraphs are
remembered better than abstract text ([Clark & Paivio, 1991](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01320076)).
They cite an example from reading research: children who listened to stories while looking at
relevant pictures recalled more than children who only heard them.

Notice what that claim covers. It isn't only about putting pictures on slides. Concrete language
and examples that evoke an image count too, and the review notes that learners are more likely to
form mental images when they're asked to. A well-chosen analogy in narration is a form of dual
coding, even with nothing on screen.

### From theory to course design

The person who turned dual coding into design rules for screen-based lessons is the educational
psychologist Richard Mayer. His cognitive theory of multimedia learning rests on three assumptions,
the first of which he calls dual channels: humans have "separate but interacting channels for
processing auditory/verbal information and pictorial/visual information". Mayer says he
incorporated the idea after reading Paivio's work on dual coding
([Mayer, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09842-1)). The other two assumptions are that each
channel's capacity is severely limited, and that meaningful learning depends on the learner
actively selecting, organising and integrating the material with what they already know.

From that, Mayer's group derived what he calls the multimedia principle: people learn better from
words and pictures than from words alone. In his 2024 review, Mayer summarises 15 design principles
drawn from more than 200 experiments he and his colleagues have run since 1989, with an effect
size and number of tests for each ([Mayer, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09842-1)). The
seven that are about how words and pictures should be combined are below.

Read together, they make a clear case. Adding a relevant picture to words had the largest effect of
any principle in the table. Presenting the words and the picture *at the same time* (temporal
contiguity) and *close together on screen* (spatial contiguity) both helped. Explaining a picture
with narration rather than on-screen text (modality) helped. Cutting extraneous material
(coherence) and adding cues that highlight what matters (signalling) helped.

### More text is not more coding

One row in that chart is close to zero. Mayer's redundancy principle states that people do not
learn better when printed text is added to graphics and narration, with an effect size of 0.10
across 12 tests ([Mayer, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09842-1)). That runs against a
common habit in course video: a diagram, a voice explaining it, and the script printed underneath.

An independent meta-analysis adds useful detail. Adesope and Nesbit pooled 57 studies, mostly with
post-secondary students, comparing spoken-only, written-only and spoken-plus-written presentations
([Adesope & Nesbit, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026147)). Spoken-plus-written beat spoken-only
overall, but the advantage showed up for learners with low prior knowledge, for system-paced
material, and for material *without* pictures. And presentations that showed key terms taken from
the narration did better than ones showing the full text, accounting for much of the advantage.

So the practical reading is: when there's a meaningful picture on screen, let the narration explain
it and keep on-screen words to labels and key terms. When there's no picture, or learners can't
control the pace, written support helps more.

### Dual coding is not learning styles

Dual coding is often confused with learning styles, the idea that some people are "visual
learners" and others "auditory learners", and that teaching should match each person's preference.
Weinstein, Madan and Sumeracki, in a review of six well-supported learning strategies, note that the
evidence doesn't support learning styles, which have come to be described as a myth within
psychology ([Weinstein et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-017-0087-y)). Dual coding works
the other way round.

Every learner gets both forms. Nobody gets a "visual version" of the course.

### How strong is the evidence?

Moderate to strong for the core claim, with limits worth knowing. The multimedia principle has a
large effect in Mayer's summary, and the review by Weinstein and colleagues counts dual coding among
strategies with robust support from decades of research. But most of Mayer's evidence comes from one
research programme, much of it with university students and short lessons tested soon afterwards.
The 2024 review doesn't define its effect-size column in the table itself, and Mayer stresses that
each principle has boundary conditions: for whom, for which lessons and under what circumstances it
applies.

Weinstein and colleagues also add two cautions. Each extra representation adds cognitive load and
can lead to over-saturation, so pictures must be helpful and relevant. And some popular classroom
versions, such as having students replace individual words with pictures, haven't been tested;
they say more research is needed on how dual coding should be implemented
([Weinstein et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-017-0087-y)). We'd treat the principle as
well supported and the specific techniques as worth testing in your own course.

## How to apply it in a course

The question to ask of every visual in a lesson is: does this picture show the idea, and do the
words about it arrive at the same time and in the same place? Here's how that plays out when you
build a lesson.

We'll use one example throughout: a lesson in a beginner photography course explaining how
aperture, shutter speed and ISO trade off against each other. It's an invented example, but it's
typical of material that is hard to follow in words alone.

### 1. Decide what the picture is for

Before you design a slide, write down what the picture has to show. For the photography lesson, it's
a relationship: open the aperture and you must shorten the shutter speed or lower the ISO to keep
the same exposure. A simple diagram of three linked dials shows that; a stock photo of a camera
doesn't.

Pictures that carry the idea include diagrams, labelled photos of the real thing, timelines,
flowcharts, annotated screenshots and worked examples drawn step by step. Decorative images, mood
photos and footage of the presenter talking are not a second code. They may have other uses, such
as building rapport, but don't count them as dual coding.

### 2. Narrate the picture; don't caption it

Put the explanation in your voice while the diagram is on screen. Keep on-screen text to the
labels on the diagram and one or two key terms ("aperture", "f-stop"). Resist printing your script
under the image. If you work from a script, that script becomes the transcript, not the slide.

### 3. Make the words arrive with the picture

Temporal contiguity was one of the largest effects in Mayer's table. In practice, that means the
diagram should be on screen *while* you talk about it, not on the slide before or after. Spatial
contiguity means labels sit on or next to the part they name, not in a legend at the bottom.

### 4. Build the picture as you explain it

Reveal the diagram in the order you explain it: the aperture dial first, then the shutter, then the
arrow linking them. Highlight each part as you mention it. This is signalling, and it tells learners
where to look at the moment it matters. If you record yourself drawing, as many course creators do
on a tablet, you get this for free.

### 5. Cut what doesn't carry meaning

Remove background music under explanations, animated transitions, clip art and the camera icon in
the corner of every slide. Each competes for the same limited attention the diagram needs. This is
the coherence principle, and it connects directly to what [cognitive load theory](/library/cognitive-load-theory/)
calls extraneous load.

### 6. Ask learners to make the picture

After the explanation, give learners a short task: sketch the three dials from memory and draw the
arrows showing what happens when the aperture opens. Mayer lists drawing an illustration among the
generative activities that prompt deeper processing, and it doubles as retrieval practice. A photo
of the sketch can be the assignment.

### 7. Keep it accessible

Captions and transcripts aren't optional extras: learners who are deaf or hard of hearing need them,
and many others use them. The redundancy research is about building full text *into* the lesson
design, not about removing access. Offer captions that learners can switch on, provide a transcript,
and write alt text for diagrams that describes the relationship they show, not just "diagram".

<Callout>
  Open the most important video in your course and pause it every 30 seconds. For each pause, ask
  two questions: does the picture on screen show what I'm saying right now, and is there any text
  on screen that just repeats my words? Fix the moments where the answer is no and yes.
</Callout>

Sometimes on-screen visuals are less about the learning itself and more about the function of good
user experience. We found that Canvas put quiz "Start" buttons below the fold on quizzes that
included a video, so we had to improvise and create breadcrumbs (buttons and links) to make the
journey more seamless. It's a reminder that you want
people spending their cognitive energy on the learning outcomes, not on figuring out how to use a
poorly designed course.

## How the platforms support it

Dual coding is mostly decided before content reaches a platform: in your slides, your diagrams and
your recording. Every major course platform will host a narrated video. The differences are in the
details around it. We haven't yet published our reviews of <PlatformLink id="thinkific" />,
<PlatformLink id="learnworlds" />, <PlatformLink id="teachable" /> and <PlatformLink id="kajabi" />,
and features change often, so check these in your platform's current documentation, on the plan
you're paying for:

- **Images and text in the same lesson block.** Can you place a diagram with its explanation
  directly beside or beneath it, rather than on a separate page?
- **Captions learners control.** Can you upload caption files and transcripts, and can learners
  switch captions on and off?
- **Learner uploads.** Can learners submit a photo or file, so a "sketch it from memory" task can be
  handed in and reviewed?
- **Interaction inside video.** Can you pause a video for a question or prompt at a set point,
  rather than splitting the explanation into separate files?

Whatever the platform, the biggest gains come from your own production choices: one meaningful
picture at a time, explained in your voice while it's on screen.
