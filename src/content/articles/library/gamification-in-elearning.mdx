Search for examples of gamification in eLearning and you'll find lists: a leaderboard here, a badge
there, a spinning wheel of prizes. Most of them come from companies that sell the feature. Few say
whether any of it helps people learn, and almost none say which elements help more than others.

The research has a clearer answer than the lists suggest. Gamification helps on average, by a small
to medium amount. But in the most cited meta-analysis, the version most courses reach for first,
learners competing against each other on a leaderboard, looked like the weakest design. Teams
competing against teams, and a story that runs through the course, did better. This entry sets out
what the studies found, then walks through examples you can build, each matched to the evidence, and
what to check in your course platform.

## What the research says

Gamification is "the use of game design elements in non-game contexts", the definition from
Deterding and colleagues that most researchers use, as cited by
[Sailer and Homner (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w). In a course, that means
adding things like points, badges, progress bars, leaderboards, a story or a team challenge to
lessons that would otherwise run the same way. It's different from game-based learning, where the
lesson itself is a game, such as a simulation.

### The 2020 meta-analysis

Michael Sailer and Lisa Homner pooled experiments that compared gamified instruction with the same
instruction without game elements. They looked at three kinds of outcome: cognitive (what learners
knew or could do on a test), motivational (how learners felt about the material) and behavioural
(what learners did, such as how many tasks they attempted or how well they performed a skill)
([Sailer & Homner, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)).

All three effects were positive:

- **Cognitive:** g = 0.49 across 19 experiments with 1,686 participants.
- **Motivational:** g = 0.36 across 16 experiments with 2,246 participants.
- **Behavioural:** g = 0.25 across 10 experiments with 951 participants.

<Chart id="sailer-homner-2020-gamification-outcomes" />

An effect size (g) of 0.49 means the average gamified learner scored about half a standard deviation
higher than the average learner in the plain version. That's a worthwhile gain for a design change,
but it isn't the transformation the marketing promises.

The more useful finding is what happened when the authors re-ran the analysis on only the studies
they rated as most rigorous. The cognitive effect held (g = 0.42 across nine experiments). The motivational and behavioural effects
didn't: they shrank or became too uncertain to call. So the evidence that gamification helps people
learn the content is sturdier than the evidence that it makes them feel more motivated, which is the
opposite of how it's usually sold.

### Which kinds of gamification worked better

Sailer and Homner tested two design choices across studies, and both mattered for what learners did.

**Competition with collaboration beat competition alone.** For behavioural outcomes, studies that
combined competition and collaboration did better than those with no social element. In the rigorous
subset, competition plus collaboration had a medium-sized effect on motivation, and outperformed
competition alone on behaviour.

<ResearchCard n={0} />

The authors' practical suggestion is concrete: let learners work together in teams while competing
with other teams. They also suggest a transparent badge system, including badges for helping and
collaborating with others.

**A game story helped what learners did.** Including game fiction (a fictional world or role that
runs through the course) had a small, significant effect on behavioural outcomes, though not on
cognitive or motivational ones. The authors' example is a fictional game world that stays relevant
throughout, combined with an avatar learners develop over time.

Two things the meta-analysis didn't do are worth knowing, because popular articles often claim them.
It didn't test individual elements such as leaderboards or badges on their own, so nobody can quote it
as proof that badges work. And its samples were small: the authors call this a limitation themselves,
and note that behavioural outcomes were almost always measured during the intervention, not after it.

### Newer meta-analyses agree on direction, not size

Later reviews also find positive average effects, often larger ones. Li, Ma and Shi pooled 41 studies
with more than 5,071 participants and found g = 0.822, with very high variation between studies
([Li, Ma & Shi, 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1253549)). In their analysis by setting,
fully online courses showed a smaller effect (g = 0.340, 12 samples) than blended ones (g = 0.863,
36 samples). Their tables and text disagree in a few places, so we treat their breakdowns as a hint
rather than a finding. Zeng and colleagues, looking at academic performance across 22 experimental
studies from 2008 to 2023, reported g = 0.782
([Zeng, Sun, Looi & Fan, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13471)).

The consistent message is that gamified courses tend to beat plain ones. The inconsistent sizes are a
reminder that "gamification" covers very different designs, and averaging them hides most of what
matters.

### The novelty problem

Most gamification studies are short, which raises an obvious question: does the boost last once
learners get used to the points? Rodrigues and colleagues followed 756 students in introductory
programming courses at a Brazilian university over 14 weeks, comparing a gamified version of an
online coding-exercise system with an earlier non-gamified cohort
([Rodrigues et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41239-021-00314-6)).

Overall, gamified students made 17.5% more attempts at exercises, spent 48.6% more time in the
built-in coding environment and logged in 220% more often. But the effect wasn't steady. It started
to decrease after four weeks, then rose again between six and ten weeks in, as students became
familiar with it, without returning to its starting level. At no point was it negative.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

This is one course design at one university, the groups weren't randomly assigned, and the authors
describe the U-shape as the product of an exploratory analysis. Still, it fits what many course
builders see: a spike of activity in the first fortnight, then a dip. The authors suggest studies of
less than 12 weeks are likely too short to show gamification's real impact. The same goes for your
own course: don't judge it, or abandon it, on the first month's data.

### How strong is the evidence?

Moderate. Several meta-analyses agree that gamified instruction beats non-gamified instruction on
average, and the effect on learning outcomes survives in the most rigorous studies. Against that,
the studies vary enormously, many are quasi-experiments with small samples, most are short, and
almost none isolate a single element. The evidence is good enough to say gamification can help, and
that design choices like team competition and a running story look better than a solo leaderboard.
It isn't good enough to say any particular badge or points scheme will work in your course.

## How to apply it in a course

The research points to one principle: game elements work as a wrapper around good learning
activities, not as a substitute for them. Points for watching videos reward clicking; points for
answering practice questions reward the thing that builds memory. Here are six examples of
gamification in eLearning, from simplest to most ambitious, using one invented course throughout: a
six-week online course teaching new team leaders to run one-to-one meetings.

### 1. Points for practice, not for progress

Award points for answering scenario questions at the end of each lesson, with more points for a
correct answer on a second attempt after feedback. Don't award points for opening a video or
reaching the end of a page. This attaches the game to
[retrieval practice](/library/retrieval-practice/) (recalling from memory rather than re-reading),
which has some of the strongest evidence in the learning sciences, so even if the points do nothing
on their own, learners are doing something useful to earn them.

### 2. A progress bar that shows the path

A progress bar across each module, like the one on many quiz screens, is the lightest element there
is. It tells learners where they are and how much is left, which in the terms of
[self-determination theory](/library/self-determination-theory/) speaks to their need to feel
competent.
It won't carry a course, but it's cheap and rarely does harm.

### 3. Team challenges instead of a solo leaderboard

This is the example the evidence backs most clearly. In week three, split the cohort into teams of
four. Each team earns points when its members complete the week's practice scenarios and post a
short reflection on a real one-to-one they ran. The leaderboard ranks teams, not people. Learners have
a reason to help each other, the competition is still there, and the person who's behind isn't
publicly last. That's the competition-plus-collaboration design that did best in the meta-analysis.

### 4. A story that runs through the course

Give the course a light fiction: learners are the new leader of a fictional team, and each module
introduces a team member with a problem to work through in a one-to-one. Each scenario question moves
the story on. This is game fiction in the meta-analysis's sense, and it had a small positive effect
on what learners did. Keep the story relevant to the skill: a fantasy world bolted onto a compliance
course adds reading without adding learning.

### 5. Badges for helping, not just finishing

Instead of a badge for completing each module, which learners get anyway, award badges for things
you want more of: answering another learner's question in the discussion, sharing a worked example,
finishing a stretch challenge. Make the criteria visible in advance. This follows the meta-analysis's
suggestion of a transparent badge system that includes badges for helping others. If your course
issues credentials that matter outside it, our look at
[digital badge examples](/trends/digital-badges-examples/) covers what employers and universities
require.

### 6. Plan for the dip

Expect activity to fall after the first few weeks. Rather than adding more points, change what the
game asks for: a new team challenge in week four, a story turn in module three, a harder optional
level. The novelty study suggests the effect recovers as learners settle, but there's no harm in
giving them something new to settle into. Track completion of practice and
[formative assessment](/library/formative-assessment/) scores across the whole course, not just the
first fortnight.

In my own courses, I try not to gamify so much as clarify. It's much better for learners to be clear on what they should know, where they are in their learning journey, and what they need to understand to complete a unit of learning. Awarding badges for routine habits is a distraction. It reminds me of the rideshare company Lyft, which gave riders arbitrary badges for things like riding on Fridays or early in the morning, and learned the hard way that it didn't boost how often they rode.

<Callout>
  Pick one module in your course that already has practice questions. Add points for correct answers
  and a team total, with learners grouped in threes or fours, and rank teams rather than individuals.
  Run it for a full cohort, then compare practice completion and module quiz scores with the previous
  cohort.
</Callout>

One caution runs through all six. If learners can earn points without learning, some will, and the
points will tell you they're engaged when they're only clicking. Before adding any element, ask what
behaviour it rewards, and whether that behaviour builds the skill.

## How the platforms support it

Gamification depends on a handful of platform features. We haven't yet published our reviews of
<PlatformLink id="thinkific" />, <PlatformLink id="learnworlds" />, <PlatformLink id="teachable" /> and
<PlatformLink id="kajabi" />, and gamification features change often and often sit on higher plans or
in add-ons. Check these in your platform's current documentation, on the plan you're paying for:

- **Points tied to quiz answers.** Can points be awarded for correct answers or completed
  assignments, rather than only for finishing lessons?
- **Groups or cohorts.** Can you put learners into teams and show a team total, or only individual
  scores?
- **Leaderboard options.** Can a leaderboard rank groups, be hidden, or be switched off?
- **Custom badges.** Can you set your own badge criteria, including for discussion posts or optional
  challenges, rather than only for course completion?
- **Branching or story paths.** Can a learner's answer change which scenario or lesson comes next?
- **Activity over time.** Can you see practice attempts and logins week by week, so you can spot the
  dip after the first month?

If your platform has none of this, a lot can still be done by hand: a weekly team total posted in the
community, scenario questions written as a story, and badges you award manually.
