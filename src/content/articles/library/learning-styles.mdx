Most people who build courses have met learning styles somewhere: a questionnaire in teacher
training, a "visual, auditory or kinaesthetic?" slide in an onboarding module, a client asking for
"something for the hands-on learners". The idea is simple and appealing. Each of us has a style,
and we learn best when teaching matches it.

The short answer to whether learning styles are debunked is: **the matching claim is, as far as the
evidence can show.** People do have preferences, and some people are better with pictures or words
than others. What decades of research haven't found is the thing that would matter to a course
designer: that teaching each learner in their preferred style makes them learn more.

This entry covers what the research tested, what the newest meta-analysis adds, why the idea
survives, and what to design around instead.

## What the research says

### Many models, one claim

There isn't one learning styles theory. A review for the UK's Learning and Skills Research Centre
identified 71 models of learning styles and examined 13 of the most influential in detail
([Coffield et al., 2004](https://wikicap.ulb.be/images/1/14/Coffield_Moseley_et_al_2004.pdf)).
The best known in online learning are the sensory ones: visual, auditory and kinaesthetic (VAK),
and VARK, which adds read/write.

What they share is a practical promise. Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork called it the **meshing
hypothesis**: instruction works best when its format matches the learner's preferred style
([Pashler et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x)). That's the claim worth
testing, because it's the one that would change how you build a course.

### The test the theory has to pass

Pashler and colleagues' main contribution was to spell out what evidence for matching would look
like. You classify learners by style, randomly assign learners of each style to different teaching
methods, then give everyone the same test. Matching is supported only if the results cross over:
visual learners do best with the visual method, and auditory learners do best with the auditory
method.

That design rules out the two results most often offered as evidence. "Learners liked the lesson
in their style" is about preference, not learning. "A lesson with pictures and narration beat a
lesson with narration alone" is a method that helps everyone, which says nothing about matching.

When the authors searched the literature, they found plenty of evidence that people state
preferences, but very few studies with a design that could test the meshing hypothesis, and several
well-designed ones whose results contradicted it. They also noted that many learning styles models
have never been tested this way at all, so "unsupported" is more accurate than "disproved" for
those. Their conclusion was blunt.

### Experiments since then

The clearest follow-ups come from Beth Rogowsky and colleagues, who ran the experiment Pashler
described. Their 2015 study with adults, the first to follow Pashler's design, tested whether a
visual or auditory preference predicts comprehension depending on how material is taught. Their
2020 paper repeated the design with fifth graders at a school in Pennsylvania, using a
learning styles inventory to classify students as visual or auditory, and having each student
experience both listening and reading on different days
([Rogowsky et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00164)).

In both, the results failed to show the crossover. In the 2020 study, there was no significant
relationship between auditory or visual preference and comprehension. Students classified as
visual scored higher than those classified as auditory on both the listening and the reading
measures, which is a difference between groups of learners, not a benefit of matching. The authors
describe their earlier adult experiments in the same terms: "Taken together, these experiments found
no evidence to support learning styles-based instruction."

### What learners feel versus what they learn

One reason the idea feels right is that preferences do shape experience. Knoll and colleagues gave
52 undergraduates a questionnaire on whether they think in words or pictures, then had them study
pairs of pictures and pairs of words and predict how well they would remember them
([Knoll et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjop.12214)). Learning style didn't predict recall
of either kind of material. It did predict learners' immediate judgements: self-described
verbalisers expected to remember the words better, and self-described visualisers the pictures.

<ResearchCard n={2} />

It was a small study with one group of students, but it fits the pattern. Matching can make a lesson
feel easier and more comfortable. That isn't the same as learning more from it, and course
evaluations that ask "did this suit your learning style?" are measuring the first.

### The 2024 meta-analysis

The most recent summary is a meta-analysis by Virginia Clinton-Lisell and Christine Litzinger,
titled "Is it really a neuromyth?" They searched for studies that matched instruction to modality
learning styles (visual, auditory, kinaesthetic and similar, often measured with VARK) and found 21
eligible studies with 101 effect sizes and 1,712 participants
([Clinton-Lisell & Litzinger, 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1428732)).

Matched instruction did come out ahead on average, with an effect size of g = 0.31 (95% confidence
interval 0.05 to 0.57). That's the number some summaries quote as a revival of learning styles. Read
on, though:

- **The crossover was rare.** Only 26% of learning outcome measures showed matched instruction
  helping at least two styles, the pattern the theory actually predicts. In 12 further studies
  that lacked the statistics for the main analysis, 25% showed it.
- **The studies were weak.** Five of the 21 met the What Works Clearinghouse's standards for
  research design; the rest did not. The authors also included quasi-experiments, which don't
  randomly assign learners to conditions.
- **The interval is wide.** A true effect anywhere from close to zero to moderate fits the data.

The authors' own reading is cautious.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

So the newest evidence doesn't rescue learning styles. It suggests that, under some conditions,
some matching may do a little good, and that finding those conditions would take better studies
than we have.

### Why the idea survives

Belief in matching remains common among educators. A survey of 242 teachers in the UK and the
Netherlands who were interested in the neuroscience of learning found 93% of the UK teachers and
96% of the Dutch teachers agreed that people learn better in their preferred learning style
([Dekker et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00429)). Teachers with more general
knowledge about the brain were more likely, not less, to believe common neuromyths.

A later systematic review pooled 37 studies of 15,405 educators in 18 countries. On average, 89.1%
endorsed matching instruction to learning styles, ranging from 58% to 97.6%, with no evidence that
belief had declined in recent years ([Newton & Salvi, 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2020.602451)).
The authors also note the surveys' own limits, such as convenience samples and loosely worded
questions.

Belief and use aren't the same. In a survey of 114 UK university academics, 58% believed in
matching, but 33% reported actually using learning styles in the previous 12 months
([Newton & Miah, 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00444)). Before reading a summary of the
evidence, 64% said they try to organise their teaching to accommodate learning styles; afterwards,
about 32% said they planned to.

### How strong is the evidence?

**Strong** against matching as a design rule. The best-designed experiments don't find the
crossover, the most recent meta-analysis finds only a small and infrequent benefit from low-quality
studies, and the reviews agree. Two limits are worth keeping in view. Many of the 71 models have
never been tested properly, and most experiments test short tasks such as reading comprehension or
recall rather than whole courses. In practice, we'd treat a learner's stated style as useful
information about what they enjoy, and nothing more.

## How to apply it in a course

The useful question isn't "what style is each learner?" but "what format does this content need,
and what does this learner already know?"

We'll use one invented example: a self-paced course teaching new hospitality staff to make
espresso drinks, with a client who has asked for "visual, audio and hands-on versions" of each
lesson.

### 1. Don't sort learners, and don't build parallel versions

Drop the learning styles questionnaire from onboarding, and don't build three versions of each
lesson. On the evidence, the extra versions cost a great deal of production time for a benefit that
may not exist. In the espresso course, that's a single well-made lesson per skill instead of three
weaker ones.

### 2. Choose the format the content needs

Format should follow what's being taught, for every learner. Grinding and tamping is a physical
skill, so it needs a close-up demonstration and then practice on the machine. Milk texture is
partly about sound, so the lesson needs audio of the steam wand at each stage. The menu and its
ratios are a structure, so a diagram does the work. Where words and pictures can work together,
use both: the evidence on combining narration with relevant visuals is far stronger than anything
for learning styles, and we cover it in our entry on
[dual coding](/library/dual-coding/).

### 3. Adjust for prior knowledge, not style

The individual difference that does change what works is how much learners already know. A
barista with two years' experience needs less step-by-step guidance than a first-week starter, and
detailed guidance that helps a novice can slow an expert down. Our entry on
[cognitive load theory](/library/cognitive-load-theory/) explains why. Offer a short diagnostic
quiz and let experienced learners skip ahead, rather than a styles questionnaire.

### 4. Offer access options to everyone

Captions, transcripts and downloadable recipe cards are worth providing, but for access, not
styles: for learners who are hard of hearing, working in a noisy café, on a poor connection, or
reading in a second language. They're cheap to add and help whoever uses them.

### 5. Spend the saved effort on practice

The time you don't spend on parallel versions goes further on retrieval and feedback. End each
lesson with questions that make learners recall the ratios and steps, write feedback that tells
them what to change (our entry on [effective feedback](/library/effective-feedback/) covers how),
and bring the key points back a week later, as described in our entry on
[microlearning](/library/microlearning/).

### 6. Answer the client with the evidence

When a client or manager asks for learning styles, explain what the research found and offer what
it does support: formats chosen for the content, access options for everyone, and more practice.
Most people asking for "something for the hands-on learners" want the course to involve doing,
which is a good instinct for a practical skill.

## How the platforms support it

Moving away from learning styles asks less of a platform, not more: no learner profiling, no
branching by style. What matters is whether you can put the right format in each lesson and offer
access options. We haven't yet published our reviews of <PlatformLink id="thinkific" />,
<PlatformLink id="learnworlds" />, <PlatformLink id="teachable" /> and <PlatformLink id="kajabi" />,
and features change often, so check these in your platform's current documentation, on the plan
you're paying for:

- **Mixed media in one lesson.** Can a single lesson hold video, text, images and audio together,
  or does each format need its own lesson?
- **Captions and transcripts.** Can you upload caption files or a transcript for each video, and
  can learners turn captions on in the player?
- **Downloads.** Can you attach a PDF or image to a lesson for learners to keep?
- **Skipping ahead.** Can learners who pass a diagnostic quiz skip lessons, or is every course
  locked to a fixed order?
- **Quizzes in every module.** Can you add a short quiz to any lesson, not only at the end of the
  course?

If your platform locks the order, a clear "If you already know this, go to lesson 4" note in the
lesson text does much the same job.
