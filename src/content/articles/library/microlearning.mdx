    a: "There is no evidence-based ideal length.     a: "The early evidence is positive but thin.   - q: "What is the difference between microlearning and chunking?"
    a: "Chunking, or segmenting, breaks one lesson into smaller parts that a learner works through in order, usually in one sitting. Segmenting has stronger evidence; microlearning adds the chance to spread practice out over days and weeks."

    a: "When the skill needs sustained attention or the parts only make sense together: a complex procedure, a case analysis, writing an argument, or anything where learners must connect several ideas at once. Microlearning is one of those terms everyone in online learning uses and few define the same way.
To a workplace L&D team it means two-minute compliance videos pushed to staff phones. To a
language app it means a daily five-question streak. To a course creator it often just means "make
the videos shorter".

Here is a working definition we'll use throughout: **microlearning is learning in short,
self-contained activities, each focused on one idea or skill, that a learner can complete in a few
minutes and come back to at a different time.** That last part matters more than the minutes. A
45-minute lecture cut into nine five-minute clips is a segmented lecture. It becomes microlearning
when each piece stands on its own, has its own small task, and is designed to be revisited.

This entry covers where the term came from, what the research actually supports, how to use short
lessons in a course without fragmenting it, and what to check in your platform.

## What the research says

### Where the term came from

The word took hold in the mid-2000s around a conference in Innsbruck, Austria, called
Microlearning 2005. In his introductory note to it, the educational researcher
Theo Hug described microlearning as a metaphor for a set of models of learning rather than a single
method, and said plainly that it had no fixed definition
([Hug, 2005](https://researchgate.net/profile/Theo-Hug/publication/237397162_Microlearning_A_New_Pedagogical_Challenge_Introductory_Note/links/54009baf0cf23d9765a3f648/Microlearning-A-New-Pedagogical-Challenge-Introductory-Note.pdf)).

<ResearchCard n={0} />

Instead, Hug listed the dimensions on which versions of microlearning differ: time (short effort,
measured or subjective), content (small units, narrow topics), curriculum (part of a course, a
module, or informal learning), form (fragments, episodes, "knowledge nuggets"), process (separate
or integrated activities), mediality (face to face or mediated) and learning type (repetitive,
reflective, problem-oriented and others). That list is still the most useful way to read any claim
about microlearning: ask which of these dimensions the claim is actually about.

### The working definition most people quote

The definition that shows up most often in later research comes from Ilona Buchem and Henrike
Hamelmann, writing about professional development. They describe microlearning as "short forms of
learning" made of short, fine-grained, loosely connected activities with small pieces of content,
lasting "a few seconds up to about 15 minutes", and contrast it with macrolearning, such as a
web-based training course that takes several hours
([Buchem & Hamelmann, 2010](https://researchgate.net/profile/Dickson_Adom/post/What_is_Micro_learning/attachment/5a7611ca4cde266d588883a2/AS%3A590037213278208%401517687242130/download/media23707.pdf)).
They also set design principles for the content itself, including that each unit "should be
self-contained".

The 15-minute ceiling in that definition is a description of what microlearning activities tended
to look like, not a finding about the best length. It's worth keeping that in mind, because it's
the source of most of the numbers in circulation.

Interest has grown fast since. A bibliometric review found 476 publications on microlearning in
Scopus between 2006 and 2019, rising from 2 a year to 94, with conference papers the largest share
and higher education the most common setting
([Leong et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1108/JWAM-10-2020-0044)). That review counted papers; it
didn't test whether microlearning works.

### What studies of microlearning have found

The most careful summary we found is a scoping review of microlearning in health professions
education. De Gagne and colleagues screened 3,096 records and included 17 studies with 2,228
participants across medicine, nursing, pharmacy, dentistry and allied health
([De Gagne et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.2196/13997)). The microlearning in these studies ranged
from a few seconds to 15 minutes: a 3-minute splinting video, 12-minute podcasts, short
screencasts, questions answered within 60 seconds.

The authors concluded that microlearning had a positive effect on students' knowledge and
confidence in performing procedures, retaining knowledge and studying. But two details limit how
far that goes. First, the studies didn't agree on what they were testing.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

Second, the outcomes were mostly at the shallow end. Using Kirkpatrick's four levels of evaluation,
16 of the 17 studies measured learners' reactions, 14 measured knowledge or skill, 5 measured
behaviour, and none measured results such as changes in practice or benefits to patients. The
review also noted downsides: pedagogical discomfort, unequal access to technology, and privacy
concerns. As a scoping review, it didn't formally assess the quality of the studies.

A more recent meta-analysis looked at higher education. Senadheera and colleagues included 12
studies with 1,177 students in their review, and pooled 5 of them (654 students) in a
meta-analysis comparing microlearning with "macro-learning", defined as teaching sessions longer
than 15 minutes ([Senadheera et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.4038/jmtr.v9i1.2)). Students taught
with microlearning scored higher on post-tests, with a mean difference of 12.6 (95% confidence
interval 1.2 to 23.9). The authors list the limits themselves: only two included studies were
randomised, heterogeneity was very high (I² of 98%, meaning the studies' results varied far more
than chance would explain), and there wasn't enough data to analyse practical skills.

So the direct evidence for microlearning as a category is early: generally positive, mostly small
studies, mostly short-term knowledge tests, with no shared definition of the thing being tested.

### Where the "six-minute" rule comes from

Many guides say microlearning videos should be under six minutes. That figure traces back to a
large study by Guo, Kim and Rubin, who analysed 6.9 million video-watching sessions across four edX
courses ([Guo et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2556325.2566239)). They found that median
engagement time was at most six minutes whatever the video's length, that students often stopped
less than halfway through videos longer than nine minutes, and they recommended segmenting videos
into chunks shorter than six minutes.

It's a useful finding about attention to MOOC video. But "engagement" here meant how long a session
lasted, which can't distinguish a learner paying attention from one with the video playing in
another tab. And the authors were explicit about what it doesn't show.

<ResearchCard n={2} />

Short videos are a reasonable default. They aren't evidence that shorter lessons teach more.

### The evidence underneath: spacing and retrieval

If microlearning works, the most likely reason isn't brevity. It's that short, standalone pieces
make two well-supported techniques easy to build in.

In a large review of ten study techniques, Dunlosky and colleagues gave only two a "high utility"
rating: practice testing (recalling information, as in a quiz, rather than re-reading it) and
distributed practice (spreading study over several sessions rather than cramming it into one)
([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)). The evidence for spacing is
especially deep. Cepeda and colleagues synthesised 839 assessments of distributed practice from 317
experiments, and found that the best gap between study sessions grows with how long you need to
remember the material ([Cepeda et al., 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354)).

A five-question quiz sent three days after a lesson, and again two weeks later, is microlearning.
It's also spaced retrieval practice, which is where the evidence is strong. A long lesson chopped
into short videos and watched in one sitting gets neither benefit.

There's a related, better-tested idea for what happens inside a lesson. Breaking multimedia
material into meaningful, learner-paced segments improves learning, with small to medium effects
across dozens of experiments. We cover that evidence in our entry on
[cognitive load theory](/library/cognitive-load-theory/). Segmenting is about pacing one lesson;
microlearning is about making each piece usable on its own and over time.

### How strong is the evidence?

**Emerging** for microlearning itself. The reviews are positive, but the studies are small, often
non-randomised and short-term, the reviews we found cover health professions and higher education,
and the studies don't share a definition. **Strong** for the mechanisms microlearning can carry: spaced practice and retrieval.
In practice, we'd treat microlearning as a format, not a method. It's worth using when it makes
spacing and quizzing easier, and worth questioning when it's just a way to make content shorter.

## How to apply it in a course

The question to ask is not "how short should my lessons be?" but "which parts of this course
should a learner meet once, and which should they come back to?"

We'll use one invented example throughout: an online course teaching retail supervisors how to
handle customer complaints, built as a two-hour course with six modules.

### 1. Decide what needs to be short, and what doesn't

Some content is best met in one sustained sitting: the model for handling a complaint, a worked
case where a supervisor talks a customer down, a scenario that needs several ideas at once. Leave
those as full lessons. Cutting them into fragments removes the connections learners need to see.

Other content is a single idea or a single step: the four questions to ask before offering a
refund, the wording for an apology that doesn't admit liability, the escalation rule. Those make
good standalone pieces.

### 2. Write one objective per piece

Each microlearning piece should have one thing the learner can do at the end of it, written the way
we describe in [how to write learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/). For
the complaints course: "Choose the right escalation path for a refund request over the store
limit." If you can't write a single objective for a piece, it's either two pieces or part of a
longer lesson.

### 3. Make each piece stand alone

A learner opening a piece on their phone a week later shouldn't need to remember what came before.
Give it a one-line context ("You're on the floor and a customer asks for a refund on a used item"),
the idea or step, and a task. Avoid "as we saw in the last video".

### 4. End every piece with a question

Retrieval is where the evidence is. Every piece ends with something to answer: two or three
questions, a short scenario with a choice, or a prompt to recall the four refund questions before
seeing them. This is the part most short-video courses leave out.

### 5. Schedule the return visits

This is what makes it microlearning rather than a short course. After the main lessons, schedule
short review pieces that bring back earlier material: a three-question quiz on day 2, a scenario on
day 7, a mixed review at day 21. The gaps here are illustrative, not prescribed by research; the
spacing evidence says longer retention needs longer gaps, so stretch them for material learners
must hold for months.

### 6. Keep the whole visible

Fragmented courses lose the big picture. Keep a one-page map of the course that each piece links
back to, and a final longer task that pulls the pieces together, such as handling a full complaint
scenario from first contact to resolution.

<Callout>
  Pick one module in your course. Write three questions that check its most important idea. Then
  schedule them to reach learners twice: about a week after the module, and again about three
  weeks later. That's a microlearning sequence, and it will likely do more than shortening the
  module's videos.
</Callout>

## How the platforms support it

Microlearning puts different demands on a platform from a long-form course: content on phones,
release over time, and lots of small quizzes. We haven't yet published our reviews of
<PlatformLink id="thinkific" />, <PlatformLink id="learnworlds" />, <PlatformLink id="teachable" />
and <PlatformLink id="kajabi" />, and features change often, so check these in your platform's
current documentation, on the plan you're paying for:

- **Scheduled release.** Can you release a lesson or quiz a set number of days after a learner
  enrols or completes an earlier lesson, so review pieces arrive spaced out?
- **Reminders.** Can the platform email or notify learners when a new piece is available, or do you
  need a separate email tool?
- **Phone experience.** Is there a mobile app or a mobile-friendly player, and do quizzes work well
  on a small screen?
- **Small quizzes anywhere.** Can you add a short quiz to any lesson, draw questions from a bank,
  and let learners retake it later?
- **Standalone access.** Can a learner jump straight to one piece from a link without clicking
  through the whole course?

If your platform can't schedule release, an email sequence with links to individual quiz lessons
does the same job.
