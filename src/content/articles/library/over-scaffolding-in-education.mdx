Over-scaffolding is the version of help that feels like teaching and works like a crutch. Learners
get through every practice task, the completion data looks healthy, and then the support is taken away
and they can't start. The clearest recent example comes from a field experiment with nearly a thousand
high school maths students: those given an unrestricted GPT-4 chat assistant during practice improved
their practice grades by 48%, then scored 17% lower than students who'd never had it once it was
removed ([Bastani et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)). That was one subject, one age
group and a short stretch of the curriculum. But the pattern it shows is old, and it turns up wherever
researchers compare performance with support against performance without it.

Most of what ranks for "over-scaffolding" is written for school classrooms: a teacher who
front-loads too much, or a worksheet that over-structures a discussion. This entry is for people
building self-paced and online courses, where the support is built in and nobody is watching to take
it away. It covers what over-scaffolding is, what the research on guidance, hints and AI help found,
and how to design support that hands the task over.

## What the research says

### Scaffolding is defined by its ending

Our entry on [scaffolding in education](/library/scaffolding-in-education/) sets out the standard
definition. Van de Pol, Volman and Beishuizen's review of a decade of research describes scaffolding as
"temporary support provided for the completion of a task that learners otherwise might not be able to
complete", with three common characteristics: it's adapted to the learner (contingency), it's reduced
over time (fading), and responsibility moves to the learner. In their words, "A teacher is fading when
the level and/or the amount of support is decreased over time", and "responsibility for the performance
of a task is gradually transferred to the learner"
([van de Pol et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-010-9127-6)).

Over-scaffolding is what happens when the support stays but those three features go. It takes three
common forms:

- **Too much.** More support than this learner needs, so they have nothing left to work out.
- **Too long.** Support that never fades, so the learner never practises without it.
- **Doing the thinking.** Support that supplies the very step the task was meant to practise: the
  answer, the structure, the decision.

The term itself comes mostly from classroom research. Daniel, Martin-Beltrán, Peercy and Silverman
studied peer tutoring with emergent bilingual students and found that their own carefully designed
curriculum tools had over-scaffolded the sessions. "Over-scaffolding limited students' productive and
substantive engagement", they write, and "the structures of scaffolding can overpower the processes of
scaffolding" ([Daniel et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1002/tesj.213)). It's a small case study, but the
phrase is a useful warning for course designers, whose scaffolds are almost all structure: templates,
prompts, worked steps and hint buttons fixed in place before any learner arrives.

### Performance with support isn't learning

The research that explains why over-scaffolding fools us comes from the distinction between learning
and performance. Nicholas Soderstrom and Robert Bjork reviewed decades of motor and verbal learning
studies and concluded that "performance during acquisition is a highly imperfect index of long-term
learning", and that "people often mistakenly interpret their performance during acquisition as a
reliable guide to long-term learning"
([Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615569000)).

Guidance is one of their clearest examples. They report that "research has shown that guiding learners
reduces performance errors during acquisition", but that "practicing a skill without guidance
frequently produces better learning than does being guided during acquisition". In one study they
describe, a physically guided group performed better during training but worse after a retention
interval than an unguided group. In another, a harness that restricted bowlers' movement improved their
technique in the short term but "failed to yield any long-term learning benefits".

<ResearchCard n={0} />

The same review is careful about the opposite error. Difficulties during practice help only when
learners can overcome them; "when the difficulties cannot be overcome by the learner", they "become
undesirable". So the lesson isn't "remove support". It's that support should be judged by what learners
can do after it's gone, not by how smoothly practice runs while it's there.

### When AI help does the work

Bastani and colleagues' trial is the sharpest recent test of this, because it compared two kinds of
AI help directly. Students worked through practice problems with one of three set-ups: no AI, "GPT
Base", described as "one that mimics a standard ChatGPT interface", or "GPT Tutor", "one with prompts
designed to safeguard learning" [TO VERIFY: that GPT Tutor's prompts steered students to hints rather than
full answers; this comes from secondary reporting, so check the PNAS methods]. Then everyone sat an exam
without any AI.

The results, from the study's abstract
([Bastani et al., 2025](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4895486)):

- **During practice**, AI access improved grades by 48% for GPT Base and 127% for GPT Tutor.
- **On the exam without AI**, GPT Base students scored 17% lower than students who'd had no AI.
- **For GPT Tutor**, the negative effects were "largely mitigated" by its safeguards.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

Both AI groups used the same model, GPT-4. What differed was how it was told to help: supply the
answer on request, or hold back and make students produce the next step. That is the over-scaffolding distinction in its plainest
form, and it matters for anyone adding an AI assistant to a course. The limits are real: one school
system, one subject, a small slice of the curriculum and teenage learners. We'd treat it as a strong
warning, not a measured effect you should expect in an adult course.

### Learners don't always use help well

On-demand help, the kind most online courses offer, has a second problem: learners choose when to use
it, and they don't always choose well. Aleven, Stahl, Schworm, Fischer and Wallace reviewed the research
on help in interactive learning environments and noted that "a number of recent studies report evidence
that learners are not using the help facilities of ILEs effectively", while "the literature suggests
that effective help-seeking behavior in ILEs is related to better learning outcomes"
([Aleven et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543073003277)).

The intelligent tutoring research makes the trade-off explicit. Koedinger and Aleven call it the
assistance dilemma: "Given the trade-offs between information giving and withholding, the
instructional designer faces the assistance dilemma"
([Koedinger & Aleven, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0)). Giving information keeps
learners moving and avoids floundering; their framework lists among its costs "shallow processing".
Withholding it makes learners generate the step, at the price of time and frustration. Their tutors
reveal hints one level at a time, with the last, "bottom-out" hint typically stating what the next step
should be. That last hint is where on-demand help can tip into doing the work, if a learner can reach it
before they've tried anything themselves.

<ResearchCard n={2} />

Their conclusion is honest about how far the evidence goes. There's no formula yet for how much help to
give; they call for more research to pin down the conditions under which giving or withholding
information works better.

### How strong is the evidence?

We rate it **moderate**. The direction is consistent across very different kinds of evidence:
experiments on guidance in motor and verbal learning, research on help use in tutoring systems, a
randomised trial of AI help, and classroom case studies. All point the same way: support that removes
the learner's own effort can raise performance during practice while lowering what they can do later.

Three limits hold the rating back. First, "over-scaffolding" isn't a single tested intervention; the
evidence comes from neighbouring research on guidance, help and assistance, and the term itself is
mostly used in qualitative classroom studies. Second, much of the cleanest evidence on guidance comes
from motor skills, and the strongest AI evidence is one trial with high school students. Third, the
opposite risk is real: Belland's meta-analysis, covered in our [scaffolding](/library/scaffolding-in-education/)
entry, found that computer-based scaffolding helped on average whether or not it faded. Too little
support for a genuine novice is also a design failure, as the [expertise reversal effect](/library/expertise-reversal-effect/)
and the research on [unguided discovery](/library/constructivist-learning-theory/) both show.

## How to apply it in a course

The aim isn't less support. It's support that hands the task over. Here's how we'd check and fix a
self-paced course, using one invented example: a short course teaching small-business owners to read a
profit and loss statement.

### 1. Name the thinking each task is meant to practise

For every practice task, write one line on what the learner should be working out. In the profit and
loss course, the third practice task is "decide whether the business made money, and why". Then look at
the support on that task. If the template already labels gross profit and net profit, and the hint
explains which line to compare, the support is doing the practised thinking. That's the third form of
over-scaffolding, and the easiest to miss, because the task still looks like practice.

### 2. Make hints build, and put an attempt first

Order hints from least to most help: a nudge towards the relevant line, then a question ("What's left
after expenses?"), then a worked version. Ask for an attempt before the first hint where your platform
allows it. This is the design difference between GPT Tutor and GPT Base, and it applies just as much to
hand-written hints as to AI. Our entry on [effective feedback](/library/effective-feedback/) covers what
to say after the attempt.

### 3. If you add an AI assistant, give it rules

An AI helper in a course is on-demand help with no bottom limit. Write its instructions to give hints and
questions, not answers, on practice tasks, and to ask the learner to explain their reasoning before it
confirms anything. Test it by trying to get it to just do the task. If you can, your learners will.

### 4. Write the fade down

Each scaffold needs a planned exit. In the profit and loss course: the first statement comes fully
annotated; the second has line labels only; the third is a real, unlabelled statement; the final task is
a statement from the learner's own business, with no support. One element goes at a time, so learners
always have something to hold on to.

### 5. Measure the gap

Compare scores on the last supported practice task with the first unsupported one. A small dip is
expected and healthy. A large drop means the support was carrying the learners. Near-identical scores
plus complaints about pace suggest support could go sooner. Retrieval questions without hints, spaced
through the course, give you this check cheaply; see our entry on [retrieval practice](/library/retrieval-practice/).

### 6. Leave a way back, not a permanent prop

Faded support doesn't have to vanish. A glossary or a worked example learners can return to by choice is
different from one built into every task. The test is whether the learner has to make a decision to
reach it.

## How the platforms support it

Avoiding over-scaffolding depends on being able to sequence and withdraw help, not just add it. We
haven't yet published our reviews of <PlatformLink id="thinkific" />, <PlatformLink id="learnworlds" />,
<PlatformLink id="teachable" /> and <PlatformLink id="kajabi" />, and features change often, so check
these in your platform's current documentation, on the plan you're paying for:

- **Hints that appear after an attempt.** Can a quiz question show a hint only after a wrong answer,
  rather than beside the question from the start?
- **Answer-specific feedback.** Can each wrong option carry its own explanation, so feedback points to
  the next step without giving away the answer?
- **Conditional or optional lessons.** Can you make worked examples and templates optional, or unlock a
  skip route from a short check?
- **AI assistant settings.** If the platform offers an AI tutor or helper for learners, can you set its
  instructions, or turn it off for specific lessons and assessments?
- **Question-level analytics.** Can you compare scores on supported practice with the unsupported final
  task, question by question?

If your platform can't do conditional hints, a simple version still works: put the hint in a separate,
clearly labelled lesson after the practice task, so learners attempt first and look second.
