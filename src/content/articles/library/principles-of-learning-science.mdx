Search for the principles of learning science and you'll find a lot of lists. A university
teaching centre offers seven principles. A training vendor offers six. A teacher-education group
frames it as six questions. Most of them are sensible, and almost none of them tells you the one
thing a course builder needs to know: which principles have strong evidence behind them, and which
are hopeful.

That question has been asked directly, more than once. A 2013 review by five psychologists
and education researchers rated ten common study techniques on how well their benefits held up
([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)). A 2021 meta-analysis pooled
1,619 effects from studies of the same ten techniques
([Donoghue & Hattie, 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216)). And a US government panel
graded its own recommendations for organising instruction as strong, moderate or low
([Pashler et al., 2007](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/20072004.pdf)). They used
different methods. The two reviews of study techniques put the same two at the top, and the panel
recommends both: spread practice out over time, and get learners to recall what they've learned
instead of reading it again.

This entry is the map for our research library. It sets out what those reviews found, how strong
the evidence is for each principle, how to build the best-supported ones into a course, and where
to read more.

## What the research says

### What "learning science" means here

Learning science, in the sense most lists use it, is the body of research from cognitive
psychology and education on how people take in, remember and use new knowledge. A principle earns
a place when experiments keep finding the same effect: people who practise one way remember or
perform better than people who practise another way.

The trouble with most lists is that they put principles with very different evidence side by side.
Some rest on hundreds of experiments in classrooms and labs. Others rest on a few studies, or on
intuition. A reader has no way to tell them apart. The three reviews below are useful precisely
because they try to.

### The 2013 review: two techniques rated high

Dunlosky and colleagues rated ten techniques, judging each one on whether its benefits
generalise. In their words, they asked whether the benefits held across
four categories: "learning conditions, student characteristics, materials, and criterion tasks"
([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266), p. 4). A technique that only
worked for one age group, one kind of material or one kind of test scored lower.

The verdict was blunt: practice testing and distributed practice "received high utility
assessments". Practice testing means recalling information from memory, through a quiz, flashcards
or a practice question. Distributed practice means spreading study sessions out over time rather
than cramming them together. Three techniques were rated moderate: elaborative interrogation
(asking why a fact is true), self-explanation (explaining how new information relates to what you
know, or explaining the steps of a solution) and interleaved practice (mixing different kinds of
problems in one session).

That list is uncomfortable, because the low-rated techniques are the ones learners reach for by
default. Rereading notes and highlighting a PDF feel productive. They are also what a course quietly
encourages when it ends a module with "review the slides".

### The 2021 meta-analysis: same top two, with numbers

The 2013 review was a narrative judgement by experts. Donoghue and Hattie, at the University of
Melbourne's Science of Learning Research Centre, tested it with numbers. They pooled 1,619 effects
from studies of the same ten techniques, with 169,179 unique participants, and calculated an average
effect size for each ([Donoghue & Hattie, 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216)).

<Chart id="donoghue-hattie-2021-ten-techniques" />

The authors summarise it directly: "The most effective techniques are Distributed Practice and
Practice Testing". Distributed practice averaged 0.85 and practice testing 0.74, against an overall
mean of 0.56. Underlining and summarisation came last, at 0.44 each.

Two details are worth noticing. First, every technique had a positive average effect. Rereading is
not useless; it is just among the least effective ways to spend the same time. Second, the gaps in
the middle of the table are small. Interleaving, which Dunlosky's group rated moderate, sits at
0.47, level with rereading. The authors describe the bands for high, moderate and low as arbitrary,
and the ordering of the middle techniques shouldn't be read too precisely.

### The catch: most of this is about remembering

The meta-analysis also shows where the evidence is thin.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

When Donoghue and Hattie split the studies by what they measured, the average effect was 0.60 for
surface learning and 0.26 for deep learning. It was 0.61 for near transfer (a test much like the
practice) and 0.39 for far transfer (using the knowledge in a new situation). The abstract also
reports that effects were "much greater for lower than higher ability students", and that whether
learners got feedback mattered.

So the strongest claim the evidence supports is this: spacing and testing reliably help people
remember what they've learned. That matters a great deal for a course, because learners can't apply
what they can't recall. But it is a narrower claim than "spacing and testing make people experts",
and it's the reason the other principles below still matter.

### The IES practice guide: what to do in instruction

The third review looks at teaching rather than studying. In 2007 the Institute of Education
Sciences, the research arm of the US Department of Education, convened a panel of seven researchers
and educators to recommend how teachers should organise instruction. The panel describes its seven recommendations
as its "consensus on some of the most important concrete and applicable principles", and gave each
a level of evidence ([Pashler et al., 2007](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Docs/PracticeGuide/20072004.pdf), pp. 1–2).

The two rated strong were using quizzes to re-expose students to key content, and asking deep
explanatory questions: prompts that get learners to pose and answer "why" and "how" questions about
the material. Rated moderate were spacing learning over time, interleaving worked example solutions
with problems to solve, combining graphics with verbal descriptions, and connecting abstract ideas
with concrete examples. Using pre-questions to introduce a topic, and teaching students to judge
what they have and haven't learned, were rated low.

The guide's case for spacing is plain: research shows that "delayed re-exposure to course material
often markedly increases the amount of information that students remember" (p. 5).

### Where the reviews meet

Put side by side, the three reviews and a later tutorial review by Weinstein, Madan and Sumeracki,
which picked out six strategies "that have received robust support from decades of research"
([Weinstein et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41235-017-0087-y)), converge on a short list.

| Principle | What it means in a course | Dunlosky et al. (2013) | IES guide (2007) | Donoghue & Hattie (2021) | Our entry |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spaced practice | Bring material back after days and weeks | High | Moderate | 0.85 | Coming soon |
| Retrieval practice | Quiz learners instead of telling them to review | High | Strong (quizzes to re-expose) | 0.74 | [Retrieval practice](/library/retrieval-practice/) |
| Explaining why | Ask learners why a fact is true or how a step works | Moderate (elaborative interrogation, self-explanation) | Strong (deep questions) | 0.54–0.56 | [Elaborative interrogation](/library/elaborative-interrogation/) |
| Worked examples | Show a solved problem, then a problem to try | Not rated | Moderate | Not rated | [Worked examples](/library/worked-examples/) |
| Words with pictures | Explain a diagram with narration | Low (imagery for text) | Moderate | 0.56 (imagery) | [Dual coding](/library/dual-coding/) |
| Interleaving | Mix problem types within practice | Moderate | Not covered | 0.47 | Coming soon |
| Rereading, highlighting | Reviewing the slides or notes | Low | Not covered | 0.44–0.47 | None |

The reviews don't use identical categories, so the table lines up techniques that are close rather
than the same. Dunlosky's "imagery use for text learning" asks learners to form mental pictures while
reading, which is not the same as giving them a narrated diagram, the practice the IES guide and
Richard Mayer's multimedia research support. That's one reason a single rating rarely tells the
whole story.

Weinstein and colleagues also make a point that explains why so many courses ignore all of this.
They point to an analysis of teacher-education textbooks that found few covered these
strategies, "suggesting that
these strategies are not systematically making their way into the classroom". The same gap shows up
in online course design.

Two things that appear on popular lists are missing from the table on purpose. Learning styles, the
idea that teaching should match a "visual" or "auditory" preference, lacks support; our
[learning styles entry](/library/learning-styles/) covers why. And the famous percentages of the
[learning pyramid](/library/learning-pyramid/) have no traceable study behind them.

### How strong is the evidence?

Strong for spacing and practice testing, as ways to help people remember. They are rated highest by
an expert review, a meta-analysis and a government panel, using different methods. The limits are
real: most studies measure surface learning over short intervals, many use students rather than
working adults, and the effects for deep understanding and far transfer are smaller. Moderate for
explaining why, worked examples, words with pictures and interleaving: consistent experiments, but
fewer of them, with more boundary conditions. Each has its own entry or is coming to the library,
with the detail.

## How to apply it in a course

The practical lesson from all three reviews is that the best-supported principles are about
structure, not content. Whether learners space and test is decided by how you build the course,
because left alone, most will reread. Here is how that plays out in one build.

Our example is an invented six-module self-paced course teaching small-business owners to read
their own financial statements. It's typical of material that has facts to remember (what goes on
a balance sheet) and skills to apply (spotting a cash-flow problem).

### 1. Put a quiz in every lesson, not just at the end

Each lesson ends with three to five questions that make learners recall what the lesson just
covered: "Which statement shows whether the business can pay its bills next month?" Not a summary
of the lesson, and not a "check you understood" button. Make the quiz low-stakes, show the right
answer with a line of explanation afterwards, and let learners retake it. Our
[guide to writing multiple-choice questions](/guides/writing-multiple-choice-questions/) covers how
to make questions that teach.

### 2. Bring earlier modules back on purpose

Spacing doesn't happen by accident in a linear course. Build it in: the quiz in module 3 includes two
questions from module 1, and the quiz in module 5 includes questions from modules 2 and 3. A short
review lesson at the start of each module opens with a question from two modules back, before
anything new is taught. If your platform can send emails on a schedule, a "two-minute recap quiz" a
week after a learner finishes is spacing too.

### 3. Follow every worked example with a problem

When you show how to calculate gross margin from a sample profit-and-loss statement, the next screen
is a different statement and a blank box. The IES guide's recommendation is to alternate: a worked
solution, then a problem, then another solution. For beginners, show more worked steps; as they
improve, show fewer. Our entry on the [expertise reversal effect](/library/expertise-reversal-effect/)
explains why the support should fade.

### 4. Explain the diagram while it's on screen

A balance sheet is easier to understand as a picture than as a paragraph. Show it, narrate it, and
highlight each section as you mention it. Keep on-screen text to labels. [Dual coding](/library/dual-coding/)
and [cognitive load theory](/library/cognitive-load-theory/) cover the detail.

### 5. Ask why, not just what

After a lesson on cash flow, ask: "Why can a profitable business still run out of cash?" Learners
write two or three sentences before they see a model answer. This is the deep explanatory question
the IES panel rated strong, and it pushes past the surface learning where most of the testing
evidence sits.

### 6. Replace "review the slides"

Find every place your course tells learners to reread, rewatch or review, and swap it for a task.
"Review the module before the final assessment" becomes "Take the module quiz again. Anything you
miss, rewatch that lesson." Same time, a better technique.

  Open your course outline and count two things: how many lessons end with learners recalling
  something, and how many modules bring back material from earlier ones. If either number is

</Callout>

This is design work for the planning stage. Our [ADDIE model guide](/guides/addie-model/) shows where
it fits, and the [online course design checklist](/guides/online-course-design-checklist/) turns it
into a module-by-module check.

## How the platforms support it

Most course platforms will host a quiz. The differences that matter for learning science are in the
detail: whether you can place practice throughout a course, bring material back later, and see
what learners get wrong. We haven't yet published our reviews of <PlatformLink id="thinkific" />,
<PlatformLink id="learnworlds" />, <PlatformLink id="teachable" /> and <PlatformLink id="kajabi" />,
and features change often, so check these in your platform's current documentation, on the plan
you're paying for:

- **Quizzes inside lessons.** Can a quiz sit at the end of any lesson, or only as a separate
  lesson or a final exam?
- **Question banks and randomisation.** Can you draw questions from a pool, so a retake isn't the
  same five questions in the same order?
- **Retakes and feedback.** Can learners retake a quiz, and can you show an explanation with each
  answer?
- **Scheduled follow-up.** Can the platform send a learner an email or a recap a set number of days
  after they finish a module, so review happens after a gap?
- **Per-question results.** Can you see which questions most learners get wrong, so you know what
  to bring back?

Whatever platform you use, the biggest gain comes from your own design: a quiz in every lesson, and
earlier material on purpose in later modules.
