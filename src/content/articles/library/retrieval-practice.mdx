Retrieval grids spread through school staffrooms because they make two good ideas easy to use at
once. A single sheet asks learners to recall material from last lesson, last week and last month,
and often gives more points for the older questions. Both ideas underneath it, retrieval practice
and spaced review, are among the best-supported findings in learning research. The grid itself, as
far as we can find, has never been tested.

That gap matters more than it sounds. The pages that rank for "retrieval practice grid" are mostly
templates and classroom how-tos: colour-coded tables for a five-minute lesson starter, marked by
cold-calling and a show of hands. They rarely separate what the research supports from what is a
teacher's design choice, and none of them deals with a self-paced online course, where there's no
teacher to read out the answers and no class to keep the routine going. This entry covers what the
evidence says, what the grid adds, and how to build one into a course that learners work through
alone.

## What the research says

### What retrieval practice is

Retrieval practice means recalling information from memory rather than re-reading or re-watching
it. A short quiz, a blank page on which learners write what they remember, a flashcard answered
before it's turned over: all of these ask the learner to pull the information back out. The
finding behind it is often called the testing effect, because the first studies used tests, but
the tests don't need to be graded or even marked by anyone else.

### The classic study, and why the benefit is easy to miss

The study most often cited is by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke
([Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)). In their first
experiment, 120 university students read short prose passages. They then either read each passage
again or took one free-recall test on it, writing down what they could remember, with no feedback.
A final test came five minutes, two days or one week later.

<Chart id="roediger-karpicke-2006-retention-interval" />

At five minutes, re-reading won: 81% of the passage's ideas recalled against 75%. After two days
the tested passages were ahead, 68% against 54%, and after a week the gap held at 56% against 42%.
The authors' conclusion is the one every summary of retrieval practice repeats: "Testing is a
powerful means of improving learning, not just assessing it."

The detail that matters for course design is the first result. If you judge a lesson by how well
learners do straight after it, re-reading looks better. In the second experiment, students who
studied a passage repeatedly were more confident they'd remember it in a week than students who
were tested, and they actually did worst. Learners, and the people who design for them, can't
reliably feel which method is working.

### Does it hold up in real classrooms?

Laboratory studies with university students and short passages are a long way from a twelve-week
course. The strongest answer to that worry is a meta-analysis by Chunliang Yang and colleagues that
pooled only classroom studies: 222 independent studies with data from 48,478 students, from
elementary school to university ([Yang et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309)).

<ResearchCard n={0} />

A g of about 0.5 is a medium effect, and it's an average across real courses with real
assessments, not a best case. The same review found that the benefit depended on how the quizzes
were run. Four of its moderators translate directly into course design:

- **Feedback.** Quizzes followed by corrective feedback had a larger effect (g = 0.537) than
  quizzes without it (g = 0.374).
- **Timing.** Quizzes given after teaching were far more effective (g = 0.536) than quizzes given
  before it (g = 0.186).
- **Format.** Quizzes helped more when their format matched the final assessment (g = 0.531) than
  when it didn't (g = 0.399).
- **Repetition.** The more occasions on which content was quizzed, the larger the gains.

### Spacing it out

The second ingredient in a grid is spacing: returning to material after a gap rather than
practising it all in one sitting. Antoine Latimier, Hugo Peyre and Franck Ramus pooled 29 studies
that compared spaced and massed retrieval practice
([Latimier et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-020-09572-8)). Spacing the recall out had
a large benefit, with an average effect of g = 0.74 across 39 effect sizes.

They also tested a popular rule: that the gaps between reviews should get longer each time
(an expanding schedule, the logic behind many flashcard apps). Across 54 effect sizes they found no
significant difference between expanding and even gaps.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

For a course designer, that's good news. You don't need an algorithm. Returning to earlier
material on a regular rhythm, such as at the start of each module, captures most of what the
research supports.

### What the grid adds, and what's untested

Pooja Agarwal's write-up for RetrievalPractice.org credits the retrieval grid to Kate Jones, a
teacher and writer, and describes it as "simply a table with prompts for retrieval practice", with
each box colour-coded by when the topic was first taught: the last lesson, last week, two weeks
ago, and so on ([Agarwal, 2018](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/strategies/2018/9/28/retrieval-grids)).
Students answer in any order, some grids give points for how far back they can recall, and
afterwards students can check their own work or get feedback from the teacher.

What the grid adds is packaging. It makes spaced retrieval a routine, and it makes the age of each
question visible, so older material doesn't quietly drop out of review. Those are useful things.
But no published study we could find has compared a grid with the same questions in a plain list,
or tested whether the points help. Andrew Watson, writing for Learning and the Brain, put it
plainly: "I don't know of any research on this subject"
([Watson, 2020](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/?p=5363)).

Watson raises one specific concern: asking learners to recall facts about a topic and keep a
running points total at the same time loads working memory with two unrelated tasks. His
suggested fixes are simple: check whether learners actually struggle, set a number of questions per
row instead of a points target, or let them note the points as they go. That's a reasoned
argument from [cognitive load](/library/cognitive-load-theory/) research, not a tested finding,
but it's a useful warning against decorating a grid until the scoring is the hard part.

### How strong is the evidence?

Strong, for retrieval practice and for spacing. Both rest on large meta-analyses, and the classroom
review shows the effect holds in real courses at every level of education. The effects vary with
feedback, timing and format, so the design matters. The grid format is a different matter: it's a
sensible way to package the two strategies, but it has no evidence of its own, so its layout,
colours and points are choices to make for your learners, not rules to copy.

## How to apply it in a course

A paper grid works in a classroom because a teacher puts it on the board, keeps time and reads out
the answers. A self-paced course has none of that, so the grid needs translating. The version we'd
build is a short review set at the start of each module.

We'll use one example throughout: an online course for new team leaders, with modules on running
one-to-ones, giving feedback, delegating and handling conflict. It's an invented example, but it
has what most professional courses have: ideas learners must still recall, and use, weeks after
the module that taught them.

### 1. Open each module with a review set

Place the review at the start of the module, before the new material. It catches what learners
have forgotten since last time, and it sits after the teaching it reviews, which is where Yang and
colleagues found quizzes worked best. Five to eight questions is enough; it should take a few
minutes, not become a module of its own.

### 2. Build the rows from earlier modules

This is the grid's real contribution: questions from several points in the past, not just the last
lesson. For the start of module 4 in the team leader course, the set might look like this.

| Last module (delegating) | Two modules back (feedback) | Earlier (one-to-ones) |
|---|---|---|
| What are the three things to agree before you hand over a task? | What's the difference between feedback on the person and feedback on the task? | What should the team member, not you, bring to a one-to-one? |
| Name one sign you've delegated the task but kept the decision. | Why does the course suggest giving feedback soon after the event? | How often did the course suggest holding one-to-ones, and why? |
| A team member says the task is "nearly done" for the third week. What do you ask? | Rewrite this into specific feedback: "Great job on the report." | |

Rotate the older questions each time, so module 5's set drops some module 1 questions and picks up
others. That keeps every topic coming back on a regular rhythm, which is the spacing the research
supports. You don't need expanding intervals.

If you'd rather plan this in a spreadsheet, our free [Retrieval Practice Kit](/kit/) does it for you.
It's one of the templates in The Evidence-Based Course Design Kit: a Google Sheet where you list the
ideas that matter in each module, then mark where learners will recall each one again, such as a
quick quiz two modules later or a scenario question at the end. The grid shows at a glance which
ideas you only ever test once.

### 3. Ask for recall, in the format learners will need

Short typed answers, or a prompt to answer in your head before revealing the answer, ask more of
memory than picking from a list. But match the format to the end point: the classroom
meta-analysis found larger effects when quizzes matched the final assessment. If the course ends
with scenario questions, make some review questions scenarios, like the third question in the
delegating column. Our guide to [Bloom's taxonomy examples](/guides/blooms-taxonomy-examples/)
covers why fact-only quizzes don't prepare learners to apply ideas, and our guide to
[writing multiple-choice questions](/guides/writing-multiple-choice-questions/) covers how to make
a choice question test reasoning rather than recognition.

### 4. Show the answer straight after each question

In a classroom, the teacher marks the grid. In a self-paced course, the course has to. After each
answer, show the correct one with a sentence on why, and let learners judge their own attempt.
Corrective feedback was linked to a larger benefit in the classroom review, and our entry on
[effective feedback](/library/effective-feedback/) covers what makes an explanation more useful
than a tick.

### 5. Keep it ungraded and drop the points

The review is for learning, not grading, so make it low-stakes: unlimited attempts, no effect on
completion, no score that unlocks anything. Skip the points-for-older-questions scheme. In a
classroom it adds a game; on a screen it mostly adds a sum, which is Watson's working-memory
concern. If you want older material to feel worth the effort, say so in one line at the top
instead: "The questions from earlier modules are the ones you're most likely to have forgotten."

### 6. Tell learners why it feels hard

Roediger and Karpicke's students who re-read felt more confident and did worse. Your learners will
feel the same pull towards re-watching the video. A sentence at the start of the first review set
helps: "You'll forget some of these. That's expected, and trying to remember is what makes it
stick."

  Take the module you're building next. Write five questions for the start of it: two from the

## How the platforms support it

A self-paced retrieval grid needs a few things from a platform: somewhere to put a short practice
set inside a module, a way to show the answer after each question, and a way to keep it out of the
grade. We haven't yet published our reviews of <PlatformLink id="thinkific" />,
<PlatformLink id="learnworlds" />, <PlatformLink id="teachable" /> and <PlatformLink id="kajabi" />,
and features change often, so check these in your platform's current documentation, on the plan
you're paying for:

- **Ungraded quizzes anywhere in a module.** Can you place a quiz at the start of a module, not
  only at the end, and set it so it doesn't count towards completion or a certificate?
- **Feedback per question.** Can you show an explanation after each answer, rather than only a
  score at the end?
- **Short-answer questions.** Is there a free-text question type, and can you show a model answer
  after learners submit, even if it isn't auto-marked?
- **Question banks across modules.** Can a quiz draw questions at random from banks tied to earlier
  modules, so each review set mixes old and recent material without you rebuilding it each time?
- **Unlimited attempts.** Can learners retake the set as often as they like?

If your platform can't mix questions from earlier modules automatically, build each review set by
hand. It's a few minutes per module, and the questions can come straight from the ones you wrote
for earlier quizzes.
