Most advice on worked examples ends with the same tip: ask learners to explain each step, so they
don't just read the solution. It sounds like good learning science. In mathematics, at least, the
largest recent review points the other way. A 2023 meta-analysis of 55 studies found that worked
examples gave learners a medium-sized boost over solving problems unaided, and that studies which
added self-explanation prompts showed a *smaller* benefit than those that didn't
([Barbieri et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1)).

That doesn't make explanation useless, and the studies were all in maths. But it does change how
we'd build a lesson around a solved problem. This entry sets out what the research says about
worked examples, where the self-explanation finding fits with older evidence, then shows how to use
examples in an online course and what to check in your course platform.

## What the research says

### What a worked example is

A worked example is a problem shown with its full solution: the starting point, each step and the
answer, usually with a line on why each step is taken. Learners study it, then solve similar
problems themselves. In a course, that might be a solved spreadsheet formula, an annotated block of
code, a finished quote for a job, or a narrated walkthrough of a calculation.

The idea comes from [cognitive load theory](/library/cognitive-load-theory/), the account of how a
small, forgetful working memory limits what a learner can handle at once. Its early experiments, by
John Sweller and Graham Cooper in the mid-1980s, compared studying examples with practising
problems. Atkinson, Derry, Renkl and Wortham's review summarises the conclusion: "traditional,
practice-based problem solving was less than an ideal method for improving problem-solving
performance". Students left to practise tended to fall back on trial and error, while those who
studied examples first more often used efficient strategies
([Atkinson et al., 2000](https://assess.ucr.edu/media/741/download), p. 183).

### Why they help beginners, and when they don't

A novice solving a problem has to work out what to do and do it, at the same time, with no stored
method to lean on. A worked example takes away the search, so working memory can go on
understanding the method instead.

That benefit depends on how hard the material is in a specific sense. Cognitive load researchers
call it *element interactivity*: how many pieces of information a learner has to hold and relate at
once. Chen, Kalyuga and Sweller tested this in two experiments. With novices, they found "a
worked example effect obtained for materials high in element interactivity" and "a generation
effect for materials low in element interactivity", meaning that for simpler material, having
learners produce the answers worked better than showing them. With more knowledgeable learners,
generating answers worked better for all the materials
([Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/edu0000018)).

The second half of that result is the [expertise reversal effect](/library/expertise-reversal-effect/):
support that helps beginners can stop helping, or get in the way, once learners know the method.
Worked examples are a tool for the start of learning a skill, not for the whole course.

### How big the worked example effect is

The best single estimate comes from mathematics. Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste and Chawla
screened 8,033 abstracts and kept 43 articles reporting 55 studies and 181 effect sizes. They found
that "the average effect size of worked examples on mathematics performance outcomes was medium
with g = 0.48 and p = 0.01" ([Barbieri et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1)).
(Hedges' g is a standardised difference between groups; 0.48 means learners who studied examples
scored about half a standard deviation higher than comparison groups.)

Three of their moderator findings matter for course design:

- **Timing didn't matter much.** Worked examples had "a medium effect on mathematics outcomes
  whether used for practice or initial skill acquisition".
- **Correct examples beat incorrect ones.** "Correct examples alone yielded larger effect sizes than
  those that used incorrect examples", whether incorrect examples were used alone or mixed with
  correct ones.
- **Self-explanation prompts went the wrong way.** This is the surprise.

### The self-explanation puzzle

The finding cuts against two decades of advice, and against older evidence. Atkinson and colleagues'
review found that "students who self-explain tend to outperform students who do not", and that
training learners to self-explain had a very strong effect on how much they did it. The same review
also found that "most learners self-explain in a passive or superficial way"
([Atkinson et al., 2000](https://assess.ucr.edu/media/741/download), pp. 197, 200, 201). Alexander
Renkl, one of the field's leading researchers, still advises that "it is advisable to foster the
learners' self-explanations" ([Renkl, 2023](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-using-worked-examples-for-ill-structured-learning-content-renkl.pdf), p. 210).

So learners who explain examples well learn more, but prompting them to do so didn't add to the
benefit in the maths studies, and was linked to less of it. Barbieri and colleagues conclude that
"pairing examples with self-explanation prompts may not be a fruitful design modification". The
abstract doesn't settle why, and a moderator in a meta-analysis is a correlation across studies,
not a controlled test.

One clue is in Renkl's own chapter. He describes a study in which examples with gaps for learners to
fill in, combined with self-explanation prompts, were detrimental: "Such gaps should therefore be
avoided" (p. 215). In practice, we read the two findings together as a warning against stacking
demands on beginners. A worked example works by taking load away. Every prompt, gap or extra task
puts some back.

### Show the example first, then the problem

The order of example and practice matters. Van Gog, Kester and Paas taught secondary students who
were novices to troubleshoot electrical circuits under four conditions: worked examples only,
example-problem pairs, problem-example pairs and problem solving only. As predicted, worked examples
and example-problem pairs gave lower cognitive load during learning and better learning outcomes
than problem solving. "Results showed no differences between WE and WE-PS or between PS-WE and PS":
showing the example after the problem did no better than no example at all
([van Gog, Kester & Paas, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2010.10.004)). Their conclusion:
"example-problem pairs should be used rather than problem-example pairs."

That fits the older advice on pairing.

The same review recommends giving at least two examples of each type of problem (pp. 191–192).

### Beyond maths and physics

Most worked example research uses well-structured problems with one right answer. Renkl's 2023
review gathers studies on less structured content: mathematical proof and argumentation, legal
cases, two-sided argument, and teachers' classroom decisions. His advice changes in three ways for
this kind of content. Teach the principles briefly first, because "learners should first study
worked examples to reach an understanding of underlying principles" (p. 209). Use modelling
examples, where someone is shown thinking aloud as they apply the principles. And don't assume video
is better: "In most cases, written worked examples may suffice or even be preferable" (p. 218).
He also warns that video's information is transient and can overload learners (p. 219).

### How strong is the evidence?

Moderate. The core effect is backed by four decades of experiments and a recent meta-analysis, and
the direction is consistent. The limits: the meta-analysis covers mathematics only; most studies use
school and university students rather than working adults; most measure performance soon after
learning; and the benefit shrinks as learners gain expertise. The self-explanation finding is a
moderator result from one meta-analysis, so treat it as a strong reason for caution, not a rule
against ever asking learners to explain.

## How to apply it in a course

Here's how we'd build worked examples into a self-paced course, using an invented example: a short
course teaching tradespeople who are starting their own business to price a job, covering materials,
labour hours, overheads and margin, ending in a written quote.

### 1. Check the task is worth an example

Use worked examples where a learner has to coordinate several steps that depend on each other.
Pricing a job qualifies: the labour estimate changes the overhead share, which changes the margin.
Memorising the definition of "overheads" doesn't. For single facts and terms, quiz learners instead;
our entry on [retrieval practice](/library/retrieval-practice/) covers how.

### 2. Build one clean, correct example

Lesson one shows a complete quote for a bathroom tile job. Break it into labelled stages (materials,
labour, overheads, margin, total) so the structure of the method is visible, and put each short
explanation next to the line it explains, not in a paragraph below. Atkinson's review found that
splitting learners' attention between a diagram and separate text held learning back (p. 187).
Keep it correct: in the meta-analysis, correct examples did best.

### 3. Put a matched problem straight after it

The next screen is a different job, a deck repair, with the same stages and blank boxes. Not three
lessons later and not at the end of the module. If you show a problem first and the solution after,
you lose the advantage.

### 4. Give two examples of each type, with different settings

Learners who only see bathroom jobs may learn "bathrooms" rather than "pricing". The second example
is an electrical job with different materials and hours, priced with the same five stages.

### 5. Choose text or video on purpose

A written example with a clear layout lets learners scan back to the step they missed. If you film
a narrated walkthrough, keep it short, show the whole solution on screen, and give learners a
written copy to work from while they practise. Our guide on [how long course videos should be](/guides/how-long-should-course-videos-be/)
covers pacing.

### 6. Go easy on the prompts

You don't need an "explain this step" box under every line. If you want learners to think about the
method, add one focused question after the example about the principle that matters most, such as
"Why is the overhead share calculated after the labour hours?", and show a model answer. Don't
combine it with gaps for learners to fill in. If you want learners to explain their thinking,
[elaborative interrogation](/library/elaborative-interrogation/) suits facts and concepts better than
step-by-step procedures.

### 7. Fade the examples as learners improve

By the third lesson, show partly worked quotes with the last stage left for the learner, then the
last two, then a job from scratch. Learners who already price jobs can skip straight to the
practice; our [scaffolding](/library/scaffolding-in-education/) entry covers fading in more detail.

## How the platforms support it

Worked examples need little technology, but a few features make them easier to build and use well.
We haven't yet published our reviews of <PlatformLink id="thinkific" />,
<PlatformLink id="learnworlds" />, <PlatformLink id="teachable" /> and <PlatformLink id="kajabi" />,
and features change often, so check these in your platform's current documentation, on the plan
you're paying for:

- **Rich lesson layouts.** Can a text lesson hold images, tables, code blocks or formulas beside the
  explanation, so the example doesn't have to be a screenshot?
- **A quiz or exercise straight after a lesson.** Can you place a practice question immediately
  after the example, rather than only at the end of a module?
- **Feedback on answers.** Can a question show a worked solution or a model answer once the learner
  has tried it?
- **Downloadable attachments.** Can learners download a written copy of a video walkthrough to work
  from while they practise?
- **Optional or skippable lessons.** Can experienced learners skip an example lesson without
  breaking their progress or their certificate?

If your platform can only place quizzes at the end of a module, a simple fix still works: put the
matched problem in the same lesson as the example, as a task with the answer revealed below it.
