The newest AI tools for instructional design don't just suggest text. They build. Between 29 September and the end of October 2026, Articulate,
Docebo, D2L and Instructure released or extended AI agents that turn documents into lessons, update
courses in bulk and act across a platform on request. In the same weeks, two economists at King's
Business School posted the most useful study we've seen on what happens when AI-made materials reach
real students. The materials helped, mostly the students who were struggling. But they were checked
by an expert first, and the checking, not the generating, took most of the time
([Dang & Nguyen, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07097)).

That's one course at one UK university, and it isn't a randomised trial. It is still a better guide to
what these new tools ask of designers than any launch keynote.

## The study: verified, not generated

Canh Thien Dang and An Nguyen gave half of a compulsory first-year Principles of Economics course (the
macroeconomics half) AI-generated podcasts, FAQs and quiz-based study guides, released weekly. The
other half got nothing comparable, and so did both halves the year before. They compared exam marks
across the two cohorts, 85 students in each, using a difference-in-differences design. Before release,
a named graduate teaching assistant checked every output against the source materials.

Three details matter more than the headline number. First, the gain came from the bottom of the
class.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

Only the 10th-percentile estimate was clearly different from zero, and
the share of marks below the upper-second boundary fell by 24.7 percentage points against the
comparison. Second, the treated half didn't improve so much as hold steady: its marks rose by 0.73
(not statistically significant) in a year when the untreated half fell by 1.61, so the authors
describe the result as protection from a decline. Third, the materials were cheap to make and not
cheap to trust. "Producing and verifying each weekly set took about 30 minutes, most of it spent
checking rather than generating," the authors write, about 2.5 hours across five weeks
([Dang & Nguyen, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07097)).

The limits are real, and the authors state them. The two cohorts shared no students, so differences in
ability can't be ruled out. Dropping the three lowest earlier marks shrinks the average effect to 1.88
marks and it is no longer statistically significant, though the drop in low marks holds. Usage logs
weren't kept for individual students. Call it early evidence: promising, from one course, and about
verified materials, not raw output. The limitations are real, and the authors are explicit about them. The two cohorts shared no students, so differences in ability between cohorts cannot be ruled out. When the three lowest-scoring students from the earlier, pre-intervention cohort are removed, the estimated average effect falls from 2.34 to 1.88 marks. It is no longer statistically significant using heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors, although it remains significant under student-clustered errors. The reduction in marks below the upper-second classification boundary, however, holds: the estimated effect remains a 22.9 percentage-point reduction (p = 0.002). Individual usage logs were not retained, so the researchers could establish which resources students accessed but not link individual usage to individual examination outcomes.

Call it early evidence: promising, drawn from one course and two cohorts at one institution, and concerning a package of source-grounded, tutor-verified AI materials rather than unverified AI output in general. The study does not isolate the contribution of verification from the contribution of the additional learning materials themselves.

## Why it matters for people who build courses

The vendors' own announcements already concede the point the study makes. D2L requires approval before
its assistant acts. Articulate's bulk updater offers review and undo. Docebo's translations are
"review before publishing". None of them claims its output is ready for learners unchecked. Designers
seem to know it: in Synthesia's survey of 421 L&D professionals, fielded in October and November 2025,
52% named accuracy as an obstacle to using AI. That is a vendor's self-selected sample, which the
report says probably over-represents early adopters
([Synthesia, 2026](https://www.synthesia.io/reports/ai-in-learning-and-development-report-2026)).

So the agents shift where the hours go. Building a lesson from a policy document used to be the slow
part; now the slow part is confirming the lesson says what the policy says, that the scenario matches
what staff face, and that the quiz tests the objective instead of the easiest fact to quote. That is
the instructional design work, and it's the part a "5X faster" claim leaves out. Bulk updating raises
the stakes: one wrong instruction applied across a hundred courses is a hundred errors.

Within a university setting, all academic decisions are made by the lecturers, but AI is used to research and outline teaching resources. AI has also proved useful in helping us find gaps in course exercises and suggesting additional readings and resources.

## What we'd do

- **Budget for checking, not just for building.** If an agent saves a day of building, plan for part of
  that day to go on review against the source document and the objectives.
- **Name the checker.** Dang and Nguyen named one trained tutor responsible for every output. Decide who
  signs off before you switch an agent on, and keep a note of what they changed.
- **Check questions hardest.** The studies we reviewed when AI course creation reached the course platforms found AI-written
  questions lean easy and factual. Our guide to
  [writing multiple-choice questions](/guides/writing-multiple-choice-questions/) has the test to apply,
  and [retrieval practice](/library/retrieval-practice/) explains why the questions carry so much weight.
- **Pilot bulk changes.** Apply an AI-described update to a handful of courses, review them, then roll it
  out. The same logic as [piloting a course before launch](/guides/how-to-pilot-an-online-course/).
- **Keep the toolkit small.** Our [guide to instructional design tools](/guides/instructional-design-tools/)
  sorts what you need by job; the new agents sit inside tools you may already pay for.
