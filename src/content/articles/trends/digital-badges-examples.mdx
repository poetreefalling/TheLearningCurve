The most talked-about digital badge of the past month has no exam. On 28 October 2026, Microsoft made
its Pro Badge for GitHub Copilot generally available: developers opt in, and the badge is awarded when
their everyday use of the tool reaches the "proficient" level
([Davis, 2026](https://howtoai.com/microsoft-pro-badge-github-copilot/);
[Sharwood, 2026](https://www.theregister.com/software/2026/11/05/microsoft-admits-certifications-can-be-a-drag-launches-creds-that-recognize-skills/5307725)).
In the same few weeks, two US universities launched badges that ask for much more traditional proof:
an A in every linked course, or research skills assessed by trained faculty. And Credly passed 150
million credentials issued. Put side by side, these examples show the one thing every badge designer
has to decide: what the learner must do to earn it.

## Three new badges, three kinds of proof

**Microsoft: proof from use.** The Pro Badge works alongside Microsoft's certifications rather than
replacing them. Microsoft's proficiency framework has five levels, from beginner to expert, and "To earn
a Pro Badge, users must reach the proficient level", The Register reports. Microsoft describes it as "a
current, verified signal of what you can do, at no cost and without a separate exam or study guide"
([Sharwood, 2026](https://www.theregister.com/software/2026/11/05/microsoft-admits-certifications-can-be-a-drag-launches-creds-that-recognize-skills/5307725)).
The level is worked out from signals across GitHub.com, the code editor, the command line and mobile.
I Programmer notes that "Participation is entirely optional and results remain private by default", and
that proficient means "consistent hands-on usage across multiple repositories and extended periods"
([Gee, 2026](https://www.i-programmer.info/news/150-training-a-education/19269-microsoft-introduces-pro-badges.html)).
The exam-based GitHub Copilot certification still exists, at 99 USD
([Davis, 2026](https://howtoai.com/microsoft-pro-badge-github-copilot/)).

**East Texas A&M: proof from grades plus practice.** On 28 September 2026, the university's Department
of Engineering and Technology launched badges in construction, electrical and industrial engineering,
technology management, and AI management for graduate students. To earn one, a student needs an A in
each designated course and must "participate in an approved discipline-related activity, such as
research, a conference, seminar or workshop". The department head, Burchan Aydin, put it plainly:
"Students must earn A's in all designated courses, but strong grades alone are not enough"
([East Texas A&M, 2026](https://www.etamu.edu/news/engineering-and-technology-launches-digital-badges-to-showcase-student-skills/)).

**San Antonio College: proof judged by trained assessors.** On 9 October 2026, the college announced an
Undergraduate Research Skills Badge built on four competencies: framing, proposing, realising and
disseminating research. Faculty complete specialised training first, after which they are "qualified to
award the badge to students who successfully demonstrate the required research competencies". Seven
faculty form the first cohort, and 15 from St. Philip's College join the second
([San Antonio College, 2026](https://www.alamo.edu/sac/news-events/news/2026/october/digital-badge/)).

## The scale behind it

Badges are no longer a niche. On 19 November 2026, Pearson said its Credly platform had issued 150
million digital credentials, the most recent 50 million in under two years
([Pearson, 2026](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/credly-issues-50-million-digital-credentials-in-under-two-years-reaching-150-million-amid-growing-demand-for-verified-skills-302944109.html)).
Single institutions now issue them at volume too. University of Phoenix announced its millionth badge in
March, from more than 170 badge offerings, and describes them as "Grounded in assessed skills—not
participation"
([University of Phoenix, 2026](https://www.phoenix.edu/press-release/millionth-digital-badge-milestone.html)).

That volume is the problem as much as the progress. When a recruiter sees a badge, the image tells
them almost nothing. The badges above all look alike on a LinkedIn profile, yet one stands for months
of tracked work, one for top grades plus a conference, one for a faculty judgement against four
competencies. Many of the "digital badge examples" lists that rank for this search show the badge
designs and skip what each one required, which is the part a course designer can actually copy.

## What the evidence says

Research on how employers read badges is thin, and what exists is small. Treat it as early evidence,
but it points one way.

In a 2020 survey of 15 HR directors and talent professionals in north-west Ohio, 60% said they
considered digital badges a legitimate credential when evaluating applicants, and 80% expected badges to
play an important role in hiring in future. The authors saw the badge's metadata as the source of its

That was a convenience sample of 15 people, collected before the recent growth in badges
([Kahle-Piasecki et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.53703/001c.125642)). A 2024 qualitative study for
UpSkill America, based on interviews with 12 companies, found the same gap from the other side:
employers were positive about digital credentials, but "They aren't sure what credentials indicate about
knowledge and skill levels", and few had a systematic way to use them in hiring
([WGU, 2024](https://www.wgu.edu/newsroom/press-release/2024/10/employers-credentials-positively-interpreting-integrating-hiring.html)).

Neither study tested whether badges change who gets hired. For that, the best evidence we know of is a
randomised trial with Coursera learners, covered in our piece on
[LinkedIn digital badges](/trends/linkedin-digital-badges/): making certificates easier to share raised
new employment, mostly for learners with the weakest CVs. Together, the research suggests a badge helps
when someone sees it and can tell what it means. The criteria do the second job.

## Why it matters for people who build courses

Microsoft's badge is a sign of where workplace credentials are heading: proof drawn from real work,
updated as the work changes. Most course creators can't track a learner's use of a tool for months. But
they can borrow the principle behind all three examples. Each badge names a standard (a proficiency
level, an A plus an activity, four competencies) and says who checks it (an algorithm against a
framework, the grade book plus the department, trained faculty). A badge awarded for watching every
lesson names neither.

This matters more as badges multiply. The more of them a recruiter sees, the more the click-through page
has to carry. Our piece on [Open Badges 3.0](/trends/open-badges-3-verifiable-credentials/) covers how
signed badges prove who issued them; nothing in the format proves the assessment behind them was sound.

## What we'd do

1. **Write the criteria before the badge.** Start from your
   [learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/) and decide what observable work earns
   the badge. If the answer is "finished the videos", call it a completion certificate instead.
2. **Assess something real.** A marked project, a scenario task or a short performance check against a
   rubric says more than a completion rule. Build the practice in along the way, with the
   [formative assessment](/library/formative-assessment/) that gets learners there.
3. **Say who judged it.** Name the assessor or the method on the badge page, as San Antonio College does
   with its trained faculty. A badge with a named standard and a named judge is easier to trust.
4. **Put the criteria where the click lands.** Whatever you issue through, make sure the public badge
   page lists the criteria and the evidence, in plain language an employer recognises. Check what your
   course platform shows on its certificates [TO VERIFY: whether Thinkific, LearnWorlds, Teachable and
   Kajabi certificates have public verification pages that display earning criteria], and use a
   credentialing service if it shows nothing.
5. **Don't stack badges for attendance.** If you also want to reward participation, give it a clearly
   different name, so your skill badges keep their meaning.

## What to watch next

Watch whether usage-based badges spread beyond Microsoft and GitHub, and whether employers treat them
as equal to exams; Microsoft hasn't yet defined its advanced and expert levels. For universities, the
test is whether badges like East Texas A&M's and San Antonio College's carry enough detail on their badge
pages to stand apart from the millions of others. For the wider credential picture, see our piece on
[microcredentials in higher education](/trends/microcredentials-higher-education-this-term/).
