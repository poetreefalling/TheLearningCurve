How big is the eLearning industry, and is it still growing? Search for an answer and you'll find
confident figures from market-research firms, each to the decimal point, that disagree with each
other by hundreds of billions of dollars. So we went to numbers that are audited: the annual reports
and earnings releases of the public companies that sell online learning, plus the longest-running
open survey of US training spend.

Read category by category, those numbers tell a consistent story. The pandemic boom is over. Growth
has slowed almost everywhere, it has moved from individual buyers to employers, and the sector is
consolidating through take-overs, take-privates and one bankruptcy.

## First, why we didn't use the market-size figures

Every eLearning industry article quotes a market size. The trouble is choosing one. For 2025, the
headline estimates we found on research firms' own pages range from US$111 billion to US$370
billion.

<Scrolly chart="elearning-market-estimates">
  <Step view="low">Market Research Future puts the global eLearning market at about US$111 billion for 2025.</Step>
  <Step view="high">IMARC Group puts it at about US$370 billion, more than three times as much. Two others, Grand View Research and Fortune Business Insights, sit near the top, though they size an "e-learning services" market.</Step>
</Scrolly>

The gap is mostly about definitions: what counts as eLearning, whether services and staff are in,
whether school technology is. The methods are in paid reports we couldn't open and check. So the
rest of this piece uses two kinds of number you can trace: survey totals with a published method,
and company revenue from filings. They cover only part of the industry, and big private companies
are missing, but every figure links to its source.

## Corporate training: a US$100 billion market that has stopped growing

Training magazine surveys US organisations' training budgets every year. Its total
crossed US$100 billion for the first time in 2022 and has hovered there since.

<Scrolly chart="us-training-spend">
  <Step view="rise">Spend rose from US$83 billion in 2019 to US$101.6 billion in 2022 as organisations moved training online.</Step>
  <Step view="flat">Since then it has barely moved: US$101.8 billion in 2023, US$98 billion in 2024 and US$102.8 billion in 2025. Prices rose over the same years, so in real terms that's a fall.</Step>
</Scrolly>

These are nominal figures from a survey projected to the whole US company universe, so treat small
year-to-year moves with caution. The same reports show each employee getting less: 40 hours of
training in 2025, down from 62.4 in 2022
([Training magazine, 2022](https://trainingmag.com/2022-training-industry-report/); [2025](https://trainingmag.com/2025-training-industry-report/)).

The workplace platform vendors show the slowdown in their own accounts. Docebo, a listed corporate
learning platform, still grows, but more slowly every year.

<DataChart id="docebo-revenue" />

Docebo's 2025 revenue was US$242.7 million, up 11.9%
([Docebo, 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1829959/000162828026012570/docebo2025q4pr.htm)).
Skillsoft, a long-standing corporate content library, has gone backwards: revenue fell to US$513
million in its year to January 2026, from US$555.1 million three years earlier, and in July 2026 it
sold its instructor-led Global Knowledge business
([Skillsoft, 2023](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1774675/000143774923010001/ex_476794.htm);
[2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1774675/000143774926011599/ex_898416.htm);
[2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001774675/000143774926029980/ex_982157.htm)).
Two other big names, Cornerstone and Instructure, no longer publish accounts at all, because
private-equity owners took them private.

## Course-creator platforms: flat sales, growth from payments

For independent course creators, the best public window is Thinkific, which reports how much its
creators sell, not just its own revenue. That figure, gross merchandise volume, jumped during the
pandemic and has been flat since.

<Scrolly chart="thinkific-gmv">
  <Step view="spike">Creators' sales on Thinkific rose from US$276.4 million in 2020 to US$414.8 million in 2021.</Step>
  <Step view="plateau">Since 2023 they have stayed at about US$445–460 million a year. Thinkific itself described 2025 as having "remained consistent at $460.0 million".</Step>
</Scrolly>

Thinkific's own revenue still grew 9% in 2025, to US$73.2 million, but most of the growth came from
taking payments for creators: commerce revenue rose 32% while subscriptions rose 5%
([Thinkific, 2026](https://investors.thinkific.com/2026-03-05-Thinkific-Announces-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2025-Financial-Results)).
In other words, the platform is earning more from each creator's sales, not seeing more sales.

The private platforms publish only cumulative marketing figures. Kajabi says its creators have
earned "over $10 billion" in total since it was founded in 2010
([Kajabi, 2025](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250806424975/en/%2410B-in-Creator-Revenue-and-Climbing-Kajabi-Creators-Are-Achieving-Long-Term-Financial-Success)).
That's a company claim with no yearly breakdown, so it can't tell us whether the market is growing.

## Course marketplaces: employers have replaced individual buyers

Udemy's accounts show the biggest shift in the industry most clearly. Its consumer course store has
shrunk every year since 2021. Its business arm, which sells course libraries to employers, took over.

<Scrolly chart="udemy-segments">
  <Step view="start">In 2021 Udemy made US$328.7 million from individual learners buying courses, and US$187.0 million from businesses.</Step>
  <Step view="cross">By 2022 the two were almost level, and from 2023 business sales were the larger part.</Step>
  <Step view="gap">By 2025 consumer revenue had fallen to US$265.8 million, while Udemy Business reached US$524.1 million, about two-thirds of the total.</Step>
</Scrolly>

Total revenue barely moved in 2025, at US$789.8 million
([Udemy, 2026](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260204247314/en/Udemy-Reports-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2025-Results)).
Seven weeks before those results came out, Udemy had agreed to combine with Coursera; the deal completed on 11 May 2026 and
Udemy shares were delisted
([Coursera, 2026](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260511605128/en/)). On a like-for-like
basis, counting Udemy's results before the merger, the combined company's revenue was down about 3%
in the third quarter of 2026, with consumer revenue down about 9%
([Coursera, 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001651562/000165156226000068/cour-20260930xshareholde.htm)).

## Online degrees and language apps: a bankruptcy and a billion-dollar app

The sharpest contrast is between online degrees and consumer apps. 2U, which ran online degree
programmes for universities, was one of the best-known companies in the space. Duolingo sells a
language app with a free tier.

<Scrolly chart="degrees-vs-apps">
  <Step view="degrees">2U's revenue peaked at US$963.1 million in 2022. In 2023 it fell to US$946.0 million, and that included US$88.0 million in one-off payments for exiting degree programmes. In July 2024 2U filed for Chapter 11, and it left bankruptcy that September as a private company.</Step>
  <Step view="mooc">Coursera, which sells degrees alongside courses, grew steadily but more slowly each year: 41% in 2021, then 9% in both 2024 and 2025, to US$757.5 million.</Step>
  <Step view="app">Duolingo grew between 39% and 55% every year from 2021 to 2025, passing US$1 billion in 2025, more than 2U ever made.</Step>
</Scrolly>

Duolingo's revenue rests on a very large daily habit.

<DataChart id="duolingo-dau" />

Even Duolingo is slowing. Its third-quarter 2026 revenue was US$309.0 million, against US$271.7
million a year earlier, growth of about 14%, well below the 39% of 2025
([Duolingo, 2026](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001562088/000162828026069692/duol-20260930.htm)).

## What it means if you build courses

These are company accounts, not learning research, and they cover only listed businesses. They say
nothing about whether learners learned. But four patterns are clear enough to act on.

1. **The buyer has changed.** Across marketplaces and platforms, growth has come from employers
   buying for staff, not individuals buying for themselves. Courses that answer an organisation's
   question (what will staff be able to do, and how will we know?) are aligned with where the money
   is going.
2. **More platform revenue doesn't mean more course sales.** Thinkific's creators sold about the same
   in 2025 as in 2023. If your own sales are flat, you're not alone; the market for one-off courses
   has stopped expanding.
3. **Habit beats completion.** The fastest-growing business in this sample is built on short daily
   practice, not long courses. That fits what the research says about spacing and retrieval, which
   our library covers in [microlearning](/library/microlearning/) and
   [cognitive load](/library/cognitive-load-theory/).
4. **Be sceptical of market-size headlines,** including in pitch decks and vendor reports. Ask what's
   counted and whether you can see the method.
