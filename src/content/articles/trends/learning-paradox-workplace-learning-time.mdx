It's 2.30 on a Tuesday afternoon, and you have a training course to finish. It's 30 minutes long,
it was assigned three weeks ago, and it's due on Friday. Your inbox is filling up. A meeting starts at
three. Three tasks you promised yesterday are still open.

So you open the course in one window and keep your email in the other. You play the video at 1.5
times speed, answer a chat that pops up halfway through, click through the five quiz questions and
press Complete. The learning platform records a completion. A dashboard somewhere turns green.

Did you learn anything?

*That afternoon is illustrative, not a reported case. If it felt familiar, the evidence below
suggests you're far from alone.*

Organisations have spent a decade building the machinery of workplace learning: learning platforms,
content libraries, microlearning, and now AI that can draft a course in an afternoon. What most of
them haven't changed is the way work itself is organised. The result is a paradox. People are
expected to build new capabilities while being left less room, in time and in attention, to do it.

Those four figures come from one survey: TalentLMS's annual benchmark of 1,000 US employees and 101
HR managers, fielded in September 2025
([TalentLMS, 2025](https://www.talentlms.com/research/learning-development-report-2026)). TalentLMS
sells a learning platform, and it appears on our [disclosure page](/disclosure/) among the companies
we have, or have applied for, an affiliate relationship with. So we've checked its story against
independent evidence: Australian official statistics, the OECD's survey of adult skills, a
meta-analysis of what happens after training, and two more industry reports. Every figure links to
the [evidence map](#ev-talentlms) at the end, with each source's method and limits.

## The disappearing learning day

Before the numbers, try the arithmetic yourself.

<DayBudget />

With the defaults, half an hour is left. Nudge meetings up by 30 minutes, or let one more fire land
on your desk, and it's gone. That is the point of the exercise: in most jobs, learning is whatever
time survives everything else. It isn't scheduled. It's residual.

## More pressure, the same eight hours

The survey evidence says the squeeze is getting tighter. Sixty-five per cent of employees in the
TalentLMS survey said their company had increased performance expectations in the past year, and
45% said they were pushed to deliver more at work, often or always. Lack of time was employees' top
obstacle to training for the third year running.

What's striking is that the people who run training see it too. Fifty-three per cent of employees
and half of HR managers agreed that high workloads in their company leave little room for training,
even when it's needed. This is not a misunderstanding between the two sides. Both can see the
problem. Neither, it seems, has been able to make room.

## Not only an American problem

A US vendor survey would be easy to dismiss if nothing else pointed the same way. The Australian
Bureau of Statistics measures something different, across the whole population aged 15 to 74, and
it does point the same way.

Participation in work-related training in Australia fell to 19% in 2024–25, from 23% in 2020–21
([ABS, 2026](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/people/education/work-related-training-and-adult-learning/2024-25)).
Five per cent of people had wanted to do work-related training in the past year but didn't. Asked

The OECD's analysis of its 2023 Survey of Adult Skills finds the same thing internationally. About a

These numbers aren't directly comparable with each other or with the US survey. The ABS counts who
took part across a whole population; TalentLMS only asked employees who had already received training.
That's why one survey can find 83% of employees saying they got enough training while the other finds
participation falling. But on the question of what gets in the way, three very different sources give
the same answer. It's time.

## The attention economy comes to work

Time is only half of it. The other half is attention, and here the TalentLMS trend line is the most
revealing chart in the report.

<DataChart id="talentlms-training-attention" />

In 2025, more employees than in either previous year said they'd had enough training. And more of
them than ever, 70%, said they multitask during it. The report describes learning that competes with
"messages, meetings, and unfinished tasks". Taking part and paying attention are not the same thing,
and the gap between them is widening.

The wider working day explains why. Microsoft's analysis of Microsoft 365 activity found that its
most-interrupted users are interrupted every two minutes during core hours, 275 times a day, by
meetings, emails or chats
([Microsoft, 2025](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/breaking-down-infinite-workday)).
That figure describes the top fifth of users by the number of pings they receive, not the typical
worker, and Microsoft sells the tools being measured. But it describes the environment a 30-minute
course has to survive in. Our library entry on [cognitive load](/library/cognitive-load-theory/)
explains why a learner who is splitting attention has less of it left for the material.

## The completion illusion

If attention is divided, completion data can mislead. A learning platform can record that someone
finished a module. It can't record whether they were also answering email, or whether they will ever
use what it covered. And when organisations judge learning by what their systems can count, it is
easy to mistake the activity for the outcome.

The TalentLMS survey offers a way to test that, because it asked HR managers and employees several
of the same questions.

The biggest gap in that chart isn't between HR and employees. It's between what both groups say about
training, mostly satisfied, and what both say about the conditions for it, short of time and treated
as a distraction. Satisfaction measures how people feel about the courses. It doesn't measure whether
the job leaves room to learn from them.

## What happens after the course

The deepest version of the problem starts when the course ends. Researchers call it transfer: whether
people use what they learned on the job, and keep using it.

Ashley Hughes, Eduardo Salas and colleagues pooled the research on three kinds of support at work:
from peers, from supervisors and from the organisation
([Hughes et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720819845988)). All three were moderately and
positively related to transfer. Together they accounted for 32% of it, and peer support accounted for
most of that. Motivation to use the training sat between support and transfer. On long-term use,
the result was even clearer:

These are correlations, so they show what goes with transfer rather than proving what causes it. But
they point somewhere a course can't reach on its own: the colleague who asks how the new approach
went, the manager who hands over a task that needs the new skill, the team that makes room to try it.
None of that shows up in a completion report. Our library entry on
[the characteristics of effective feedback](/library/effective-feedback/) covers one piece of that
support in detail.

## The organisational contradiction

Which brings us back to the most uncomfortable number in the survey. Nearly half of employees (46%)
and HR managers (49%) say their company views training as time away from real work.

Read that alongside everything else. Organisations ask people to build new skills, often urgently.
The World Economic Forum's survey of more than 1,000 large employers suggests that if the world's
workforce were 100 people, 59 would need training by 2030, and that employers expect 39% of workers'
existing skill sets to be transformed or become outdated by then
([World Economic Forum, 2025](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/)).
Yet the same organisations signal, through workloads and priorities, that learning isn't really part
of the job.

LinkedIn's 2025 Workplace Learning Report finds the same tension in career development. Only 36% of
organisations in its survey qualified as what it calls career development champions, with robust
programs that yield business results, and it describes a lack of time and resources as "a glaring
pain point" ([LinkedIn Learning, 2025](https://learning.linkedin.com/content/dam/me/business/en-us/talent-solutions/resources/pdfs/linkedIn-workplace-learning-report-2025.pdf)).

When learning isn't part of the job, it competes with the job. And on any given Tuesday afternoon,
the job wins.

## Five changes the evidence supports

We'd resist the usual list of ten tips. These five follow directly from the evidence above. Where the
research is thin, we say so. They overlap with several of the ten interventions in the TalentLMS
report itself, which starts with allocating and protecting learning time.

1. **Protect learning time.** Time is the most consistent barrier in every source here, from US
   employees to Australian and OECD adults. If learning matters, it belongs in workforce planning and
   workload allocation, not in the gaps between meetings. A course that is assigned without taking
   anything else off the list has been scheduled for after hours.
2. **Design for application, not delivery.** Nearly a third of employees in the TalentLMS survey said
   their training was too theoretical. Build in practice on realistic tasks and feedback on it. Our
   guide to [writing multiple-choice questions that teach](/guides/writing-multiple-choice-questions/)
   shows how even a quiz can be practice rather than a box to tick.
3. **Make managers and peers part of the design.** The meta-analysis evidence is strongest here: peer
   and supervisor support go with training being used and kept up. Plan what happens in the weeks
   after a course, and who will be involved, before you build it.
4. **Measure capability, not just completion.** Completion tells you who clicked through. Track
   whether people apply the skill, how their work changes and whether the skill lasts. The evidence on
   which measures work best in organisations is thinner than we'd like, so start with one you can
   observe directly.
5. **Use AI to give time back, not only to make more content.** AI can shorten course production.
   The TalentLMS report itself cautions organisations to balance AI with human oversight. If the time
   saved simply becomes more courses for the same overloaded people, the paradox gets worse. We'd
   reinvest it in coaching, practice and better design. That is our judgement; the evidence on AI in
   workplace learning is still early.

## The question for the people who build learning

Learning and development teams are very good at building courses. The evidence in this feature
suggests that courses aren't the scarce resource. Time, attention and the chance to use a new skill
are.

So the question we'd leave with anyone who designs learning for a living is this: are we designing
courses, or are we designing the conditions in which people can develop? The first fits neatly into
a learning platform and a completion report. The second means changing how work is organised, and
that's a conversation that has to happen well outside the L&D team.
