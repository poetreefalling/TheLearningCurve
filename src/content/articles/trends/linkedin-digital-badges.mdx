Getting a course certificate onto a LinkedIn profile is worth more than it looks, at least for some
learners. In a randomised trial with more than 800,000 Coursera certificate earners in developing
countries, all without college degrees, a two-click "add to LinkedIn" feature and two reminders raised
new employment by 6% within a year. Most of the gain went to the learners whose CVs were weakest
([Athey & Palikot, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.00247)). That's one platform and one population,
but it lands as LinkedIn changes what a profile can prove. Since January, LinkedIn has added verified
signals for skills, jobs, schools and the apps people use. A course badge still sits in the same
place it always did, checked on the issuer's page rather than by LinkedIn.

## What changed

Two developments in the past two months build on a year of profile changes.

**Colleagues and classmates can now vouch for a profile.** In late September 2026, LinkedIn began
testing a way for members to confirm the work and education history of people they worked or studied
with. Once a member gets enough confirmations, LinkedIn adds a verification badge to their profile,
and members can opt out ([Help Net Security, 2026](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/09/28/linkedin-profile-verification-features/);
[Social Media Today, 2026](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-expands-verifications/831212/)).
A confirmation says only that two people overlapped: "People can confirm that they worked or studied
with someone during the period listed on that person's profile"
([Malwarebytes, 2026](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2026/09/linkedin-adds-new-checks-for-fake-profiles-and-work-histories)).
The same update lets admins of verified company pages remove people who falsely claim to work there,
and adds Truecaller and PeerSpot to the sites where members can show their LinkedIn verification.
LinkedIn's existing tools have verified "115 million members and more than 700,000 companies".

**More apps can write to your profile.** On 17 June 2026, LinkedIn launched connected apps. A member
links an app they use, and "each app generates a simple statement based on your real activity". The
statement "is provided by the app and cannot be edited", and the most engaged users get a "Top User"
signal. LinkedIn said more than a million members had connected apps
([LinkedIn, 2026a](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/visibility-builds-credibility---the-tools-you-use-every-day--now)).
Duolingo, Descript and Replit were among the first; Adobe Express, GitHub and Zapier were listed as
coming ([Social Media Today, 2026b](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-adds-more-digital-skills-visibility/823219/)).
Adobe Express is now live: its help page, last updated on 5 November 2026, says that as you create
designs "you unlock skills", and that "Your selected skills sync to LinkedIn and appear on your profile
as verified credentials" ([Adobe, 2026](https://helpx.adobe.com/express/web/publish-and-share/share-to-social-media/linkedin-skills.html)).

Both build on January's launch of verified AI skills, where tool makers including Descript, Lovable,
Relay.app and Replit "assess your proficiency based on your usage of their product" and issue a
certificate the member can show on their profile
([LinkedIn, 2026b](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/Professional_Edge_Skills_Verified)). LinkedIn's
Pat Whelan told Computerworld that certification from the companies themselves is more trustworthy
than self-reported skills ([Shah, 2026](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4137409/linkedin-moves-to-offer-skill-validations-in-the-ai-era.html)).

## What hasn't changed: the course badge

None of this verifies a course certificate. A badge from a course or a credentialing service still
goes in the Licences and certifications section, and the checking still happens elsewhere. In
Credly's flow, for example, the learner connects LinkedIn once and chooses "Add to profile"; anyone
viewing it can "click 'See Credential' to access the full badge details on Credly", where they can
check it's genuine and see the skills and earning criteria
([The Tax Institute, 2026](https://www.taxinstitute.com.au/insights/articles/2026/how-to-upload-a-credly-digital-badge-to-your-linkedin-profile)).
Our piece on [Open Badges 3.0](/trends/open-badges-3-verifiable-credentials/) covers how signed,
verifiable badges make that check sturdier.

So a profile now carries signals of different strength side by side: a job confirmed by colleagues, an
app description the member can't edit, a tool maker's skill certificate, and a course badge the
learner chose to add. A recruiter can see the first three without leaving LinkedIn. The course badge
asks them to click.

## What the evidence says

The best evidence we've found on course credentials and LinkedIn comes from Susan Athey and Emil
Palikot's experiment, revised in November 2025 and not yet peer reviewed
([Athey & Palikot, 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.00247)). Between September 2022 and March 2023,
Coursera randomly gave some new certificate earners a notification offering to "Add the certificate to
your LinkedIn profile in just two clicks", plus one follow-up. Everyone else could still share, by
copying the certificate link into LinkedIn by hand. The treatment group was 17.3% more likely to share,
and the authors tracked jobs for about 37,000 learners whose LinkedIn profiles they could match:

A separate test in the paper points the same way. When the authors scored CVs with and without a
certificate, it improved perceived candidate quality by 8.5 points on a 100-point scale on average,
"with effects of 15-20 points for weak resumes but near-zero for strong resumes."

We'd read this as moderate evidence that visibility matters: a credential nobody sees can't signal
anything, and the people with the least else to show gain most from making it seen. The limits are
real. Jobs were measured from members' own LinkedIn updates, the learners who share are probably more
career-minded than most, outcomes were tracked for under a year, and every participant was from a
developing country without a degree. An older experiment adds a ceiling: in hiring simulations with
online respondents rating a hypothetical web developer, a MOOC certificate was strongly preferred to
no credential, but every traditional degree was preferred to the MOOC
([Rivas et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/2332858420973577)). A course badge helps most where it
fills a gap, not where it competes with a degree.

## Why it matters for people who build courses

If a two-click share moved employment for the learners who needed it most, the share button is part of
the course. Most course creators treat the certificate as the end of the learning design and the
LinkedIn step as the learner's business. The Coursera trial suggests that friction at that step costs
some learners real opportunities.

The 2026 changes also raise the bar for what a course badge has to say. Sitting next to colleague-confirmed jobs and app-written skill descriptions, a badge that says only "completed" looks thin. When
a recruiter does click through, the issuer's page is the whole case: the criteria, the assessment and
the skills.

## What we'd do

1. **Put the LinkedIn step in the course.** On the completion page and in the completion email, give
   learners the add-to-profile link and one sentence on why it's worth doing. Remind them once more a
   few days later, as the trial did. If you use a credentialing service, check that its badges have a
   direct add-to-profile option rather than a download.
2. **Make the click-through worth it.** The page a recruiter lands on should say what the learner did,
   in the observable terms of your [learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/), and
   how it was assessed. Award the badge for assessed work, as the assessment section of our
   [online course design checklist](/guides/online-course-design-checklist/) sets out.
3. **Know what your platform gives you.** Check whether your course platform's certificates include a
   LinkedIn add-to-profile link and a public verification page
   [TO VERIFY: which of Thinkific, LearnWorlds, Teachable and Kajabi certificates offer a LinkedIn add-to-profile link and a public verification URL, from each platform's current help pages].
   If yours doesn't, a credentialing service can add both.
4. **Don't oversell it to learners.** The evidence is strongest for people without a degree and with
   little else on their profile. For an experienced professional, a course badge is a detail, not a
   door-opener. Say so, and your learners will trust the rest of your advice.

## What to watch next

Watch which tools join connected apps and verified skills, and whether any course or credentialing
platform becomes one. If a course platform could write a non-editable, assessment-backed statement to
a learner's profile, the gap between a course badge and the other signals would close. Until then, the
course badge is the one the learner has to add and the recruiter has to click, so make both steps easy.
