The news in microcredentials this term isn't that there are more of them. It's that the people who
fund and recognise them have started asking what they prove. An international review, a new set of
US funding rules and the term's university launches all point the same way: a short credential is
worth what its assessment, its credit and its evidence of results are worth. For anyone who builds
courses, that moves the work from issuing a certificate to designing something an employer can trust.

## What happened

Three developments since the start of September show the shift.

**The OECD asked countries to line their systems up.** On 30 September 2026 the OECD published
*International Review of Micro-Credential Systems: From Fragmentation to Alignment*, in its Getting
Skills Right series ([OECD, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1787/bd913f1c-en)). It looks at how countries
define and organise microcredentials across education and training, how they build trust in their
quality, how they link them to labour market needs, and how learners and employers use them. Its
summary puts the problem plainly: "Without greater alignment, micro-credentials risk adding confusion
rather than opportunity."

**US funding now comes with outcome tests.** Workforce Pell grants let eligible US students use
federal Pell money for short programmes. The Department of Education's FAQ, updated in October 2026,
sets out what a programme must show
([Federal Student Aid, 2026](https://fsapartners.ed.gov/sites/default/files/2026-10/WorkforcePellFAQs.pdf)).
It must run for at least eight but fewer than 15 weeks (150 to 599 clock hours), be certified by a
state governor and approved by the department, and reach a completion rate and a job placement rate
of at least 70 per cent each. Tuition and fees may not exceed the programme's value-added earnings,
and completers must be able to receive academic credit that counts towards a certificate or degree.
That is a much higher bar than "finished the videos".

**Universities are designing for stacking.** On 12 October 2026, American Military University and
American Public University announced six undergraduate microcredentials for first responders, among
them Basics of Emergency Management and Disaster Management Fundamentals
([AMU and APU, 2026](https://soldiersystems.net/2026/10/13/american-military-university-american-public-university-launch-new-ai-certificate-two-new-finance-programs-and-six-new-microcredentials-for-first-responders/)).
Each is a three-course programme designed to stack into a bachelor's degree in emergency and
disaster management or fire science management. In Spain, the University of Málaga began a 3.5
ECTS microcredential in hydrogen technologies on 5 October 2026, recognised by the university, with
25 senior engineering students and working professionals in its first edition
([H2Excellence, 2026](https://h2excellence.eu/university-of-malaga-launches-first-h2excellence-hydrogen-microcredential/)).

## Why it matters for people who build courses

For a few years, "microcredential" could mean almost anything: a badge for watching a webinar, a
certificate of completion, a credit-bearing unit of a master's degree. The developments above draw a
line between those. The credentials that funders and universities now take seriously have three
things in common: they state what the learner can do, they assess it, and they plug into something
larger, whether that's a degree, a licence or a job outcome someone measures.

Most course platforms make it easy to issue a certificate when a learner reaches the end. That's a
record of completion. It isn't evidence of competence, and the systems taking shape this term are
built to tell the difference. If your course promises a career outcome, that's the comparison your
learners and their employers will increasingly make.

## What the evidence says

The research on microcredentials is newer and thinner than the research on how people learn, but two
findings are useful for anyone designing one.

The first is about what employers want to know. Soovendran Varadarajan and colleagues reviewed 60
studies published between 2015 and 2022 on microcredentials in higher education
([Varadarajan et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41239-023-00381-x)). Each group wanted something
different:

Concern about consistency came up far more often than concern about formal recognition: almost 80 per
cent of the studies they reviewed raised employers' worries about the consistency of
microcredentials, compared with 22 per cent that raised the lack of formal recognition. A 2025 survey
of 150 US hiring decision-makers by Eduvantis, a higher education consultancy, points the same way:
employers treated microcredentials as "supplemental signals rather than primary hiring criteria", and
57 per cent said they almost always look one up to understand it better
([Eduvantis, 2026](https://eduvantis.com/do-employers-trust-microcredentials-what-150-hiring-managers-told-us/)).
That's one consultancy's survey, so we'd read it as a signal, not a measurement.

The second finding is about visibility. Susan Athey and Emil Palikot ran a randomised experiment with
more than 800,000 online certificate earners from developing countries who lacked college degrees,
encouraging some to share their credentials on LinkedIn with reminders and less friction
([Athey & Palikot, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00247)). Among the 40,000 learners whose
employment they tracked, the nudge raised new employment by 6 per cent (1.0 percentage point), and by
9 per cent for jobs related to the certificate.

<ResearchCard n={1} />

Two limits matter. The paper is a working paper, not yet peer-reviewed in a journal, and its learners
were in developing countries without degrees, so the size of the effect may not carry over to your
audience. And it tests the value of showing a credential, not the quality of the course behind it.
Taken together, we'd treat these findings as moderate evidence that a credential helps most when it
is easy to see and easy to understand, and early evidence on everything else.

## What we'd do

If you build a course that ends in a certificate, here's how we'd respond this term.

1. **Name the competencies on the credential.** List what the learner can now do, in the same
   observable terms as your [learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/). "Can
   build a cash-flow forecast for a small business" tells an employer something; "Completed Module 6"
   doesn't.
2. **Award it for assessment, not completion.** Tie the certificate to a task that shows the skill: a
   project, a scenario, a graded assignment against a rubric. Watching every lesson shouldn't be
   enough. Our [online course design checklist](/guides/online-course-design-checklist/) covers how to
   line the assessment up with the objectives.
3. **Make it easy to check.** A shareable, verifiable credential with the criteria attached beats a
   PDF. Ask your platform whether its certificates carry a verification link or can be issued as
   Open Badges.
4. **Tell learners to show it.** The cheapest intervention in the evidence above was a nudge to share.
   Build a short final step into the course that shows learners how to add the credential to their
   profile and describe what it proves.
5. **Decide whether you want it to stack.** If your learners need credit towards a qualification,
   that means partnering with a university, college or industry body that can recognise it. That's a
   design decision to make before you build the assessment, not after, and the analysis phase of the
   [ADDIE model](/guides/addie-model/) is where it belongs.

## What to watch next

The useful signal over the next few months won't be the number of new microcredentials. It will be
whether funders publish the completion, placement and earnings data their rules now require, and
whether employers start asking for credentials by what they assess rather than who issued them. Until
then, design the credential as if someone will check what it claims, because increasingly they will.
