The most useful piece of online learning research this month is not a success story. In a randomised
trial at the University of Maryland, undergraduates whose instructors switched on an AI tutor inside
their course site ended the semester with lower grades, and took part much less in the course site
itself. For anyone adding an AI assistant to a course, it is the clearest warning yet that the tool
can push aside the learning activities that were already working.

## What happened

At the end of September 2026, a team led by Jing Liu posted the trial as a working paper through
Brown University's Annenberg Institute
([Liu et al., 2026](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai26-1598)). It ran in the Fall 2025 semester with
2,379 undergraduates and 30 instructors, in courses from 11 of the university's 12 schools and
colleges, including biology, computer science and communication.

The tutor, called the Virtual Study Assistant, was a GPT-4o chatbot built into the university's
learning management system (LMS). It sat in the course's sidebar, drew on the course's own materials
and declined questions it judged unrelated to the course. Randomisation happened at the instructor
level: within each block of comparable courses, some instructors had the tutor switched on for all
their students and some didn't.

Three findings stand out.

**Grades went down.** In the cleanest comparison, sections of exactly the same course (1,353
students, 22 instructors), students with the tutor scored 4.12 points lower on final grades, or 0.37
standard deviations. Across the full sample the drop was smaller, 2.88 points.

**Activity in the course site fell sharply.** Recorded participation in the LMS fell by 0.90 standard
deviations in the same-course comparison, with page views and active days down too. The authors
report declines of similar size in the full sample.

**The losses were not shared evenly.** In the full sample, first-generation students' final grades
fell by an estimated 5.10 points (0.50 standard deviations), against 2.23 points (0.22 standard
deviations) for other students.

## What the tutor was actually used for

The usage data are as interesting as the results. Only 14.63 per cent of students with access ever
used the tutor, and use was episodic, bunching around exams. When they did use it, they mostly wanted
answers:

Practice and test preparation was the second most common kind of request. And the tutor was set up
to oblige: it had a tutor mode that guides students towards an answer, but it ran in a
direct-instruction mode by default, and the paper reports that only two instructors ended the term
with tutor mode switched on. Instructors were given no prescribed way to build the tool into their
teaching.

That puts this trial alongside earlier work showing that an assistant which hands over answers can
harm later performance, while one designed to make learners do the thinking can help. We covered the
best-known of those trials in
[AI in eLearning: what changed this term](/trends/ai-in-elearning-this-term/).

## Two other studies worth reading

**Course, chatbot or both?** On 13 October 2026, Lendel Narine and John Diaz published a small
randomised pilot with US Cooperative Extension professionals learning programme evaluation
([Narine & Diaz, 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci16101700)). People were assigned to an online
certificate course, a custom GPT, both, or neither. The course and the GPT each lifted knowledge test
scores to around 91 per cent, against 67 per cent for the control group, and having both added no
clear further gain. Confidence in their evaluation skills rose least in the group that had both. The
authors call the findings preliminary: only 78 of 144 registrants gave usable data, and attrition
differed between groups.

**Getting learners to use the tutor at all.** In August 2026, Philip Oreopoulos and Nina Low reported
a two-year trial of Khanmigo, Khan Academy's AI tutor, in 18 Tennessee middle schools
([Oreopoulos & Low, 2026](https://edworkingpapers.com/ai26-1551)). The tutor was set up to coach
rather than give answers. Maths achievement rose modestly, by about 0.06 to 0.08 standard deviations
over a school year, similar to Khan Academy practice without the AI. Almost every student tried it,
but the median student messaged it on about a third of practice days, and in only 17 per cent of
sessions where they made a mistake. The authors conclude that getting students to use the tutor is
the main constraint.

## How strong is this evidence?

Treat it as early evidence that should change how you pilot an AI tutor, not as proof that AI tutors
lower grades.

The Maryland study is large and randomised, but it is a working paper that hasn't yet been through
peer review. It measured course grades rather than an independent test. Students in both groups
could use ChatGPT and Gemini through the university anyway. And the authors are careful about the
mechanism: they say the drop in LMS activity doesn't establish that disengagement caused the lower
grades, and with so few students using the tutor, the effect may have worked through broader AI use
or changes in instructors' practice. Fewer page views could even reflect students finding things
faster.

What the three studies share is narrower and more useful. Putting an AI tutor in front of learners
doesn't, on its own, make them use it well. Left to their own devices, most ask for answers or don't
ask at all, and a tutor can quietly replace practice they would otherwise have done.

## What we'd do

If you're adding an AI assistant to an online course, or your platform has just switched one on,
here's how we'd respond.

1. **Give it a job before you switch it on.** Decide which task it supports, such as feedback on a
   draft answer or hints on a problem set, and tell learners when to use it and when not to. A
   general helper in the sidebar is what this trial tested.
2. **Check the default.** If the assistant can give hints before answers, or ask the learner to try
   first, turn that on. In this trial the answer-first default stayed in place in almost every
   section.
3. **Protect the practice that already works.** Quizzes, worked problems and assignments with
   feedback are where learning happens. If the assistant makes them optional in practice, it is
   costing you. Our guides to [writing multiple-choice questions](/guides/writing-multiple-choice-questions/)
   and [effective feedback](/library/effective-feedback/) cover what to keep.
4. **Measure before and after.** Compare quiz attempts, assignment submissions and final scores for a
   cohort with the assistant against one without, as in the evaluation phase of the
   [ADDIE model](/guides/addie-model/). Watch the learners who were already finding the course hard.
5. **Keep an AI-free check.** A short closed-book quiz at the start of each module shows what
   learners can do on their own, whatever tools they used along the way.

## What to watch next

The useful follow-ups will be trials that switch the tutor's default to hint-first, give instructors
a plan for using it, and measure learning with an independent test weeks later. Until then, the
safest reading of this month's research is the one the Maryland authors offer themselves: adding AI
help to a course changes how learners engage with everything else in it, and that change needs
checking.
