Every December brings a wave of eLearning trend lists, and most of them are predictions: AI will transform everything, microlearning is the future, the metaverse is coming back. This isn't one of those. We went to the primary data instead, the national statistics, the peer-reviewed trials and the industry surveys with their methods attached, and asked what the numbers actually show going into 2027.

Seven shifts stand out. Each one comes with an interactive chart you can explore, the data table behind it, and the source it came from. And each one ends with what we'd do about it, depending on whether you design courses, run a learning function or build the tools.

The 54% is our sum of two NCES figures for fall 2023: 26.1% of students studied exclusively online and 28.0% took some of their courses online.

## 1. Online study has settled at a new, higher level

The pandemic pushed most of higher education online overnight. The question for 2027 is what stayed, and the US data answers it more clearly than anywhere else, because the National Center for Education Statistics has counted every student's distance-education status each fall for over a decade ([NCES, 2024](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d24/tables/dt24_311.15.asp)).

<Scrolly chart="us-distance-ed">
  <Step view="before">
    **Before the pandemic, online study was growing slowly.** Between fall 2012 and fall 2019, the share of US students studying entirely online rose from 12.8% to 17.6%, about two-thirds of a percentage point a year.
  </Step>
  <Step view="spike">
    **Then fall 2020 happened.** Almost half of all students, 46.7%, studied exclusively online, and only a quarter took no online courses at all.
  </Step>
  <Step view="settle">
    **It didn't snap back.** By fall 2023 the exclusively online share had settled at 26.1%: lower than the peak, but around eight and a half points above where it was in 2019.
  </Step>
  <Step view="mixed">
    **And mixed study is now the norm.** Another 28.0% took some courses online. Fewer than half of US college students, 45.9%, now study without any online course at all.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

The shift is sharper at postgraduate level. In fall 2023, 39.1% of postbaccalaureate students studied exclusively online, against 23.5% of undergraduates ([NCES, 2024](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/digest/d24/tables/dt24_311.15.asp)).

Australia's universities have had a large external cohort for decades, and the mix has held steady. An analysis of Department of Education data found that 22.4% of domestic undergraduates at public universities studied externally in 2024 and 28.7% studied multi-modally, almost unchanged from 2021 ([ACSES, 2026](https://s3.ap-southeast-2.amazonaws.com/assets.acses.edu.au/app/uploads/2026/03/ACSES-Data-Insights-Mode-of-Attendance-Mar26.pdf)).

**What it means for 2027.** Online is no longer the alternative pathway; for most students it's part of the degree. For course designers, that means designing every unit as if some of it will be taken online, even when it's timetabled as face to face. For university leaders, a quarter of students studying entirely online is a cohort that needs its own support, not a scaled-down version of the campus experience. Our guide to [cognitive load](/library/cognitive-load-theory/) is a good place to start on what changes when a lecture becomes a self-paced lesson.

## 2. AI use among students is close to universal

No technology in education has spread this fast. The Higher Education Policy Institute's annual survey of UK full-time undergraduates gives the clearest year-on-year picture ([Stephenson & Armstrong, 2026](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/HEPI-Report-199-Gen-AI-Survey-2026.pdf)).

<Scrolly chart="uk-students-ai">
  <Step view="start">
    **In the 2024 survey, two-thirds of UK undergraduates, 66%, said they used AI tools in at least one way.**
  </Step>
  <Step view="jump">
    **A year later it was 92%.** In a single year, AI went from something most students had tried to something almost all of them used.
  </Step>
  <Step view="now">
    **By the 2026 survey it was 95%,** and 94% said they used generative AI to help with assessed work. The 2026 survey polled 1,054 students in December 2025.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

US data shows the same pattern, and a gap. In Tyton Partners' 2025 survey of more than 3,300 students, instructors and administrators at over 900 US institutions, 42% of students used generative AI weekly, against 30% of instructors ([Tyton Partners, 2025](https://tytonpartners.com/time-for-class-2025/)).

<DataChart id="us-weekly-ai" />

The surveys measure use, not learning. The 2024 UK survey was run by UCAS and the later ones by Savanta, so the first step in that chart compares slightly different polls.

**What it means for 2027.** Assume every learner has an AI assistant open while they study, because almost all of them do. For course designers, that means assessment that still tells you something when a chatbot is available: tasks that ask learners to apply, critique or explain their own work, and practice that's worth doing without shortcuts. For leaders, it means the policy question has moved from "should students use AI" to "what do we want them to learn to do with it". For EdTech builders, the students are already there; the instructors aren't yet.

## 3. AI tutors can help, if they're built to teach

The bigger question is whether AI helps people learn. Until recently there was little rigorous evidence either way. Now there are randomised trials, and their message is more interesting than either the hype or the alarm.

<Scrolly chart="ai-tutor-effects">
  <Step view="harvard">
    **At Harvard, an AI tutor beat a good class.** In a crossover trial in an introductory physics course, 194 students learned more from an AI tutor built around research on teaching than from an active-learning class, a 0.63 SD effect that the authors call an underestimate. They also spent less time: a median of 49 minutes, against a 60-minute class ([Kestin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6)).
  </Step>
  <Step view="nigeria">
    **In Nigeria, a six-week after-school programme worked too.** Secondary students who used an AI tutor for English, with teachers guiding the sessions, scored 0.31 SD higher on the final assessment than the control group ([De Simone et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-11125)). It's a working paper, and fewer control students sat the final test, so treat the size with some caution.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<ResearchCard n={2} />

The third trial is the warning. In a Turkish high school, nearly a thousand maths students were given either a standard ChatGPT-style assistant or a version with tutoring safeguards that gave hints rather than answers ([Bastani et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122)). With access, practice grades rose 48% with the standard assistant and 127% with the tutor version. But when access was taken away, students who'd had the standard assistant did 17% worse on exams than students who'd never had it. The safeguarded tutor largely avoided the harm.

The difference between the helpful tools and the harmful one isn't the AI. It's the design: tutors that make learners do the thinking, built on what's known about how people learn, help. Assistants that do the thinking for them can raise scores in practice and lower them when it counts.

**What it means for 2027.** For course designers: if you add an AI assistant to a course, make it a tutor, not an answer machine. Prompt it to ask questions and give hints, and keep practice without it. For leaders: ask vendors for evidence that their AI improves learning, not just engagement or speed. For builders: the Harvard and Turkish studies are practically a specification. ## 4. Workplace training is getting shorter

Corporate learning tells a different story. Spending has held up, but the time employees spend in training has fallen sharply. Training magazine's annual survey of US organisations with 100 or more employees put total training spend at US$102.8 billion in 2025, up 4.9% on 2024, while average hours of training per employee fell to 40 ([Training magazine, 2025](https://trainingmag.com/2025-training-industry-report/)).

<DataChart id="training-hours" />

ATD's separate survey found a similar squeeze on budgets: direct learning spend per employee fell from US$1,254 in 2024 to US$846 in 2025 ([ATD, 2026](https://www.td.org/content/atd-blog/2026-state-of-the-industry-report-indicates-latest-td-trends)). Both surveys sample different organisations each year, so read them as snapshots rather than a tracked panel.

Where the training happens is changing more slowly. Online and computer-based learning carried the largest share of training hours in both years, and AI-delivered training, though still small, more than doubled.

<DataChart id="training-delivery" />

And the career-development gap remains. In LinkedIn's 2025 Workplace Learning Report, only 36% of organisations were "career development champions" with robust programmes, and only 15% of learners said their manager had helped them build a career plan in the past six months ([LinkedIn Learning, 2025](https://business.linkedin.com/content/dam/lem/business/en/learn/workplace-learning-report-2025/LinkedIn-Workplace-Learning-Report-2025.pdf)).

**What it means for 2027.** Fewer hours means every hour has to count. For L&D teams, that argues for shorter, sharper programmes with practice and follow-up built in, rather than more content: the evidence on spacing and retrieval matters more when the time is short. For leaders, the gap between investment in content and support for careers is where the next budget conversation should start. For course designers working with corporate clients, expect briefs that ask for the same outcomes in less time.

## 5. Credentials have multiplied

The number of credentials on offer in the US has grown fast, and almost all the growth is in small ones. Credential Engine's 2025 count found 1,850,034 unique credentials, up from 1,076,358 in 2022 ([Credential Engine, 2025](https://credentialengine.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Counting-Credentials-2025-Report.pdf)).

<Scrolly chart="us-credentials">
  <Step view="badges">
    **Badges did most of it.** The count of digital badges rose from about 430,000 to more than a million. Credential Engine attributes much of that to better counting, as the major badge platforms reported more fully, as well as to genuine growth.
  </Step>
  <Step view="all">
    **Certificates grew by about half, degrees barely moved.** Certificates rose from about 331,000 to 486,000, many of them course-completion certificates. Degrees grew by about 10%, and the small "micro-credential" category more than doubled to 3,384.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

**What it means for 2027.** A million badges is a signalling problem as much as an opportunity. For course designers and creators, a credential is only worth something if the assessment behind it is: make the evidence of what learners can do visible, not just the completion. For leaders and associations, the question is which credentials employers will recognise, and the data on that is far thinner than the data on how many exist. We covered the university side in [microcredentials in higher education](/trends/microcredentials-higher-education-this-term/).

## 6. Course platforms keep growing; completion doesn't

The large open platforms are still adding learners at a remarkable rate. Coursera reported 197 million registered learners at the end of 2025, up 17% from 168 million a year earlier ([Coursera, 2026](https://s27.q4cdn.com/928340662/files/doc_financials/2025/q4/COUR_Earnings-Release_Q4-2025.pdf)).

<Scrolly chart="coursera-learners">
  <Step view="before">
    **Steady growth before 2020,** from 30.1 million registered learners at the end of 2017 to 46.4 million at the end of 2019.
  </Step>
  <Step view="pandemic">
    **A pandemic jump:** 76.6 million by the end of 2020, the biggest single-year rise in the series.
  </Step>
  <Step view="now">
    **And it hasn't slowed:** roughly 20 to 30 million new registered learners every year since, to 197 million.
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

<ResearchCard n={1} />

Registration isn't learning, and completion is where open online courses have always struggled. The best long-run evidence comes from Justin Reich and José Ruipérez-Valiente's analysis of 5.63 million learners in HarvardX and MITx courses ([Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aav7958)).

<DataChart id="mooc-completion" />

<ResearchCard n={0} />

The dataset ends in 2018, and completion isn't the only measure of value in an open course. But the pattern it shows, very low completion overall and much higher completion among learners who've committed, matches what most course creators see in their own data.

**What it means for 2027.** Scale and completion are different problems. For course creators, the paying-learner figures are the encouraging part: people who commit, finish. Design for them, with structure, deadlines and practice, rather than for the casual browser. For platform builders, the next differentiator isn't the catalogue; it's the support that gets people to the end. Our [online course design checklist](/guides/online-course-design-checklist/) covers the features that help.

## 7. The EdTech money has reset

Behind the products, the investment picture has changed completely. Global EdTech venture funding more than doubled in the pandemic, to US$20.8 billion in 2021, then fell for three years running.

<Scrolly chart="edtech-vc">
  <Step view="boom">
    **The boom:** from US$7 billion in 2019 to US$16.1 billion in 2020 and US$20.8 billion in 2021, through more than 1,500 funding rounds that year.
  </Step>
  <Step view="bust">
    **The bust:** US$10.6 billion in 2022, about US$3 billion in 2023, and US$2.4 billion in 2024, which HolonIQ called the lowest level of investment in a decade.
  </Step>
  <Step view="steady">
    **A floor:** US$2.6 billion in 2025, which HolonIQ described as a market settling into steadier ground, with bigger bets on AI and workforce training ([HolonIQ, 2026](https://www.holoniq.com/notes/edtech-hits-2-6b-in-investment-as-the-market-stabilizes-bigger-bets-in-ai-and-workforce-training)).
  </Step>
</Scrolly>

**What it means for 2027.** Less venture money means fewer new tools and more consolidation among the ones you already use. For anyone choosing a platform, a vendor's funding and business model are now part of due diligence: ask how they make money, and what happens to your courses if they're acquired. For builders, the money that is available is going to AI and workforce training, the two areas where the learning evidence above is also strongest. We looked at what the platforms are shipping in [AI course creation comes to course platforms](/trends/ai-course-creation-course-platforms/).

## What we'd do in 2027

Put the seven shifts together and the direction is clear. More learning is online, learners bring AI with them, and budgets and attention are tighter. The courses that do well will be the ones that make the most of limited time, and that are honest about whether learners can do something at the end.

- **If you design courses:** design every course for online and hybrid study, with practice and feedback in every module. Add AI only as a tutor that makes learners think, and assess what learners can do with AI available.
- **If you run a learning function:** plan for fewer hours, not less impact. Shorter programmes, spaced follow-up and real career support will do more than more content. Ask for learning evidence from every AI vendor.
- **If you build the tools:** the evidence now points to what works: guided practice, AI that teaches rather than answers, and support that gets committed learners to the finish.
