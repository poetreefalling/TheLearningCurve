    a: "Many 2.0 issuers 'host' their badges: the badge data sits on the issuer's server, and the 3.0 specification notes that hosted data is not tamper-evident.   - q: "What is a verifiable credential?"
    a: "A W3C standard for digital records that are tamper-evident and cryptographically signed by whoever issued them. The learner holds the credential, usually in a wallet app, and shares it with an employer or institution, who can check who issued it and that nobody has changed it. Open Badges 3.0 uses this model for learning achievements."

Version 2.0 is itself a W3C Verifiable Credential and shares its definition of an achievement with Open Badges 3.0, so one CLR can carry many badge-style achievements."

Open Badges 3.0 turns a course badge from a picture with a link into a signed record the learner
owns. This term, the US organisations that shape how learning is recorded started building around
that idea: a new national coalition for portable learning records, and fresh certifications for the
standards that carry them. Federal policy, though, still hasn't named the standard. For anyone who
issues certificates, the format question is getting easier to answer. The question of what a badge
proves is not.

## What happened

Three developments since the start of October show where things stand.

**Sixteen national organisations formed a coalition for portable records.** On 20 October 2026, the
Learning Mobility Coalition launched as "a cross-sector effort to make learning records more
portable, trusted, and useful" ([AACRAO, 2026](https://www.aacrao.org/news/national-organizations-align-to-advance-learning-mobility/)).
Its members include 1EdTech (the standards body behind Open Badges), the American Council on
Education, EDUCAUSE, UPCEA, NACE and the U.S. Chamber of Commerce Foundation. It succeeds the LER
Accelerator, which began in 2023 to promote interoperable learning and employment records (LERs:
records of what someone has learned and done that can travel between colleges, employers and job
systems). Its four priorities include helping institutions create and use interoperable records,
developing shared standards and tools so records can move between systems, and linking those records
to state talent marketplaces. The announcement doesn't name a technical standard.

**A platform used in 42 states certified for the new standards.** On 27 October 2026, SchooLinks, a
US K-12 college and career readiness platform, announced 1EdTech certification for Open Badges 3.0
and the Comprehensive Learner Record (CLR) 2.0, a standard for bundling many achievements into one
verifiable record ([1EdTech CLR 2.0](https://imsglobal.org/spec/clr/v2p0); [SchooLinks, 2026](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/10/27/3389254/0/en/schoolinks-achieves-certifications-for-1edtech-open-badges-3-0-and-clr-2-0-standards.html)).
Students keep their achievements in a digital credential wallet as "tamper-proof, machine-readable
credentials", with continued free access to their data after graduation. The company says millions
of students in 42 states use its platform. It joins credentialing providers that moved earlier:
Accredible, for example, added export and import of Open Badges 3.0 and W3C Verifiable Credentials
in January ([Accredible, 2026](https://www.accredible.com/newsroom/accredible-launches-support-for-open-badge-3-0-and-w3c-verifiable-credentials)).

**Federal policy describes the idea but not the standard.** A white paper published on 2 October 2026
by Greg DiDonato of EBSCOed mapped US federal policy on digital wallets, LERs and credential
registries through 2026 ([DiDonato, 2026](https://policy-in-action.com/federal-policy-foundation)).
It lists a run of Labor and Education guidance encouraging states to fund LERs and talent
marketplaces, including a July 2026 Labor letter it summarises as describing "verifiable,
learner-controlled records built on open standards such as W3C Verifiable Credentials". But its
conclusion on the badge standards is blunt: "No federal document reviewed cites Open Badges 3.0 or
the Comprehensive Learner Record 2.0." The one federal badging programme it lists, the Department of
the Air Force's, issues badges under Open Badges 2.0. This is one author's review, written by someone
whose company sells into the sector, so we'd read it as a useful map rather than the last word.

## What Open Badges 3.0 changes

The standard itself isn't new: 1EdTech's current version, document 1.4.5, was issued on 29 June 2026
([1EdTech, 2026](https://www.imsglobal.org/spec/ob/v3p0)). What it changes matters more as systems
like the ones above start to rely on it.

- **Every badge is a verifiable credential.** The specification puts it in one line: "an Assertion
  is also a Verifiable Credential (VC)". A verifiable credential is a W3C format for records that are
  signed by the issuer and tamper-evident.
- **The proof travels with the badge.** Hosting badge data on the issuer's server has been the
  preferred method of many Open Badges issuers, and the specification notes that "Hosted badge data is also not tamper-evident." A 3.0
  badge carries its own proof, so it can be checked after it moves platforms.
- **The learner holds it.** 3.0 badges are "designed to be issued and offered to learners who may
  accept them into their digital wallet", rather than living only on the issuer's site.
- **It isn't backwards compatible.** The specification says 3.0 badges "will not be conformant to all
  of the existing 2.x data model requirements", which is why platforms have to upgrade and get
  certified rather than flip a switch.

## Why it matters for people who build courses

If your course ends in a certificate, the record of that certificate is moving out of your platform
and into the learner's hands. A PDF or a badge image on a course page is easy to copy and hard to
check. A signed credential in a wallet can be checked by an employer, a college or a job system
without contacting you, and it keeps working if you change platforms.

That's good for learners, but it raises the stakes on what you put inside the badge. A verifiable
credential makes its contents easy to read and easy to trust as untampered. If the contents say
"completed 12 videos", that's what an employer will see, verified.

## What the evidence says

The research on credentials is thinner than the research on how people learn, and none of it tests
Open Badges 3.0 directly. The most useful review we've found is Soovendran Varadarajan and

By comparison, 22 per cent of the studies raised the lack of formal recognition, and 38 per cent
raised integrity and the risk of fraudulent credentials. That is the gap Open Badges 3.0 closes and
the gap it leaves open. A signature deals with fraud: it proves who issued a badge and that nobody
has changed it. It says nothing about whether two badges with the same name required the same work.
We'd read this as moderate evidence that employers care most about what a credential means, and that
verification helps only once that's clear. Our piece on

## What we'd do

1. **Write the criteria before choosing the format.** An Open Badge carries a description of the
   achievement and its criteria. Write them in the same observable terms as your
   [learning objectives](/guides/how-to-write-learning-objectives/): "can build a cash-flow forecast
   for a small business", not "completed Module 6".
2. **Award it for assessed work.** Tie the badge to a project, scenario or graded assignment rather
   than to watching every lesson. The assessment section of our
   [online course design checklist](/guides/online-course-design-checklist/) shows how to line it up
   with the objectives.
3. **Check certification, not marketing.** If you use a credentialing service, look for it in
   1EdTech's list of products certified for Open Badges 3.0. Ask whether learners can export their
   badges to a wallet of their choice and keep them if you stop paying for the service.
4. **Know what your course platform issues.** None of the four platforms we cover (Thinkific,
   LearnWorlds, Teachable and Kajabi) issues Open Badges natively. Several have built-in
   gamification badges or completion certificates, but those don't meet the 1EdTech Open Badges
   standard: they can't travel with the learner or be verified from their own metadata. All four
   can issue Open Badges through a third-party credentialing service such as Accredible, Credly or
   Canvas Badges (formerly Badgr). If you go that way, check the service's 1EdTech certification
   for version 3.0, not just 2.0.
5. **Don't rush a migration for its own sake.** If you already issue Open Badges 2.0 through a
   provider that is upgrading, the move to 3.0 should come through that provider. The bigger gain is
   usually in the criteria and the assessment, not the file format.

At [Avondale University](https://www.avondale.edu.au/), we issue Open Badges for the completion of our short courses and microcredentials. Apart from sharing those badges on LinkedIn and on resumes, the primary utility of the badges in our ecosystem is the stackability of our courses, particularly in the new MBA program, where professionals have a flexible pathway to build towards a full degree. Academic credit for successfully completed and assessed microcredentials is granted under Standard 1.2 of the [Higher Education Standards Framework](https://legislation.gov.au/F2021L00488/asmade/2021-04-27/text/original/pdf), which requires assessment of prior learning to be consistent with the credit policy in the Australian Qualifications Framework, and under the [AQF Qualifications Pathways Policy](https://www.aqf.edu.au/download/416/aqf-qualifications-pathways-policy/10/aqf-qualifications-pathways-policy/pdf) itself. Clause 2.1.10 of that policy uses 50% as the basis for negotiating credit from an Advanced Diploma or Associate Degree into a 3-year bachelor (37.5% into a 4-year one): a starting point for agreements between institutions, not a ceiling or an entitlement. The maximum credit limit is set by each institution's policy, and [TEQSA's guidance](https://teqsa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-07/guidance-note-credit-and-recognition-of-prior-learning-v2-0.pdf) asks that credit be consistent with the pathways policy, not disadvantage the student and maintain the integrity of the course.

## What to watch next

Watch whether the Learning Mobility Coalition settles on named standards for the records it wants to
move, and whether federal guidance starts to cite Open Badges 3.0 and CLR 2.0 rather than describing
verifiable records in general terms. Until then, design your badge as if an employer will open it and
read every line, because the technology is making that easier every term. For more on what
learners are doing alongside courses like these, see
[what changed in AI in eLearning this term](/trends/ai-in-elearning-this-term/).
